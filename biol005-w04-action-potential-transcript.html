The whole idea in a few sentences

Read this before you record. If these sentences make sense, the rest is detail.

  1. A neuron has two electrical signals. A graded potential is small, its size matches the stimulus, and it fades as it spreads because charge leaks out through the membrane and the cytoplasm resists its flow. An action potential is large, always the same size, and does not fade.
  2. Graded potentials add together at the trigger zone. If the total reaches threshold, the neuron fires. If it does not, nothing else happens.
  3. The voltage gated sodium channel has two gates. Depolarization opens the fast activation gate and, about half a millisecond later, closes the slow inactivation gate. So the channel opens briefly and then shuts itself, and it cannot reopen until the membrane has repolarized.
  4. Threshold is the potential where sodium entry through these channels becomes larger than potassium leaving through leak channels. Past it, depolarization opens more sodium channels, which causes more depolarization. That positive feedback is the rising phase.
  5. The peak stops near plus 30 because sodium channels inactivate and slow voltage gated potassium channels open. Potassium leaving is the falling phase. The potassium channels are slow to close, which gives the after-hyperpolarization. Leak channels, not the pump, return the membrane to rest.
  6. The action potential is all or none because the stimulus only has to reach threshold. After that, its size is set by the ion gradients and the channel properties. So few ions cross that the concentrations do not change.

03

A neuron uses two kinds of electrical signal

65 sec

Here is the map for today. Electrical signals in a neuron come in two kinds. On the left, the graded potential. It is a short distance signal, its size depends on the stimulus, and it fades as it spreads. On the right, the action potential. It is a long distance signal, it is always the same size, and it does not fade.

Now look at the neuron underneath. You saw these four zones in the neurons and glia lecture. Graded potentials happen in the input zone, the dendrites and the cell body. Action potentials start at the trigger zone, which is the first part of the axon, and then travel down the conducting zone to the terminals.

So the two signals work together. Graded potentials bring information in to the trigger zone. The trigger zone decides whether to send an action potential out.

DrawIn navy, circle the input zone bracket and write GRADED above the dendrites. In rust, circle the trigger zone and draw an arrow along the axon to the terminals, and write AP along it.
04

A graded potential is as large as the stimulus that caused it

90 sec

Let us start with the graded potential. It begins when a stimulus opens some gated channels. At a synapse, the stimulus is a neurotransmitter, and it opens ligand gated channels. In a touch receptor in your skin, the stimulus is stretch, and it opens mechanically gated channels. These are the channel types from the end of last lecture.

What happens next is last lecture's rule. If the channels let sodium in, the membrane moves toward the sodium equilibrium potential, so it depolarizes. If the channels let potassium out, or chloride in, the membrane hyperpolarizes. That is the fourth trace in the figure, the one that dips down.

Now, why is it called graded? Look at the first three traces. A weak stimulus opens a few channels, a little sodium comes in, and you get a 3 millivolt bump. A stronger stimulus opens more channels, or holds them open longer, more sodium comes in, and the bump is bigger. The size of the graded potential reports the size of the stimulus. There is no threshold for a graded potential, and there is no standard size. Any stimulus that opens channels makes one.

DrawIn rust, trace each of the three depolarizing bumps and then tap the stimulus bar under it, saying: bigger stimulus, bigger response. In navy, trace the fourth one and write: hyperpolarizing.
05

A graded potential gets smaller as it spreads

90 sec

Graded potentials have one big weakness. They fade. Here is a dendrite drawn as a long tube. The channels opened at the left end, and sodium came in there. That sodium is positive charge, and it spreads along the inside of the tube. We call that local current flow.

Two things make it shrink as it goes. The first is current leak. The membrane has leak channels open all along its length, the same leak channels that set the resting potential. As the positive charge moves along, some of it leaves through those channels. Picture a garden hose with small holes all along it. The farther you go from the tap, the less water is left.

The second is cytoplasmic resistance. The cytoplasm is not a perfect conductor. It resists the flow of charge, the way a narrow pipe resists flow more than a wide one.

So look at the four recordings across the top. Twenty millivolts right beside the channels, then 12, then 7, then 4. Same graded potential, measured farther and farther away. Which of those two losses could you reduce by making the tube wider? Decide, and then open the box.

DrawIn rust, draw down each of the leak arrows along the bottom and write: current leak. In navy, trace the three arrows inside the tube from thick to thin and write: cytoplasmic resistance.
DrawIn navy, write 20, 12, 7, 4 above the four boxes as you say them.

06

What reaches the trigger zone decides whether the neuron fires

95 sec

If graded potentials fade, then what matters is how big the graded potential still is when it arrives at the trigger zone. If it is still big enough to reach threshold, we call it suprathreshold, and the neuron fires. If it has faded too much, it is subthreshold, and nothing else happens.

Graded potentials have one more property, and it is a useful one. They add. If two arrive at about the same time, the trigger zone sees the sum. That is called summation. Look at the right hand panel. Each dashed line alone stays under threshold. Together they cross it, and you get an action potential.

Let us put numbers on it. This is worked problem 1. The neuron rests at minus 70, and threshold is minus 55, so it needs 15 millivolts. One synapse makes 20 millivolts out at the dendrite, but only 8 of it survives to the trigger zone. Minus 70 plus 8 is minus 62. Not enough. Two of those synapses together: 8 plus 8 is 16. Minus 70 plus 16 is minus 54. That crosses threshold, so the neuron fires. Pause here and do the third case yourself, with the inhibitory synapse, before you open the work.

DrawOn the left panel write in navy: minus 70 plus 8 equals minus 62, NO. On the right panel write in rust: minus 70 plus 16 equals minus 54, FIRES.

07

A voltage gated sodium channel has two gates

95 sec

Now the most important protein of the day, the voltage gated sodium channel. I am going to draw it four times, and each drawing adds one step. If you understand this one channel, the action potential is easy.

It is a pore that only sodium fits through, and part of the protein can sense the voltage across the membrane. The new idea is that it has two gates, one after the other. Picture a hallway with a door at each end. You can only walk through if both doors are open at the same time. If either door is shut, nobody gets through.

The first door is called the activation gate. In my drawing it is the bar across the pore. At rest, it is closed. The second door is called the inactivation gate. In my drawing it is the ball hanging on a chain on the inside of the cell. At rest, it is open, hanging out of the way.

So at rest: activation gate closed, inactivation gate open. One closed door is enough, so no sodium gets in. But notice that this channel is ready. It is closed, and it is able to open. In two slides you will see a different kind of closed.

DrawIn navy, trace the bar and write ACTIVATION, closed. In rust, circle the ball and write INACTIVATION, open.
DrawUnder the panel write: closed, but ABLE to open.
08

Depolarization opens the activation gate within a fraction of a millisecond

55 sec

Step 2. Something depolarizes the membrane. The voltage sensor feels that change and the activation gate opens. It is very fast, a small fraction of a millisecond.

Now look at both doors. The activation gate has just opened. The inactivation gate was already open. Both doors are open, so sodium comes in. And it comes in with a lot of force behind it. Remember from last lecture that sodium has both its concentration gradient and the negative inside pulling it inward, and it is more than 100 millivolts away from its equilibrium potential.

One more thing to notice, because we need it in a few slides. The more the membrane depolarizes, the more of these channels open.

DrawIn navy, trace the opened bar in panel 2 and write: opens FAST. Trace the sodium arrow down through the pore.
DrawBetween panel 1 and panel 2, circle the words then: depolarization.
09

The same depolarization closes the inactivation gate about half a millisecond later

2 min

Step 3 is the one students find hardest, so let us take it carefully. The depolarization that opened the activation gate was also felt by the inactivation gate. Both gates got the same message at the same moment. The difference is speed, and direction. The activation gate is fast, and its response is to open. The inactivation gate is slow, and its response is to close.

So what happens? The fast door opens first. For about half a millisecond both doors are open, and sodium comes in. Then the slow door finishes closing, the ball moves into the pore, and sodium stops. The channel opened itself and then shut itself, and the membrane is still depolarized. Nothing had to tell it to stop.

Here is an everyday version. Picture a door with an automatic closer at a store entrance. You push it open, and the closer starts working the moment you push. It is just slower than you are. There is a short time when the door is open, and then it shuts again by itself.

Now look carefully at panel 3 and compare it with panel 1. Both channels are closed. No sodium passes in either one. But they are not the same. In panel 1 the bar is closed and the ball is open. In panel 3 the bar is open and the ball is plugging the pore. We call panel 3 inactivated. And an inactivated channel cannot be opened by depolarizing it again, because depolarizing is what closed that gate in the first place.

DrawIn rust, circle the ball sitting in the pore in panel 3 and write: closes SLOW, about half a millisecond.
DrawIn navy, draw a double headed arrow between panel 1 and panel 3 and write: both closed, NOT the same.

10

Only repolarization returns the channel to its resting state

70 sec

Step 4. How does the channel get back to the start? Only one way. The membrane has to repolarize, back down near minus 70. When it does, both gates go back to their resting positions. The bar closes, and the ball swings back out of the pore.

So panel 4 looks exactly like panel 1, and that is the point. The channel has three states, and it goes through them in order: closed and able to open, then open, then inactivated, and then back to closed and able to open. It cannot skip from inactivated straight back to open.

Open the box and read the question with me. Both closed states stop sodium, so why do we care which gate is closed? Because one of them can be opened by a stimulus and the other cannot. Keep this distinction for next lecture, because it is the whole explanation for the refractory period.

11

The voltage gated potassium channel has one gate, and it is slow

85 sec

The second channel is simpler. The voltage gated potassium channel has just one gate. It is closed at rest, and it opens when the membrane depolarizes. It is not the potassium leak channel from last lecture, which is always open.

The important thing about it is that it is slow. Look at the timing bars on the right. At time zero the membrane reaches threshold. The sodium activation gate opens right away. About half a millisecond later two things happen at nearly the same time: the sodium inactivation gate closes, and the potassium gate finally opens. And then the potassium gate is slow to close as well. It stays open even after the membrane is back near rest.

When it opens, potassium leaves the cell, because the membrane is far above minus 95, the potassium equilibrium potential.

Ask yourself what would happen if potassium channels were as fast as sodium channels. Sodium coming in and potassium going out at the same time would cancel, and the membrane would barely move. The delay is what makes an action potential possible.

DrawIn navy, trace the top bar and say: fast. In rust, trace the bottom bar and say: slow to open, slow to close. Draw a vertical line at 0.5 ms through the start of the lower two bars.
DoAsk the reveal question aloud before opening it.
12

Threshold is the potential where sodium entry becomes larger than potassium leak

90 sec

Now we can say what threshold really is. A lot of students learn threshold as a number, minus 55. It is better to understand it as a contest between two currents.

Look at the left panel. A small graded potential has depolarized the trigger zone to minus 62. A few sodium channels have opened, so a little sodium is coming in. But potassium is still leaving through the leak channels, and because we moved away from the potassium equilibrium potential, that outward current is a bit bigger than it was at rest. Potassium out is larger than sodium in. So the membrane drifts back down to rest. That is all a subthreshold graded potential does.

Middle panel. A bigger depolarization, more sodium channels open, and now sodium in exactly equals potassium out. That balance point is threshold.

Right panel. Just past that point, sodium in is larger than potassium out. The membrane depolarizes a little more by itself, and that opens more sodium channels. So threshold is the membrane potential where sodium entry becomes larger than potassium exit. It happens to be near minus 55 in many neurons. Books give anything from minus 50 to minus 55.

DrawIn each panel, trace the sodium arrow in navy and the potassium arrow in rust, and write under them: K bigger, equal, Na bigger.
DrawUnder the middle panel write: THRESHOLD = Na in just beats K out.
DoOpen the reveal and read the definition aloud.
13

Past threshold, depolarization sustains itself until the channels inactivate

70 sec

Here is what happens past threshold. Follow the loop with me. Activation gates open. Sodium enters. The membrane depolarizes further. And further depolarization opens more activation gates. The loop keeps going around, and each turn makes the next turn bigger. That is positive feedback, and you saw it in Week 1 with homeostasis. It is rare in the body because it cannot stop itself.

This loop is the rising phase of the action potential. It is why the rise is so steep, and why an action potential cannot stop halfway up.

So what stops it? Look at the bottom of the figure. Two slower events were started by that same depolarization. The inactivation gates close, so sodium entry stops and the loop is broken. And the potassium channels open, so potassium leaves and the membrane comes back down. Both of those are slow, which is why the loop gets about half a millisecond to run.

DrawIn rust, trace around the loop twice with the pen, faster the second time. Write POSITIVE FEEDBACK in the middle.
DrawIn navy, circle the two boxes at the bottom and write: these end it.

15

Falling phase, after-hyperpolarization and the return to rest

95 sec

Here are the two reasons. First, the sodium channels inactivate about half a millisecond after they open, so sodium entry is ending. Second, the potassium channels are now opening. Look at the bottom graph at the time of the peak. Sodium permeability is already falling and potassium permeability is rising.

Point 5, the falling phase. Sodium channels are inactivated. Potassium channels are open. Potassium leaves the cell, positive charge goes out, and the membrane repolarizes.

Point 6, the after-hyperpolarization. The membrane does not stop at minus 70. It goes below it. Why? Because the potassium channels are slow to close. For a few milliseconds the membrane has more potassium permeability than it has at rest, so it moves even closer to the potassium equilibrium potential, minus 95. On the bottom graph you can see potassium permeability is still raised.

Point 7, back to rest. The potassium channels finish closing, only the leak channels are left, and they bring the membrane back to minus 70. Notice what I did not say. I did not say the pump. A very common wrong answer on exams is that the sodium potassium pump repolarizes the membrane. It does not. Open the box and we will read why.

16

The Goldman equation gives the potential at each phase

90 sec

Worked problem 2 uses the equation you already know. The Goldman equation says the membrane potential depends on the concentrations and on the permeabilities. During an action potential the concentrations do not change. Only the permeabilities do. So we can predict which way the membrane moves at each phase.

At rest, the ratio of sodium permeability to potassium permeability is 0.04 to 1, and you got minus 71 last time. Now suppose every voltage gated sodium channel is open, and the ratio is 20 to 1. Put 20 and 1 into the equation and you get plus 50. The arithmetic is in the box. That is where the membrane would go if the sodium channels stayed open.

They do not stay open. At the real peak the ratio is only about 4.3 to 1, and that gives plus 30. And in the after-hyperpolarization, sodium permeability is back to 0.04 and potassium permeability is about three times normal. That gives minus 84.

So read those four answers in a row: minus 71, toward plus 50, plus 30, minus 84. That is the shape of the action potential, and we got it without changing a single concentration.

17

Once threshold is crossed, every action potential is the same size

95 sec

Here are four stimuli, each one stronger than the last. Look at the responses. Stimulus 1 gives a small graded potential. Stimulus 2 gives a bigger one, but it still does not reach threshold, so it fades. Stimulus 3 reaches threshold, and you get a full action potential with a peak of plus 30. Stimulus 4 is a lot stronger than stimulus 3. And the action potential is exactly the same.

That is what all or none means.

Why does the size not depend on the stimulus? Because the stimulus only has one job, which is to get the membrane to threshold. After that, the positive feedback loop runs by itself, using the energy stored in the sodium gradient. The height of the peak is set by the sodium equilibrium potential and by how fast the channels inactivate, and none of that knows anything about the stimulus. It is like striking a match. Once it lights, it burns with its own fuel, and striking harder does not give a bigger flame.

That leaves a puzzle. If every action potential is identical, how does your brain tell a light touch from a hard one? That is the first thing we do next lecture.

DrawIn rust, draw a horizontal line across the tops of the two action potentials and write: same peak. In navy, circle stimulus bars 3 and 4 and write: very different.

18

How many ions cross during one action potential

80 sec

Last lecture I told you that almost no ions have to move to make the resting potential. The same is true here, and this time we will count them.

Here is what you are given. It takes about 6,000 charges per square micrometer of membrane to change the potential by 100 millivolts. That is a measured property of cell membranes. Our cell body is a sphere 20 micrometers across. The surface area comes to about 1,257 square micrometers. Multiply by 6,000 and you get about seven and a half million sodium ions.

That sounds like a lot. Now count the sodium ions already inside the cell at 15 millimolar. It is about 38 billion. So the sodium that came in is about 1 part in 5,000. Sodium inside went from 15 millimolar to 15.003. You could not measure it.

This is why the pump is not part of the action potential. After one action potential there is essentially nothing to fix. The pump matters in the long run, over thousands of action potentials, and that is all.

19

Graded potential and action potential compared

60 sec

Here are the two signals side by side. You do not need to memorize this table as a list. Try to explain each row from what you now know about the channels.

Graded potentials come from ligand gated or mechanically gated channels, so their size depends on how many of those channels the stimulus opened. They fade because of current leak and cytoplasmic resistance. They have no threshold, and they add. Action potentials come from voltage gated sodium and potassium channels. They need a threshold because of the contest between sodium and potassium currents, and they are all or none because of positive feedback.

The last row is the one to remember. The graded potential measures the input. The action potential carries the result over a distance.

22

Why the diner could not breathe

90 sec

Back to the man who ate the pufferfish. You can explain him now. Tetrodotoxin plugs the outside of the voltage gated sodium channel. It does not affect leak channels or the pump, so his resting potentials are normal. It does not affect ligand gated channels, so his graded potentials are normal. What he has lost is the rising phase. His neurons reach threshold, and nothing opens.

Without action potentials, sensory axons cannot report to the brain, so his lips and tongue go numb. Motor axons cannot carry commands, and skeletal muscle cannot fire either, so he becomes weak. When no action potentials reach the diaphragm, he stops breathing. His heart keeps beating because heart muscle has a different version of the sodium channel that the toxin barely affects, and he is awake because very little of the toxin gets into the brain.

Look at it as an experiment. What failed is the delivery of action potentials to the breathing muscles. A ventilator replaces that one missing output. It moves the air. It does nothing to the toxin. The toxin comes off the channels as the body clears it, and the channels were never damaged, so function returns.
