The baseline story

Graded potentials and the action potential, told as a story

Now let's turn this ordinary cell into a cell that can send an electrical message. We start with the resting cell you already built, and we add one small piece at a time.

  • Each part adds one new piece of information and then asks you a question you can already answer.
  • Two everyday things will help: a pebble dropped in a pond, and the handle of a toilet.
  • You draw along on your own sheet. A scientific name arrives only after you understand the idea. There are no numbers today.

What we are building

The ligand gated and voltage gated doors from the end of the first story suddenly become important. By the end, the cell can turn a small chemical signal into one large electrical event that is the same size every time, and you can explain every part of it.

02

Part 1

Sodium is waiting outside

This is where the first story ended. The inside is negative. Na+ has two reasons to come in: there is lots of it outside, and the negative inside pulls on it. But it has almost no doors.

Ask

What would happen to the inside if we suddenly gave sodium a lot of doors?

Answer

Na+ would come in, and every Na+ brings a positive charge in with it. The inside would become less negative. With enough doors it would even become positive for a moment.

That one answer is the whole idea of today. Everything else is how the cell opens those doors, and how it shuts them again.

Lots of Na+ doors → Na+ comes in → inside less negative, then positive.

03

Part 2

Back to the nucleus: a door with a key

Back to the nucleus: gene, protein, membrane. The cell builds a door with a key and puts copies at its input end. This door came up at the end of the first story. It is shut until a chemical key binds to it, and this one lets Na+ through.

Ask

A key binds and the door opens. Which way does Na+ go, and what does that do to the inside right there?

Answer

Na+ comes in, for both of its reasons. A little positive charge comes in with it, so the inside becomes a little less negative, right there under the door.

You already know this door's name: a ligand gated channel. Ligand is the scientific word for the key. Now the change itself gets a name. Any time the inside becomes less negative, it is called a depolarization.

At many real synapses this door lets both Na+ and K+ through, and the Na+ coming in has the larger effect. The synapse story returns to that.

Key binds → door opens → a little Na+ comes in → inside a little less negative.

Now name it: depolarization

04

Part 3

More keys arrive

One key opened one door and made a small change. A stronger signal means more keys.

Ask

If more keys arrive, is the change the same size, smaller or bigger? Why?

Answer

Bigger. More keys open more doors, more Na+ comes in, and the inside becomes less negative by a larger amount.

So this kind of change does not have one fixed size. A small signal makes a small change and a large signal makes a large change.

More keys → more doors open → more Na+ in → a bigger change.

05

Part 4

The change spreads, and it fades

Drop a pebble in a pond. The ripple is tall where the pebble landed and gets smaller as it spreads, and a bigger pebble makes a bigger ripple. The positive charge that came in also spreads along the inside of the cell, away from the doors.

Ask

As the change spreads along the inside toward the trigger zone, does it stay the same size?

Answer

No. It gets smaller with distance, and far enough away it is gone.

Here is where the pond stops being a fair comparison. Nothing travels through the cell as a wave. The positive charge spreads along the inside, and on the way some of it leaks back out through the leak doors, so less and less of it is left.

A change whose size depends on the size of the signal, and that fades as it spreads, now gets its name.

Bigger signal → bigger change. Farther from the doors → smaller change.

Now name it: graded potential

06

Part 5

Back to the nucleus: a door that senses voltage

Back to the nucleus: gene, protein, membrane. This is the other door from the end of the first story. It has a sensor, and it opens when the inside becomes less negative. It lets only Na+ through, and the cell packs a lot of these doors into the trigger zone.

Ask

Na+ comes in through the first door. What does that do to the door next to it?

Answer

The Na+ that came in makes the inside less negative there too. That is exactly what the neighbouring door senses, so it opens too. More Na+ comes in, the inside becomes even less negative, and more doors open.

The result feeds its own cause, so once it starts it runs faster and faster. You found the loop yourself. A loop like this is called positive feedback, and the door is a voltage gated sodium channel.

Less negative → Na+ doors open → Na+ comes in → even less negative → more doors open.

Now name it: positive feedback, voltage gated Na+ channel

07

Part 6

What decides whether the loop starts?

The loop does not start by itself. The graded potential from the input end has to open the first few voltage sensing doors. Meanwhile the K+ leak doors are open as always, and K+ leaving pulls the inside back toward rest.

Ask

The change has faded by the time it reaches the trigger zone. What decides whether the loop starts?

Answer

How big the change still is when it arrives.

Too small: only a few sodium doors open, more K+ leaves than Na+ enters, and the inside goes back to rest. It was only a graded potential.

Big enough: so many sodium doors open that more Na+ enters than K+ leaves. Now the loop feeds itself and cannot be stopped partway.

The level the inside has to reach for the loop to start now gets its name. No value yet.

Big enough on arrival → the loop starts. Too small → it fades away.

Now name it: threshold

08

Part 7

What stops a runaway loop?

A loop that feeds itself needs something outside the loop to stop it. This door has a second gate. The same change that opens the fast gate, the bar, also starts a slow gate, the ball, which closes a moment later.

Ask

The door is closed in drawing 1 and closed in drawing 3. Which of the two can be opened by a new signal?

Answer

Only drawing 1. There the fast gate is shut and the slow gate is open, so the door is closed and ready. A change in voltage will open it.

In drawing 3 the fast gate is already open and the slow gate is plugging the door. It is shut and not ready. Nothing opens it until the inside is negative again, and then both gates go back to where they started. So each sodium door opens only for a moment, and that is what ends the entry of Na+.

The grown-up names: the fast gate is the activation gate, the slow gate is the inactivation gate, and a door in state 3 is inactivated.

Fast gate opens → a moment later the slow gate closes → sodium entry stops.

Now name it: activation gate, inactivation gate, inactivated

09

Part 8

Back to the nucleus: a slow potassium door

The sodium doors are shut, but the inside is still positive. Back to the nucleus once more: gene, protein, membrane. This door also senses voltage and also starts to open when the inside becomes less negative. It lets only K+ through, and it is SLOW.

Ask

When this door opens, which way does K+ go, and what does that do to the inside?

Why does it matter that this door is slow?

Answer

Out. There is lots of K+ inside, and now the inside is positive, so both forces push K+ out. Positive charge leaves and the inside becomes negative again. Going back toward negative is called repolarization.

If this door were as fast as the sodium door, K+ would leave at the same moment Na+ came in, the two would cancel, and the inside would hardly change. Because it is slow, sodium goes first and potassium goes second.

K+ leaves → positive leaves → inside becomes negative again.

Now name it: voltage gated K+ channel, repolarization

10

Part 9

Put it in order: one trace

Now we draw the whole event as one line over time. There are no numbers on these axes. You already have every piece.

Ask

A few keys arrive, and later many keys arrive. Draw what the inside does each time, and say which door is responsible for each part of the line.

Answer

A few keys: a small bump that does not reach the dashed line and fades. A graded potential.

Many keys: the bump reaches threshold. Up: Na+ in through the voltage gated sodium doors. Top: the slow gates shut and the K+ doors are opening. Down: K+ out. A small dip below rest: the K+ doors are slow to close. Back to rest: the extra K+ doors close, and the leak doors, which were open all along, hold the inside at rest again.

Now the names, in order: rising phase, peak, falling phase, after-hyperpolarization. The whole event is an action potential.

Na+ in: up. Slow gates shut: top. K+ out: down. K+ doors slow to close: dip.

Now name it: rising phase, peak, falling phase, after-hyperpolarization, action potential

11

Part 10

All or none

Think about flushing a toilet. Push the handle a little and nothing happens. Push it past a certain point and you get a full flush. Push it much harder and the flush is no bigger.

Ask

Draw the response to each of the three signals. Then say why, in cell terms, a stronger signal does not make a bigger action potential.

Answer

Weak: a graded potential and nothing more. Just enough: a full action potential. Much stronger: the same action potential, the same height and the same shape.

The size of a flush comes from the water stored in the tank, and the handle only starts it. The size of an action potential comes from the Na+ difference that the pump built, and from how the doors open and shut. The signal only has to get the trigger zone to threshold.

The full lecture uses a match for the same idea: once it lights, it burns on the energy stored in the match.

Below threshold → none. At or above threshold → all of it, the same every time.

Now name it: all or none

12

Part 11

Did the cell just lose its differences?

Here the toilet stops being a fair comparison. A flush empties the tank. Na+ came into our cell and K+ went out, so it is fair to ask whether the cell has emptied its tank too.

Ask

After one action potential, does the cell still have lots of Na+ outside and lots of K+ inside?

Answer

Yes. Just as in the first story, only a very small number of ions has to cross to change the charge at the membrane. The differences are almost exactly what they were.

The pump keeps working in the background and, over time, moves the few Na+ back out and the few K+ back in. The pump is not what ended the action potential. The slow gates and the K+ doors did that.

Very few ions crossed → the differences are still there → the pump tidies up slowly.
