The whole idea in a few sentences

Read this before you record. If these sentences make sense, the rest is detail.

  1. When one patch of axon fires, Na+ comes in and the inside becomes positive there, and that positive charge spreads along the inside toward the negative patch next door. That spread is local current.
  2. Local current brings the next patch to threshold, its own sodium doors open, and it makes a full new action potential. So the action potential is made again at every patch and arrives full size, unlike the little ripple, which nothing renews.
  3. The patch that has just fired has sodium doors that are shut and not ready, so the current flowing backward cannot fire it, and the message goes one way. First no push works (absolute refractory period), then only a bigger push works while the slow potassium doors are still open (relative refractory period).
  4. Because every action potential is the same size, strength is carried by how often an axon fires (frequency coding) and by how many axons fire (recruitment), and the refractory period sets the fastest possible firing.
  5. A wider axon has less resistance inside, and myelin stops the current leaking out through the membrane, so the current reaches farther. With myelin the action potential is made again only at the nodes of Ranvier. Nothing jumps. Where myelin is lost, the next node fires late or not at all.
  6. More K+ outside makes the resting cell less negative and closer to threshold, and if it is held there the sodium doors end up shut and not ready and the cell cannot fire. Less K+ outside does the reverse. A local anesthetic blocks the sodium doors in one stretch, and the message stops there.

How action potentials carry information, told as a story

In Story 3 our cell fired an action potential at one spot on its membrane. Today we send that message all the way down the axon. We start with a row of dominoes. I add one small piece at a time, and each time I ask you a question you can already answer.

02

Part 1. A row of dominoes

50 sec

Here is a row of dominoes. If I push the first one hard enough, the whole row falls. If I push too gently, nothing falls. It is the whole row or nothing.

Two questions. Does the last domino fall more gently than the first? And can a domino that has fallen fall again right away?

No, and no. My push only tipped the first domino. After that, each domino falls with its own stored energy, the energy somebody put in when they stood it up. So the last one falls just as hard as the first. And a fallen domino cannot fall again until it has been stood back up.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw a short arrow over the first domino and an arrow of exactly the same size over the last one, and write: same fall. Under the row write: each one falls with its OWN energy. In rust, write: fallen = cannot fall again until stood back up.
03

Part 2. Turn the dominoes into an axon

65 sec

Now let's turn the dominoes into our cell. The little map in the corner shows our cell with its long axon, and the big drawing is one stretch of that axon. All along the membrane are the doors you built in Story 3: the voltage sensing sodium door, in navy, and the slow potassium door, in rust. I have divided the stretch into three patches.

Patch 1 has just fired, exactly as in Story 3. Na+ came in, so the inside of patch 1 is positive. Next door, in patch 2, the inside is still negative. Which way does that positive charge move?

Toward the negative. Opposite charges attract, and nothing is in the way along the inside of the axon. So positive charge spreads into patch 2, and the inside of patch 2 becomes less negative. Now the name: local current.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, one curved arrow along the INSIDE of the axon, from the plus signs under patch 1 to the minus signs under patch 2. Over patch 2 write: less negative. Then write the name under the axon: local current.
04

Part 3. The next patch fires

45 sec

The inside of patch 2 is becoming less negative. So what do its sodium doors do?

When patch 2 reaches threshold, they open. Na+ comes in through patch 2's own doors, and patch 2 fires a full action potential of its own. Now its local current spreads on to patch 3, and patch 3 does the same.

So the action potential does not slide along the axon like a bead on a string. It is made again, patch after patch. The name for that is regenerated, and the traveling is called conduction.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, redraw the gate of the sodium door in patch 2 swung open, draw an arrow IN through that door, and change the signs of patch 2: plus inside, minus outside. Then a second curved arrow along the inside from patch 2 to patch 3. Write: made again.
05

Part 4. Why it arrives full size

55 sec

Here is the whole axon. In Story 3 a little ripple spread along the membrane and got smaller with distance. I have drawn the ripple and the action potential at the start of the axon. You draw them in the middle and at the far end. Why are they different?

The ripple fades because nothing makes it again. The charge spreads out and leaks away. So it is smaller in the middle and gone at the far end. The action potential is the same size everywhere, because every patch makes it again with its own doors and its own Na+ difference. That is the domino falling with its own stored energy. The one that reaches the far end is a fresh copy.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, on the lower screens draw a smaller bump in the middle and a flat line at the far end, and write: nothing makes it again. On the upper screens draw the same tall spike twice more, the same height as the first, and write: made again at every patch.
06

Part 5. Why it only goes forward

65 sec

Patch 2 is firing. Its local current spreads both ways, toward patch 3 and back toward patch 1. Look closely at the sodium door in patch 1. The ball is in the opening. That is the state we called shut and not ready. Can the current flowing backward open it again?

No. A door that is shut and not ready cannot open, however hard it is pushed. It has to reset first. Patch 3 is closed and ready, so patch 3 opens next. The action potential can only go forward. The time when a patch cannot fire again is the refractory period.

This is the fallen domino. And here the dominoes stop being a good comparison. Nobody has to reset an axon. Each patch stands its own dominoes back up within a moment, again and again.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, a curved arrow along the inside from patch 2 BACK to patch 1. In rust, a big X where it arrives under the sodium door of patch 1, and write: shut and not ready. Over patch 3 write in navy: ready, opens next. Under the axon draw one long arrow pointing forward and write: one way. Then the name: refractory period.
07

Part 6. Two kinds of not ready

70 sec

A moment later patch 3 is firing. Compare the two patches behind it. In patch 2 the sodium door is still shut and not ready. In patch 1 it is closed and ready again, but the slow potassium door is still open, and K+ is still leaving.

So, patch 2: would any push fire it now? And patch 1: would it take the usual push, a smaller one or a bigger one?

Patch 2: nothing works. There are no ready doors. Patch 1: it takes a bigger push. K+ leaving carries positive charge out as fast as the push brings it in, and the inside is a little more negative than at rest. Now the names: first the absolute refractory period, then the relative refractory period. A toilet that has just been flushed is a fair comparison. While the tank is empty, nothing happens. While it refills, you get a weaker flush.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, an arrow OUT through the open potassium door in patch 1 and another in patch 2, and write: K+ still leaving. In rust over patch 2 write: NO push works, then absolute. In navy over patch 1 write: needs a BIGGER push, then relative.
08

Part 7. Hard press or light touch?

50 sec

Every action potential is the same size. So how does your brain know a hard press from a light touch? A doorbell has the same problem. It makes the same sound however hard you press the button. If it were urgent, how could you tell the people inside? Find two ways.

Ring it again and again, quickly. And more people can each ring their own bell. An axon does the first. A stronger stimulus makes it fire more action potentials each second, all the same size. That is frequency coding. The nervous system does the second. A harder press reaches more endings, so more axons fire. That is recruitment.

DoAsk for the two ways before anyone draws. Then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, short upright lines, all the same height. Light touch: three lines, far apart, on axon 2 only, and write silent on axons 1 and 3. Hard press: many lines close together on axon 2, and some lines on axons 1 and 3. Write: same size, more per second = frequency coding. More axons = recruitment.
09

Part 8. What sets the fastest firing?

50 sec

The two kinds of not ready follow every action potential. I have drawn them as blocks under the strip. With an ordinary push, where is the earliest the next action potential can start? And with the strongest possible push?

With an ordinary push, not until both blocks are over. With a bigger push it can start inside the dashed block, and it may come out a little smaller, because only some of the sodium doors are ready. But even the strongest push cannot start one inside the shut and not ready block. So that block sets how close together two action potentials can ever be. That is the fastest an axon can fire.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy on the upper strip, a tall line at the end of the dashed block: write ordinary push. In rust, a slightly shorter dashed line inside the dashed block: write bigger push only. On the lower strip, tall navy lines one after another, each starting exactly where the block before it ends, and redraw the block under each. Write: fastest possible.
10

Part 9. Make it faster: a wider axon

45 sec

What would make the message travel faster? Here is the first idea. Local current flows through the fluid inside the axon. A wide pipe carries water more easily than a narrow pipe, because there is more room for the flow. In which axon does the local current reach farther?

The wide one. More room inside means less resistance to the flow of charge, so the current reaches farther along before it fades. Doors farther along reach threshold sooner, and the action potential travels faster. The name for this is simply axon diameter. A larger diameter is faster.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, a thin short arrow along the inside of the narrow axon that stops before the second door. A thick long arrow along the inside of the wide axon that reaches past the second door. Write: wide = less resistance = reaches farther = faster.
11

Part 10. The leaky hose

55 sec

Here is the second idea. A hose with holes along it loses water before the far end. Our membrane has holes too: the leak doors from Story 1. Positive charge flowing along the inside can go out through them. So what happens to the local current on its way to the next sodium doors? And what would you do to a leaky hose?

Some current leaks out at every leak door, so less arrives, and the next sodium doors take longer to reach threshold. You would wrap the hose. The axon is wrapped too. Story 2 covered the glial cells that wrap their own membrane around the axon, layer on layer. Its name is myelin.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, an arrow along the inside that gets thinner after each leak door, and a small arrow going OUT through each leak door. Write: leaks out, little arrives. Then sketch a sleeve over the leak doors and write: wrap it = myelin.
12

Part 11. Wrapped, with gaps

60 sec

Now the axon is wrapped. The myelin covers the leak doors, so very little current leaks out. And the sodium doors are gathered in the short bare gaps. The door at gap 1 has just opened. Where is the next place an action potential can be made?

The current flows along the inside, under the myelin, and reaches gap 2 still strong. Gap 2 is the next place with sodium doors, so the action potential is made again there, and then at gap 3. The gaps are the nodes of Ranvier, and this is saltatory conduction, from the Latin word for jumping. But nothing jumps. Current flows quickly under the wrapping, and the slow step, opening doors, happens only at the gaps. Far fewer stops, so the message arrives much sooner.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, one thick arrow along the inside, under the myelin, from gap 1 to gap 2. Redraw the gate at gap 2 swung open. Over gap 2 write: made again HERE. Label a gap: node of Ranvier. Write: nothing jumps.
13

Part 12. Strip the wrapping from one stretch

55 sec

Now change one thing. In some diseases the myelin is lost from a stretch of axon. I have stripped it between gap 2 and gap 3. The membrane that was underneath has leak doors, and almost no sodium doors of its own. Gap 2 is firing. What happens to the current in the bare stretch? Does gap 3 still get enough?

The current leaks out through the uncovered leak doors, just like the leaky hose. Less reaches gap 3, so gap 3 takes longer to reach threshold, and the message is late. If too little arrives, gap 3 never reaches threshold, and the message stops. The bare stretch cannot help, because it has almost no sodium doors. The name is demyelination.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, an arrow from gap 2 along the inside that gets thinner, with small arrows going OUT through the two uncovered leak doors. In rust over gap 3 write: late, or not at all.
14

Part 13. Change the fluid outside: more potassium

55 sec

Now we change the fluid outside, and go back to Story 1. The inside of our resting cell is negative mostly because K+ leaks out. Suppose there is more K+ than normal outside. Does more or less K+ leak out? Is the inside more or less negative? And is the cell closer to threshold or farther from it?

Less K+ leaks out, because the difference between inside and outside is smaller, so the push out is weaker. Fewer positive charges leave, so the inside at rest is less negative. Less negative is closer to threshold. At first this cell is easier to fire. The name for more K+ than normal in the blood is hyperkalemia.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, thin arrows OUT through the two leak doors: write less leaks out. In navy, only a few minus signs inside the membrane and plus signs outside. On the scale at the right, a rust mark between normal rest and threshold: write new rest, closer.
15

Part 14. Held there for a long time

60 sec

Here is the second step. Remember the slow gate of the sodium door. It shut because the inside became less negative, and it could reset only after the inside went back to its negative resting level. This cell is held less negative, and it stays there. So what state do its sodium doors end up in? Can it fire?

Shut and not ready. The slow gates shut and cannot reset. No ready doors, so the cell cannot fire. Easier to fire at first, then not able to fire at all. And less K+ outside? That is the reverse of the last part. More leaks out, the inside is more negative, the cell is farther from threshold and harder to fire. Its name is hypokalemia.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, draw the ball up into the opening of the sodium door and write: shut and not ready, cannot fire. On the scale, a gray mark below normal rest: write less K+, farther.
16

Part 15. Numbing medicine

60 sec

Last one. This is the numbing medicine you get at the dentist. It blocks the voltage sensing sodium doors, only in the stretch where it was put. Here that is patch 2. Patch 1 has just fired. Where does the message stop? And is anything wrong with patch 3?

It stops at patch 2. Local current still flows in, but no sodium doors can open, so no new action potential is made, and the current fades like the little ripple. Nothing is wrong with patch 3. It is closed and ready, but no push ever reaches it. One blocked stretch is enough, because the message has to be made again at every patch. When the medicine is carried away, everything works again. The name is a local anesthetic.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, the local current arrow from patch 1 into patch 2, fading to a dashed line. In rust, a big X in patch 2 and write: STOPS here. Over patch 3 write in navy: ready, but no push arrives.
