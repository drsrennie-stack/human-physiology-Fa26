## Why this lab is clinical

This is the introduction I want students to read before they touch the first test.

The lab in this course is a clinical physiology lab, and the word clinical is doing real work there. It is not decoration and it is not an attempt to turn a physiology class into a medical one. It is there because physiology becomes memorizable trivia the moment it stops touching anything real, and it stays learnable when it does.

Here is the honest reason. Some of you are headed for provider roles, and the level you land at will decide how much of this you use directly. Some of you are headed for research, where you will meet the same measurements from the other side, as data you generate rather than data you act on. But every single one of you, regardless of where you end up, is going to sit in a room while someone explains a result. It will be your own lab work. It will be your parents, and later it will be your children. Somebody in that room needs to understand what the number is actually measuring, and what it would mean if it moved. That person can be you.

That is why the interpretation in this lab stops where it stops. You are not being asked to diagnose anyone. You are being asked to explain what the body is doing, which is both the more useful skill and the one that transfers everywhere.

## What you are actually practicing

Underneath the content, this lab is training four skills. They matter more than any individual test on the list, and they are the reason the lab exists as a lab instead of as reading.

**Measuring.** Taking a real value off a real instrument, and understanding what the instrument physically detects. Every method has a blind spot, and knowing the blind spot is part of knowing the measurement. A dipstick pad detects a chemical group, not a cell. A pulse oximeter needs a pulse to work at all. A refractometer and an osmometer will hand you two different answers about the same urine, and both are correct for what they measure.

**Calculating.** Working the equations rather than being handed the output. Mean arterial pressure, anion gap, cardiac output and index, systemic vascular resistance, the alveolar gas equation and the A-a gradient, fractional excretion of sodium, static compliance and driving pressure. When you have derived a number yourself, you know which inputs move it and by how much, and that is what makes you able to reason about it later.

**Collecting data.** Getting values in an organized, repeatable way, with the conditions recorded. Posture, timing, temperature, time of day, and how long a specimen sat all change results. A value without its conditions is not data yet.

**Interpreting data, including charts and graphs.** This is the one students most often skip and most often need. You will read curves, waveforms, tracings, and tables all term, and you will be expected to say what mechanism produced the shape in front of you. Not what it is called, what caused it.

## Reading charts, specifically

Chart reading is its own competency in this lab, and it runs through every unit.

- **Curves with a shape that means something.** The oxygen hemoglobin dissociation curve and what shifts it. The pressure volume loop of the ventricle. The flow volume loop and why its expiratory limb is effort independent. Frank-Starling and the length tension relationship. Enzyme kinetics and how inhibition changes the shape. In each case you should be able to say what the axes are, what the slope represents, and what physically has to change to move the line.
- **Waveforms and tracings over time.** The ECG, the capnography waveform, the arterial pressure trace, the jugular venous waveform, the ventilator scalars. These read left to right as a sequence of events, so the skill is naming which event each deflection corresponds to.
- **Titration and threshold plots.** Glucose filtration, reabsorption, and excretion against plasma concentration, with the transport maximum and the splay. Renal autoregulation of blood flow across a pressure range. Dose response and receptor occupancy.
- **Panels and tables of numbers.** A metabolic panel, a blood count, a blood gas, a urinalysis. These are charts too. The skill is reading them as a pattern rather than as a list, noticing which values move together, and asking what single mechanism could produce all of them at once.
- **Trends versus single values.** One creatinine tells you very little. Three creatinines over three days tell you what is happening. A rising and falling troponin means something different from a flat elevated one. Direction and rate of change carry information that any single point cannot.

For every chart in this lab, answer these four questions in order, out loud or on paper, before you say anything else about it. What are the axes and their units. What is the shape. What physically produces that shape. What would have to change to move it, and in which direction.

## How each test entry is built

Every test here is something you will run into in a clinic, a hospital, a chart, or a conversation with your own doctor. A test is a window onto a physiologic variable, and if you understand the variable you can reason about the number.

So each entry answers four questions and then two more:

1. **Measures.** What is physically being detected, in plain terms.
2. **Range.** Typical adult reference values, so students can see where the regulated variable normally sits.
3. **Loop.** The control system behind it. Sensor, integrator, effector, regulated variable. Where a test reads a structural property or a released molecule instead of a regulated set point, the loop line names that mechanism instead.
4. **Anatomy.** Where the value physically comes from and why that structure matters.

Then the two interpretation lines:

5. **High means.** What a raised value tells you about the physiology, at the mechanism level.
6. **Low means.** Same, in the other direction.

The high and low lines stay general on purpose. They describe what the body is doing, not what the patient has. No diagnoses, no disease lists. That boundary is what keeps this physiology instead of pathophysiology.

## 1. Foundations and whole-body control

### Core body temperature
- **Type.** Measurement, oral, tympanic, rectal, or esophageal probe.
- **Measures.** Heat content of the body core, reported as degrees.
- **Range.** 36.5 to 37.5 C, 97.7 to 99.5 F. Rectal and esophageal read a few tenths higher than oral.
- **Loop.** Sensor is peripheral and hypothalamic thermoreceptors. Integrator is the preoptic hypothalamus. Effectors are shivering, brown fat, cutaneous vasomotor tone, and sweat glands. Regulated variable is core temperature.
- **Anatomy.** Core means the trunk and cranial cavity, where enzyme-dependent organs sit. Skin and limbs are the shell and are allowed to swing.
- **High means.** The hypothalamic set point moved up, or heat production is outrunning heat loss, or heat loss is blocked. Enzyme rates and oxygen demand rise with it, roughly ten percent per degree C.
- **Low means.** Heat loss is outrunning production, or the thermoregulatory response is blunted. Enzyme rates and conduction velocity slow.

### Heart rate
- **Type.** Measurement, palpation, pulse oximeter, or monitor.
- **Measures.** Ventricular depolarizations per minute, or peripheral pulses per minute.
- **Range.** 60 to 100 beats per minute at rest in adults. Trained endurance athletes commonly sit in the 40s and 50s.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the arterial baroreceptor plus chemoreceptor and central drive. Integrator is the medullary cardiovascular center. Effector is autonomic input to the SA node. Regulated variable is arterial pressure, with rate as one of the two levers.
- **Anatomy.** The SA node in the right atrial wall sets the pace. Vagal fibers slow it, sympathetic fibers from the cardiac plexus speed it.
- **High means.** Sympathetic drive is up or vagal tone is down, usually because the body is defending cardiac output, oxygen delivery, or blood pressure. Filling time shortens as rate climbs, so stroke volume eventually falls.
- **Low means.** Vagal tone dominates, intrinsic nodal rate has fallen, or conduction to the ventricle is delayed. Output is preserved only if stroke volume rises to compensate.

### Blood pressure, systolic and diastolic
- **Type.** Measurement, cuff or arterial line.
- **Measures.** Lateral pressure the blood exerts on the arterial wall at peak ejection and at the end of filling.
- **Range.** Roughly 120 over 80 mmHg as the reference point in adults.
- **Loop.** Sensor is carotid sinus and aortic arch baroreceptors on a fast loop, and the renal juxtaglomerular apparatus on a slow loop. Integrator is medulla plus the renin angiotensin system. Effectors are heart rate, contractility, vessel radius, and blood volume. Regulated variable is mean arterial pressure.
- **Anatomy.** Systolic reflects ejection against aortic compliance. Diastolic reflects runoff through arterioles, so it is mostly a resistance number.
- **High means.** Cardiac output, peripheral resistance, blood volume, or arterial stiffness is raised. Afterload on the left ventricle rises with it.
- **Low means.** Output, resistance, or volume has dropped below what the baroreflex can correct. Perfusion pressure to brain and kidney is the thing at stake.

### Mean arterial pressure
- **Type.** Calculation or monitor readout.
- **Measures.** Average driving pressure across one cardiac cycle. Estimated as diastolic plus one third of pulse pressure.
- **Range.** 70 to 100 mmHg. About 60 to 65 is the usual floor for organ perfusion.
- **Loop.** Same loop as blood pressure. MAP is the variable the baroreflex actually defends.
- **Anatomy.** The number that matters at the level of the renal glomerulus and the cerebral arteriole, both of which autoregulate around it.
- **High means.** Perfusion pressure is generous and afterload is high.
- **Low means.** Driving pressure is approaching the bottom of the autoregulatory range, where flow starts tracking pressure directly instead of being defended.

### Pulse pressure
- **Type.** Calculation, systolic minus diastolic.
- **Measures.** The pressure swing generated by one stroke volume moving into the arterial tree.
- **Range.** 40 mmHg or so, roughly 25 to 50.
- **Loop.** Not a regulated variable. It is stroke volume divided by arterial compliance.
- **Anatomy.** The elastic aorta and great vessels absorb the ejection and give it back during diastole. Their elastin content is the compliance.
- **High means.** Stroke volume is large, arterial compliance is low, or both. Stiff conduit vessels widen the swing.
- **Low means.** Stroke volume is small relative to the arterial tree, so each beat moves the pressure less.

### Respiratory rate
- **Type.** Measurement, observed or monitored.
- **Measures.** Breathing cycles per minute.
- **Range.** 12 to 20 per minute in resting adults.
- **Loop.** Sensor is central medullary chemoreceptors reading CSF pH and peripheral carotid and aortic bodies reading oxygen, carbon dioxide, and pH. Integrator is the medullary respiratory center. Effector is the diaphragm and intercostals. Regulated variable is arterial carbon dioxide first, oxygen second.
- **Anatomy.** Phrenic output from C3 to C5 drives the diaphragm. Rate and depth together set alveolar ventilation.
- **High means.** Ventilatory drive is up, most often to blow off carbon dioxide or to raise oxygen delivery. Rapid shallow breathing raises dead space fraction and can move less air than it looks like.
- **Low means.** Drive is suppressed, the respiratory muscles are failing, or carbon dioxide is deliberately being retained to compensate for something else.

### Pulse oximetry, SpO2
- **Type.** Procedure, non-invasive optical.
- **Measures.** Percentage of hemoglobin binding sites occupied by oxygen, read from how pulsatile tissue absorbs red and infrared light.
- **Range.** 95 to 100 percent on room air.
- **Loop.** Not directly regulated. It sits on the oxygen hemoglobin dissociation curve, which is shifted by pH, carbon dioxide, temperature, and 2,3-BPG.
- **Anatomy.** Requires a pulsatile capillary bed, so a fingertip, earlobe, or forehead. Perfusion has to be adequate for the signal to exist.
- **High means.** Saturation is full. Note that above about 90 percent the curve is flat, so a normal reading can hide a falling arterial oxygen tension.
- **Low means.** Either arterial oxygen tension has fallen far enough to come down the steep part of the curve, or the curve has right shifted, or the reading is unreliable because perfusion is poor.

### Body mass index and body composition
- **Type.** Calculation, plus bioimpedance or DXA for composition.
- **Measures.** Mass relative to height squared. Composition methods separate fat mass from lean mass and bone.
- **Range.** BMI 18.5 to 24.9 as the conventional band. Composition ranges differ by sex and method.
- **Loop.** Sensor is adipose leptin plus gut hormones. Integrator is the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus. Effectors are appetite, activity, and metabolic rate. Regulated variable is long-term energy stores.
- **Anatomy.** Adipose tissue is an endocrine organ, not inert storage. Visceral and subcutaneous depots behave differently.
- **High means.** Stored energy exceeds the set point the system is defending, or lean mass is high enough to inflate a height-weight ratio that cannot tell muscle from fat.
- **Low means.** Energy stores are below the defended range, which lowers leptin and raises the drive to eat and conserve.

## 2. Molecules, enzymes, and energy

### Serum glucose
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Concentration of free glucose in plasma.
- **Range.** 70 to 99 mg/dL fasting.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the pancreatic beta cell reading glucose through GLUT2 and ATP-sensitive potassium channels. Integrator is the islet itself plus hypothalamic input. Effectors are insulin, glucagon, epinephrine, cortisol, and growth hormone. Regulated variable is plasma glucose.
- **Anatomy.** Islets of Langerhans, scattered through the pancreas. Beta cells in the core, alpha cells more peripheral, so paracrine crosstalk is built into the arrangement.
- **High means.** Glucose entering the blood exceeds glucose leaving it. Either the insulin signal is weak, the counter-regulatory signal is strong, or intake outran the response.
- **Low means.** Removal is outrunning supply. The brain has no glucose store, so counter-regulation fires hard and early.

### Hemoglobin A1c
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Fraction of hemoglobin that has been non-enzymatically glycated, which tracks average glucose exposure over the red cell lifespan.
- **Range.** 4 to 5.6 percent.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. It is a cumulative chemical record, so it reads the integral of glucose, not the current value.
- **Anatomy.** Red cells live about 120 days, so the value is weighted toward the last two to three months and is distorted by anything that changes cell lifespan.
- **High means.** Average glucose has been running above the reference band for weeks.
- **Low means.** Average glucose has been low, or red cells are being replaced faster than usual, which shortens the exposure window.

### Serum lactate
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Concentration of lactate, the end product of anaerobic glycolysis.
- **Range.** 0.5 to 2.2 mmol/L at rest.
- **Loop.** Not a set point. It reflects the balance between glycolytic flux and mitochondrial oxidative capacity, plus hepatic and renal clearance through the Cori cycle.
- **Anatomy.** Produced heavily by skeletal muscle, red cells, and gut. Cleared mostly by liver, some by kidney and heart.
- **High means.** Glycolysis is outrunning oxidative phosphorylation somewhere, or clearance has fallen. Either oxygen delivery is short or the cell cannot use the oxygen it has.
- **Low means.** Oxidative metabolism is comfortably keeping up. Rarely the interesting direction.

### Enzyme assay, general activity measurement
- **Type.** Lab, spectrophotometric.
- **Measures.** Rate of substrate conversion per unit time, usually by tracking absorbance change.
- **Range.** Depends entirely on the enzyme and the assay conditions.
- **Loop.** Not regulated in the homeostatic sense. Governed by enzyme concentration, substrate concentration, temperature, pH, and the presence of inhibitors or activators.
- **Anatomy.** Tissue of origin matters. An enzyme in serum came from cells that either secrete it or released it.
- **High means.** More active enzyme is present, or conditions favor faster turnover. Vmax reflects enzyme quantity, Km reflects affinity, and the two move independently.
- **Low means.** Less enzyme, less favorable conditions, or an inhibitor is present. Competitive inhibition raises apparent Km without changing Vmax, non-competitive lowers Vmax.

### Serum albumin
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Concentration of the main plasma protein.
- **Range.** 3.5 to 5.0 g/dL.
- **Loop.** Not tightly regulated to a set point. It is a balance of hepatic synthesis, distribution between plasma and interstitium, and losses.
- **Anatomy.** Made only by hepatocytes. Half-life around 20 days, so it changes slowly.
- **High means.** Usually the plasma water is down rather than the protein being up, so the concentration rises.
- **Low means.** Synthesis is down, the protein is leaking out, or plasma volume is expanded. Because albumin generates most of the plasma colloid osmotic pressure, low values shift Starling forces toward filtration into the interstitium.

### Serum total protein
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Albumin plus all globulins together.
- **Range.** 6.0 to 8.3 g/dL.
- **Loop.** Not regulated as a unit. It sums two independent pools with different sources.
- **Anatomy.** Albumin from liver, globulins largely from plasma cells and liver.
- **High means.** One of the two fractions is up, or plasma water is down. Always read with albumin to see which.
- **Low means.** Synthesis is down or losses are up. Colloid osmotic pressure falls with it.

### Serum osmolality
- **Type.** Lab, blood, freezing point depression.
- **Measures.** Total solute particle concentration per kilogram of water.
- **Range.** 275 to 295 mOsm/kg.
- **Loop.** Sensor is hypothalamic osmoreceptors in the organum vasculosum. Integrator is the hypothalamus. Effectors are ADH release from the posterior pituitary and thirst. Regulated variable is plasma osmolality, defended within about one percent.
- **Anatomy.** Sodium and its anions supply most of it, urea and glucose the rest. Estimated as 2 times Na, plus glucose over 18, plus BUN over 2.8.
- **High means.** Water is short relative to solute, or an unmeasured solute has been added. ADH and thirst should both be maximal.
- **Low means.** Water is in excess relative to solute. ADH should be fully suppressed, and if it is not, something is overriding the osmotic loop.

## 3. Membranes and transport

### Sweat chloride test
- **Type.** Procedure, pilocarpine iontophoresis with chloride measurement.
- **Measures.** Chloride concentration in stimulated sweat, which reports on epithelial chloride channel function.
- **Range.** Under 30 mmol/L is the normal band.
- **Loop.** Not a homeostatic loop. It is a direct functional test of a membrane transport protein in an intact epithelium.
- **Anatomy.** Eccrine sweat glands. The secretory coil makes an isotonic fluid, the reabsorptive duct pulls sodium and chloride back out without water following.
- **High means.** The duct is not reclaiming chloride, so the fluid reaching the skin stays salty. This is the classic teaching case for how one channel protein sets the composition of a secretion.
- **Low means.** Duct reabsorption is intact.

### Osmotic fragility testing
- **Type.** Lab, graded saline series.
- **Measures.** The tonicity at which red cells begin to lyse.
- **Range.** Hemolysis starts around 0.5 percent NaCl, complete near 0.3 percent.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. It is a direct read on membrane surface area to volume ratio and membrane deformability.
- **Anatomy.** The biconcave disc has excess surface area for its volume, which is exactly what lets it swell before it bursts.
- **High fragility means.** Cells lyse in a less dilute solution than normal, so surface area to volume is reduced and there is less room to swell.
- **Low fragility means.** Cells tolerate more swelling, so surface area to volume is unusually generous.

### Urine specific gravity
- **Type.** Lab, urine dipstick or refractometry.
- **Measures.** Density of urine relative to water, a proxy for solute concentration.
- **Range.** 1.005 to 1.030, and it should be able to swing across that whole span.
- **Loop.** Sensor is hypothalamic osmoreceptors and volume receptors. Integrator is hypothalamus. Effector is ADH acting on aquaporin-2 insertion in the collecting duct. Regulated variable is plasma osmolality and volume.
- **Anatomy.** Requires an intact medullary concentration gradient built by the loop of Henle countercurrent multiplier and maintained by the vasa recta.
- **High means.** Water is being reabsorbed aggressively, so ADH is acting and the medullary gradient is working.
- **Low means.** Water is being dumped, so ADH is suppressed, absent, or not being heard at the collecting duct. A value stuck near 1.010 in both directions means the kidney has lost the ability to move it at all.

## 4. Electrical signaling

### Serum potassium
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Extracellular potassium concentration.
- **Range.** 3.5 to 5.0 mEq/L.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the adrenal zona glomerulosa cell, which senses potassium directly. Integrator is the adrenal cortex plus renal tubule. Effectors are aldosterone driving renal secretion, and insulin and beta-2 stimulation driving cellular uptake. Regulated variable is extracellular potassium.
- **Anatomy.** Ninety-eight percent of body potassium is intracellular, so the measured pool is a small fraction sitting on top of a huge reservoir. The gradient across the membrane is what sets resting membrane potential.
- **High means.** The extracellular value has risen, so the potassium gradient shrinks and resting membrane potential moves closer to threshold. Excitable cells depolarize toward threshold at first, then inactivate sodium channels and become less excitable.
- **Low means.** The gradient steepens and resting potential hyperpolarizes, so cells sit farther from threshold and take longer to repolarize.

### Serum calcium, total and ionized
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Total calcium includes protein-bound and free. Ionized measures only the physiologically active free fraction.
- **Range.** Total 8.5 to 10.5 mg/dL. Ionized 4.6 to 5.3 mg/dL, about 1.15 to 1.35 mmol/L.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the calcium sensing receptor on parathyroid chief cells. Integrator is the parathyroid gland. Effectors are PTH acting on bone, kidney, and indirectly gut through calcitriol. Regulated variable is ionized calcium.
- **Anatomy.** Four parathyroid glands on the posterior thyroid. Bone is the reservoir, kidney the fine control, gut the intake gate.
- **High means.** Calcium entering the extracellular fluid exceeds removal. Membrane threshold shifts away from resting potential, so excitable tissue becomes harder to fire.
- **Low means.** Free calcium has fallen, threshold moves closer to resting potential, and nerve and muscle become hyperexcitable. Alkalosis lowers the ionized fraction without changing total, because albumin binds more calcium at higher pH.

### Serum magnesium
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Extracellular magnesium concentration.
- **Range.** 1.7 to 2.2 mg/dL.
- **Loop.** Loosely regulated. Renal handling in the thick ascending limb, driven largely by the same lumen positive potential that moves calcium.
- **Anatomy.** Mostly intracellular and in bone. Cofactor for every ATP-dependent reaction and a natural block on NMDA channels.
- **High means.** Neuromuscular transmission is damped, since magnesium competes with calcium at the presynaptic terminal and reduces acetylcholine release.
- **Low means.** Transmitter release goes up and excitability rises. It also impairs renal potassium retention, so low magnesium makes low potassium hard to correct.

### Electroencephalogram, EEG
- **Type.** Procedure, scalp electrodes.
- **Measures.** Summed postsynaptic potentials of cortical pyramidal neurons, read as voltage over time.
- **Range.** Reported by frequency band. Alpha 8 to 12 Hz in relaxed eyes-closed adults, beta 13 to 30 Hz with alert attention, theta 4 to 7 Hz, delta under 4 Hz in deep sleep.
- **Loop.** Not a control loop. It reflects thalamocortical synchrony and the level of ascending arousal input.
- **Anatomy.** Signal comes from cortex directly under the electrode, dominated by pyramidal cells whose dendrites are aligned perpendicular to the surface. That alignment is why the summed signal is detectable at all.
- **Fast, low amplitude means.** Cortical neurons are firing out of step with each other, which is what desynchronized alert processing looks like.
- **Slow, high amplitude means.** Large populations are firing together, which is synchrony driven by thalamic pacing and reduced ascending arousal.

### Nerve conduction study
- **Type.** Procedure, surface stimulation and recording.
- **Measures.** Conduction velocity in meters per second, plus amplitude and latency of the evoked response.
- **Range.** Roughly 50 to 60 m/s in large myelinated peripheral nerves. Slower in smaller and unmyelinated fibers.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. Velocity is set by axon diameter, myelination, and internodal distance.
- **Anatomy.** Saltatory conduction jumps between nodes of Ranvier. Myelin raises membrane resistance and lowers capacitance, which is the whole reason the jump works.
- **Slow velocity means.** The insulating or nodal properties of the axon are degraded, so current leaks and each node takes longer to reach threshold.
- **Low amplitude means.** Fewer axons are contributing to the response, so the population signal is smaller even if the surviving fibers conduct normally.

### Reflex testing, deep tendon reflexes
- **Type.** Procedure, tendon tap with graded response.
- **Measures.** Latency and force of a monosynaptic stretch reflex.
- **Range.** Graded 0 to 4, with 2 as the expected response.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the muscle spindle. Integrator is a single spinal synapse. Effector is the alpha motor neuron and its motor units. Regulated variable is muscle length.
- **Anatomy.** Ia afferent from the spindle synapses directly on the alpha motor neuron in the ventral horn. Gamma motor neurons set spindle sensitivity, and descending tracts set the overall gain.
- **Brisk means.** The loop gain is high, usually because descending inhibition onto the segment has been reduced.
- **Diminished means.** Somewhere in the arc the signal is not getting through, whether that is the afferent, the synapse, the motor neuron, or the muscle.

## 5. Nervous system

### Lumbar puncture with CSF analysis
- **Type.** Procedure plus lab.
- **Measures.** Opening pressure, plus CSF glucose, protein, cell count, and appearance.
- **Range.** Opening pressure 6 to 18 cmH2O lying down. CSF glucose about 60 percent of serum. Protein 15 to 45 mg/dL. Under 5 white cells per microliter, no red cells.
- **Loop.** CSF volume is set by choroid plexus secretion, roughly 500 mL per day, against arachnoid granulation reabsorption, with the total pool held near 150 mL. Intracranial pressure is buffered by the Monro-Kellie relationship between brain, blood, and CSF inside a fixed skull.
- **Anatomy.** Needle enters below the conus medullaris, usually L3 to L4 or L4 to L5, into the lumbar cistern where only cauda equina roots float.
- **High pressure means.** One of the three intracranial compartments has expanded and the others have run out of room to compensate. Cerebral perfusion pressure equals MAP minus ICP, so this directly threatens flow.
- **CSF composition shifts mean.** The blood brain barrier and choroid plexus normally hold protein low and glucose in a fixed ratio to serum. Protein rising or the glucose ratio falling tells you barrier permeability or local consumption has changed.

### Glasgow Coma Scale
- **Type.** Structured bedside assessment.
- **Measures.** Eye opening, verbal response, and motor response, summed.
- **Range.** 3 to 15, with 15 as fully awake and oriented.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It samples the ascending reticular activating system for arousal and cortical function for content.
- **Anatomy.** Arousal depends on brainstem reticular formation projecting through thalamus to cortex. Content depends on the cortex itself. The scale separates them.
- **Falling score means.** Either the ascending arousal pathway is being interrupted or cortical function is failing globally. Motor response carries the most information about brainstem level.

### Evoked potentials, visual, auditory, somatosensory
- **Type.** Procedure, timed stimulus with averaged EEG response.
- **Measures.** Latency and amplitude of a cortical response locked to a known stimulus.
- **Range.** Named by peak latency in milliseconds, for example the visual P100 near 100 ms.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. It times a defined sensory pathway from receptor to cortex.
- **Anatomy.** Each modality has a known relay chain, so a delay localizes to a segment of that chain.
- **Delayed latency means.** Conduction along the pathway is slowed, most often at a myelinated segment.
- **Reduced amplitude means.** Fewer fibers are carrying the signal, or the cortical population responding is smaller.

### Autonomic function testing, tilt table and Valsalva
- **Type.** Procedure, postural or pressure challenge with continuous heart rate and blood pressure.
- **Measures.** How fast and how completely the baroreflex corrects an imposed pressure change.
- **Range.** On standing, systolic should not fall more than 20 mmHg or diastolic more than 10 mmHg, and heart rate should rise about 10 to 20 beats per minute.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the carotid and aortic baroreceptors. Integrator is the medulla. Effectors are heart rate, contractility, venous tone, and arteriolar resistance. Regulated variable is mean arterial pressure.
- **Anatomy.** Standing shifts about 500 to 800 mL of blood into the legs and splanchnic veins, so venous return falls before the reflex can respond.
- **Exaggerated fall means.** The reflex is not compensating, whether because the sensor, the central pathway, the efferent limb, or the circulating volume is inadequate.
- **Excessive rate rise means.** The rate limb is working hard to defend a pressure that the vascular limb is not holding.

### Pupillary light reflex
- **Type.** Bedside procedure.
- **Measures.** Constriction of both pupils to light in one eye, and the speed of it.
- **Range.** Brisk, equal, and consensual. Resting diameter 2 to 4 mm in light.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the retinal ganglion cell. Integrator is the pretectal nucleus and Edinger-Westphal nucleus. Effector is parasympathetic fibers riding cranial nerve III to the sphincter pupillae. Regulated variable is retinal illumination.
- **Anatomy.** The afferent limb crosses at the pretectum to both Edinger-Westphal nuclei, which is exactly why lighting one eye constricts both.
- **Sluggish or absent means.** The afferent limb, the midbrain relay, or the efferent parasympathetic limb is interrupted. Testing each eye separately separates afferent from efferent.

## 6. Muscle

### Creatine kinase, CK
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Activity of the enzyme that regenerates ATP from phosphocreatine.
- **Range.** Roughly 30 to 200 U/L, higher in people with more muscle mass and after exercise.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. It is an intracellular enzyme, so serum levels report membrane integrity of muscle cells.
- **Anatomy.** Three isoforms by tissue. MM in skeletal muscle, MB in cardiac muscle, BB in brain. The isoform tells you the source.
- **High means.** Muscle cell membranes are releasing contents into the interstitium and then the blood. The magnitude tracks how much muscle is involved, not how sick the person is.
- **Low means.** Little muscle mass or low baseline turnover. Rarely the informative direction.
- **Read it with the urine.** When large amounts of skeletal muscle break down, the same event puts myoglobin in the urine, which turns the dipstick heme pad positive with no red cells on microscopy, and pushes potassium and phosphate out of the same cells while calcium falls binding to that phosphate. See the urinalysis discordance section in the renal unit, since this is one mechanism visible in four different samples.

### Electromyography, EMG
- **Type.** Procedure, needle or surface electrodes.
- **Measures.** Electrical activity of muscle fibers at rest and during voluntary effort, reported as motor unit potential size, shape, and recruitment pattern.
- **Range.** Electrical silence at rest, orderly recruitment with increasing effort.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. It reads the motor unit, meaning one alpha motor neuron and every fiber it innervates.
- **Anatomy.** Motor unit size varies by job. Extraocular muscles run a handful of fibers per neuron for fine control, gastrocnemius runs a thousand or more for force.
- **Spontaneous activity at rest means.** Fibers are depolarizing without a motor neuron telling them to, which happens when they have lost their innervation and upregulated receptors.
- **Small, early-recruited units mean.** Each unit generates less force, so more units have to be called in earlier to produce the same output.
- **Large units mean.** Surviving motor neurons have taken over orphaned fibers, so each unit is bigger than it should be.

### Grip strength dynamometry
- **Type.** Procedure, handheld dynamometer.
- **Measures.** Maximum voluntary isometric force in kilograms or pounds.
- **Range.** Broad, and interpreted against age and sex norms rather than a single number.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. Force output is set by cross-sectional area, motor unit recruitment, firing rate, and sarcomere length at the tested joint angle.
- **Anatomy.** Forearm flexor compartment acting through long tendons. Joint angle changes sarcomere overlap, which is why the test is standardized for position.
- **High means.** Contractile mass and neural drive are both good.
- **Low means.** Fewer or smaller fibers, reduced recruitment, or the test position put sarcomeres off the plateau of the length tension curve.

### Exercise stress testing with VO2 max
- **Type.** Procedure, graded exercise with expired gas analysis.
- **Measures.** Maximum rate of oxygen consumption per kilogram per minute, plus the ventilatory and heart rate responses on the way there.
- **Range.** Roughly 30 to 45 mL/kg/min in untrained adults, above 60 in trained endurance athletes.
- **Loop.** Not a set point. It is the ceiling of the whole oxygen cascade, from ventilation through diffusion, cardiac output, and mitochondrial extraction. Fick relationship, VO2 equals cardiac output times the arteriovenous oxygen difference.
- **Anatomy.** The usual limiting step is cardiac output, specifically maximum stroke volume, not lung or muscle.
- **High means.** Every link in the oxygen cascade is generous, with cardiac output usually the reason.
- **Low means.** One link is limiting. The pattern of ventilation, heart rate, and extraction at the ceiling tells you which one, which makes this the best single teaching test for integrated physiology.

### Serum myoglobin
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Concentration of the intracellular oxygen binding protein of muscle.
- **Range.** Under 85 ng/mL, roughly.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. Released on muscle cell injury, cleared by the kidney.
- **Anatomy.** Myoglobin has higher oxygen affinity than hemoglobin, which is what lets it pull oxygen off blood and hold it inside the fiber.
- **High means.** Muscle contents are entering the circulation. It rises earlier than CK because the molecule is small and diffuses out fast, and it clears fast for the same reason.
- **Where it goes.** At about 17 kDa and carried by no binding protein, myoglobin is freely filtered, so it reaches the urine quickly and in quantity while plasma stays visually clear. Filtered heme pigment is also directly toxic to tubular cells and precipitates with Tamm-Horsfall protein in acidic urine, which is why the kidney is the organ that pays for a muscle event.

## 7. Chemical signaling and endocrine

### Thyroid stimulating hormone, TSH
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Anterior pituitary thyrotropin concentration.
- **Range.** 0.4 to 4.0 mIU/L.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the pituitary thyrotroph reading local T3 converted from T4. Integrator is hypothalamus and pituitary. Effector is thyroid follicular cell hormone synthesis. Regulated variable is circulating thyroid hormone. Negative feedback, and the pituitary response is logarithmic, so small hormone changes make large TSH changes.
- **Anatomy.** Hypothalamic TRH reaches the anterior pituitary through the hypophyseal portal system, not the general circulation, which is why this axis can be regulated locally.
- **High means.** The pituitary is not seeing enough thyroid hormone at the tissue level, so it is pushing harder on the gland.
- **Low means.** The pituitary is seeing plenty of thyroid hormone, so it has backed off, or the pituitary signal itself has failed.

### Free T4 and free T3
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** The unbound, biologically available fraction of thyroxine and triiodothyronine.
- **Range.** Free T4 0.8 to 1.8 ng/dL. Free T3 2.3 to 4.2 pg/mL.
- **Loop.** The output arm of the same axis. T4 is the reservoir hormone, deiodinases in target tissue convert it to the active T3 locally, which is a second layer of control the blood test cannot see.
- **Anatomy.** Made in thyroid follicles on a thyroglobulin scaffold, stored extracellularly in colloid, which is unusual for a hormone.
- **High means.** More active hormone is reaching receptors, so basal metabolic rate, sodium potassium ATPase density, and beta receptor expression all rise.
- **Low means.** Less receptor occupancy, so metabolic rate and adrenergic sensitivity fall.

### Serum cortisol with diurnal timing
- **Type.** Lab, blood, timed. Salivary or 24 hour urinary free cortisol as alternatives.
- **Measures.** Circulating glucocorticoid concentration at a stated clock time.
- **Range.** Morning peak 6 to 23 mcg/dL, late evening trough under 5.
- **Loop.** Sensor is hypothalamic CRH neurons plus limbic input. Integrator is hypothalamus and pituitary. Effector is adrenal cortex zona fasciculata. Regulated variable is cortisol itself, with negative feedback at both pituitary and hypothalamus, overlaid on a circadian rhythm from the suprachiasmatic nucleus.
- **Anatomy.** Three cortical zones with three products. Glomerulosa makes aldosterone, fasciculata cortisol, reticularis androgens. Medulla is separate and is really sympathetic tissue.
- **High means.** Drive on the axis is up or feedback is not being heard. Physiologically it mobilizes glucose, raises vascular responsiveness to catecholamines, and suppresses immune signaling.
- **Low means.** Central drive or adrenal output is inadequate. Without cortisol the vasculature responds poorly to catecholamines and gluconeogenesis falls off.

### ACTH stimulation test, cosyntropin
- **Type.** Procedure, synthetic ACTH given with timed cortisol draws.
- **Measures.** Whether the adrenal cortex can produce cortisol when maximally stimulated.
- **Range.** Cortisol should rise above roughly 18 to 20 mcg/dL at 30 or 60 minutes.
- **Loop.** A deliberate override of the feedback loop. It removes the question of central drive and tests only the effector organ.
- **Anatomy.** ACTH acts on melanocortin-2 receptors on fasciculata cells, driving cholesterol into the mitochondrion through StAR protein, which is the rate limiting step.
- **Blunted response means.** The gland itself cannot make the hormone, either because tissue is missing or because it has been unstimulated long enough to atrophy.
- **Brisk response means.** The gland is capable, so any deficiency lies upstream in the hypothalamus or pituitary.

### Parathyroid hormone, PTH
- **Type.** Lab, blood, drawn with a simultaneous calcium.
- **Measures.** Intact PTH concentration.
- **Range.** 15 to 65 pg/mL.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the calcium sensing receptor on the chief cell. Integrator is the parathyroid gland. Effectors are osteoclast activity through RANKL, distal tubule calcium reabsorption, proximal tubule phosphate wasting, and renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase making calcitriol. Regulated variable is ionized calcium.
- **Anatomy.** The receptor is unusual in that binding calcium turns secretion off, so the gland is inhibited by its own regulated variable.
- **High means.** The gland has been told calcium is low, or it is secreting without listening. Read only alongside calcium, because the pair tells you whether the gland is responding appropriately or driving the problem.
- **Low means.** The gland is seeing plenty of calcium, or it cannot secrete.

### Insulin and C-peptide
- **Type.** Lab, blood, usually fasting or during a glucose challenge.
- **Measures.** Insulin concentration, and C-peptide as a marker of endogenous secretion.
- **Range.** Fasting insulin 2 to 25 mIU/L. C-peptide 0.5 to 2.0 ng/mL.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the beta cell. Effectors are GLUT4 translocation in muscle and fat, hepatic glycogen synthesis, and suppression of lipolysis. Regulated variable is plasma glucose.
- **Anatomy.** Proinsulin is cleaved into insulin plus C-peptide in equal amounts, and C-peptide is not extracted by the liver, which is why it reports secretion more faithfully than insulin does.
- **High means.** Beta cells are working hard, either because glucose is high or because target tissues are responding weakly to the signal.
- **Low means.** Secretory capacity is limited, or glucose is low and the beta cell has appropriately gone quiet. C-peptide separates injected insulin from made insulin.

### Growth hormone and IGF-1
- **Type.** Lab, blood. GH is pulsatile, so IGF-1 is the usable integrated measure.
- **Measures.** GH concentration at a moment, and IGF-1 as the liver's summed response to GH over time.
- **Range.** IGF-1 is reported against age norms. Random GH is often under 5 ng/mL and not interpretable alone.
- **Loop.** Sensor and integrator are hypothalamic GHRH and somatostatin neurons. Effector is the pituitary somatotroph, then hepatic IGF-1 production. IGF-1 feeds back on both hypothalamus and pituitary.
- **Anatomy.** GH acts directly on adipose and muscle, and indirectly on the epiphyseal growth plate through IGF-1.
- **High IGF-1 means.** Sustained GH exposure at the liver, so anabolic and anti-insulin effects are running high.
- **Low IGF-1 means.** GH signaling is weak, or the liver cannot respond, or nutrition is inadequate, since IGF-1 falls with undernutrition regardless of GH.

### Aldosterone and plasma renin activity
- **Type.** Lab, blood, usually reported as a ratio with posture noted.
- **Measures.** Aldosterone concentration and the rate of angiotensin I generation.
- **Range.** Upright aldosterone 4 to 31 ng/dL. Plasma renin activity 0.6 to 4.3 ng/mL/hr. Interpretation rests on the ratio between them.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the juxtaglomerular cell reading afferent arteriolar stretch, the macula densa reading tubular sodium chloride, and the glomerulosa cell reading potassium directly. Effectors are principal cell sodium channels and potassium secretion in the collecting duct. Regulated variables are effective circulating volume and potassium.
- **Anatomy.** The juxtaglomerular apparatus sits where the distal tubule touches its own afferent arteriole, which is the anatomic basis for tubuloglomerular feedback.
- **High aldosterone with high renin means.** The system is responding to a real or perceived volume signal.
- **High aldosterone with low renin means.** The effector is running independently of its input, so the feedback arm has been bypassed.

### Antidiuretic hormone, ADH, with paired osmolality
- **Type.** Lab, blood, with simultaneous serum and urine osmolality. Water deprivation testing as the functional version.
- **Measures.** Vasopressin concentration, or functionally, whether urine concentrates when plasma osmolality rises.
- **Range.** Interpreted against plasma osmolality rather than as a standalone number. Urine should exceed 600 to 800 mOsm/kg when water restricted.
- **Loop.** Sensor is hypothalamic osmoreceptors, plus atrial and arterial baroreceptors for the volume arm. Integrator is supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei. Effector is aquaporin-2 insertion into collecting duct apical membrane. Regulated variable is plasma osmolality, with volume able to override it.
- **Anatomy.** Made in hypothalamic cell bodies and transported down axons to the posterior pituitary, which stores and releases rather than synthesizes.
- **Urine concentrates appropriately.** Hormone, receptor, aquaporin, and medullary gradient are all intact.
- **Urine stays dilute means.** Either the hormone is not being released or the collecting duct is not answering it. Giving desmopressin separates those two.

### Catecholamines, plasma metanephrines
- **Type.** Lab, blood or 24 hour urine.
- **Measures.** Epinephrine, norepinephrine, and their O-methylated metabolites.
- **Range.** Plasma free metanephrines under about 0.5 nmol/L, normetanephrine under about 0.9 nmol/L.
- **Loop.** Not a classic negative feedback loop. Output is driven by sympathetic preganglionic input to the adrenal medulla and by postganglionic spillover.
- **Anatomy.** The adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion whose cells lost their axons and secrete into blood instead. It sits inside the cortex, so medullary cells bathe in high local cortisol, which is what induces the enzyme that converts norepinephrine to epinephrine.
- **High means.** Sympathoadrenal output is elevated, which raises heart rate, contractility, glycogenolysis, and lipolysis, and redistributes flow.
- **Low means.** Little useful information on its own, since basal secretion is low by design.

## 8. Reproduction

### LH and FSH
- **Type.** Lab, blood, timed to cycle day where relevant.
- **Measures.** Anterior pituitary gonadotropin concentrations.
- **Range.** Follicular phase LH 2 to 12 IU/L, FSH 3 to 12 IU/L, with an LH surge above 20 at midcycle. Adult male LH 1.5 to 9, FSH 1.5 to 12.
- **Loop.** Sensor and integrator are hypothalamic GnRH neurons, released in pulses. Effector is the pituitary gonadotroph, then the gonad. Regulated variables are gonadal steroids and inhibin. Feedback is negative for most of the cycle and flips to positive when estradiol stays high long enough to trigger the LH surge.
- **Anatomy.** GnRH must be pulsatile. Continuous exposure downregulates the receptor and shuts the axis off, which is a clean demonstration of receptor regulation.
- **High means.** The gonad is not producing enough steroid or inhibin to restrain the pituitary, so drive rises.
- **Low means.** Gonadal feedback is strong, or the hypothalamic pulse generator is suppressed. Energy deficit, stress, and high prolactin all suppress the pulse generator.

### Estradiol and progesterone
- **Type.** Lab, blood, cycle timed.
- **Measures.** Concentrations of the two ovarian steroids.
- **Range.** Estradiol 20 to 400 pg/mL across the cycle with a preovulatory peak. Progesterone under 1 ng/mL in the follicular phase, above 5 to 10 in the luteal phase.
- **Loop.** Output arm of the HPG axis. Estradiol from granulosa cells, progesterone from the corpus luteum after ovulation.
- **Anatomy.** Two cell two gonadotropin model. Theca cells respond to LH and make androgen, granulosa cells respond to FSH and aromatize it to estradiol.
- **Estradiol high means.** Follicular development is active, and the endometrium is in its proliferative response.
- **Progesterone above the luteal threshold means.** Ovulation happened and a corpus luteum formed, which is the whole reason the test is drawn a week before the expected period.

### Testosterone, total and free
- **Type.** Lab, blood, drawn in the morning.
- **Measures.** Total testosterone plus the fraction not bound to sex hormone binding globulin.
- **Range.** Adult male total 300 to 1000 ng/dL, with a morning peak.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the hypothalamus and pituitary reading testosterone and inhibin B. Effector is the Leydig cell for steroid and the Sertoli cell for spermatogenesis. Regulated variable is gonadal steroid output.
- **Anatomy.** LH drives Leydig cells in the interstitium, FSH drives Sertoli cells inside the seminiferous tubule. The blood testis barrier between Sertoli cells keeps the tubular environment separate from plasma.
- **High means.** Gonadal or adrenal androgen output is up, or binding protein is high enough to raise total without raising free.
- **Low means.** Gonadal output or central drive is down. Pairing it with LH separates the two, since a low testosterone with high LH points at the gonad and with low LH points upstream.

### Beta hCG
- **Type.** Lab, blood quantitative or urine qualitative.
- **Measures.** Human chorionic gonadotropin concentration.
- **Range.** Under 5 mIU/mL when not pregnant. Roughly doubles every 48 to 72 hours in early pregnancy.
- **Loop.** Not a feedback loop in the usual sense. hCG is the embryonic signal that rescues the corpus luteum from its programmed regression.
- **Anatomy.** Made by syncytiotrophoblast. It shares an alpha subunit with LH, FSH, and TSH, which is why the beta subunit is what gets measured and why very high levels can cross-stimulate the TSH receptor.
- **Rising means.** Trophoblast tissue is present and growing, so progesterone support of the endometrium continues.
- **Falling or plateauing means.** Trophoblastic output is not increasing as expected, so luteal rescue is not being sustained.

### Semen analysis
- **Type.** Lab, specimen.
- **Measures.** Volume, sperm concentration, total count, motility, and morphology.
- **Range.** Volume 1.5 mL or more, concentration 15 million per mL or more, total motility 40 percent or more.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It reports the output of a 70 day production cycle plus accessory gland function.
- **Anatomy.** Sperm made in seminiferous tubules, matured and stored in the epididymis, with most of the fluid volume coming from seminal vesicles and prostate. Testicular temperature sits a couple of degrees below core, maintained by the pampiniform plexus countercurrent exchanger and the cremaster.
- **Low concentration or motility means.** Something disrupted production or maturation, and because the cycle is 70 days, the test reflects conditions two to three months back.
- **Low volume means.** Accessory gland contribution or emission is the issue rather than sperm production.

### Basal body temperature charting
- **Type.** Measurement, daily on waking.
- **Measures.** Resting core temperature trend across a cycle.
- **Range.** A sustained rise of about 0.3 to 0.5 C after ovulation.
- **Loop.** Progesterone raises the hypothalamic thermoregulatory set point, so the same body defends a slightly higher temperature.
- **Anatomy.** The corpus luteum is the source, so the shift is a bedside readout of luteal function.
- **Biphasic pattern means.** Ovulation occurred and a corpus luteum is producing progesterone.
- **Flat pattern means.** No sustained progesterone rise, so no luteal phase to detect.

## 9. Digestion, liver, and energy balance

### ALT and AST
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Activity of two transaminases that move amino groups between amino acids and keto acids.
- **Range.** ALT 7 to 56 U/L, AST 10 to 40 U/L.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. These are intracellular enzymes, so serum activity reports hepatocyte membrane leakage.
- **Anatomy.** ALT is concentrated in liver cytosol and is the more liver-specific of the pair. AST is in liver, cardiac and skeletal muscle, red cells, and kidney, and much of it is mitochondrial.
- **High means.** Cell contents are entering the blood. The ratio between them carries information about which cell compartment and which tissue, because a mitochondrial enzyme leaking implies deeper disruption than a cytosolic one.
- **Low means.** Not informative. Very low values sometimes reflect reduced enzyme cofactor availability.

### Alkaline phosphatase
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Activity of a membrane-bound enzyme that removes phosphate groups at alkaline pH.
- **Range.** 44 to 147 U/L in adults, higher in growing adolescents and in pregnancy.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. Expression is induced rather than released, so it rises over days, not minutes.
- **Anatomy.** Two main sources, the canalicular membrane of hepatocytes, where adjacent apical membranes form the bile canaliculi that feed the ducts, and osteoblasts. Placenta and intestine contribute too.
- **High means.** Either biliary canalicular membranes are being induced or bone formation is active. Fractionating by isoenzyme, or pairing with GGT, separates the two sources.
- **Low means.** Rarely useful. Very low values point at reduced cofactor availability or reduced bone turnover.

### Total and direct bilirubin
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Total heme breakdown product, and the conjugated water-soluble fraction.
- **Range.** Total 0.1 to 1.2 mg/dL, direct under 0.3 mg/dL.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. It is a throughput measure across three sequential steps, production, hepatic conjugation, and biliary excretion.
- **Anatomy.** Heme from senescent red cells becomes unconjugated bilirubin, which is lipid soluble and rides on albumin. Hepatocytes conjugate it with glucuronic acid, making it water soluble so it can enter bile.
- **Mostly unconjugated high means.** Production is outrunning conjugation, or the uptake and conjugation step is limited.
- **Mostly conjugated high means.** The liver did its chemistry but the product is not reaching the gut, so it backs up into blood.

### Serum ammonia
- **Type.** Lab, blood, handled on ice.
- **Measures.** Free ammonia concentration.
- **Range.** 15 to 45 mcg/dL.
- **Loop.** Not a set point. Ammonia is generated by gut bacteria and by protein and amino acid catabolism, and is cleared by the hepatic urea cycle and by glutamine synthesis.
- **Anatomy.** Portal blood carries gut ammonia straight to hepatocytes, so first pass extraction normally keeps systemic levels low. Periportal hepatocytes run the urea cycle, perivenous ones mop up with glutamine synthetase.
- **High means.** Either production exceeds capacity or portal blood is bypassing hepatocytes. Astrocytes are the tissue that pays, since they handle brain ammonia by making glutamine, which is osmotically active.
- **Low means.** Not clinically pursued.

### Lipase and amylase
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Activity of two pancreatic digestive enzymes in serum.
- **Range.** Lipase 10 to 140 U/L, amylase 30 to 110 U/L.
- **Loop.** Not regulated in serum. Secretion into the duct is regulated by CCK and vagal input, so serum presence means the enzymes escaped the duct.
- **Anatomy.** Acinar cells make them. Protease precursors are made as inactive zymogens for obvious reasons, while lipase and amylase are secreted already active because they do not threaten the gland's own protein.
- **High means.** Acinar contents are reaching the circulation instead of the duodenum. Lipase stays elevated longer because it is cleared more slowly.
- **Low means.** Little functioning acinar tissue, so digestive capacity for fat and starch is reduced.

### Lipid panel
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, and calculated or measured LDL cholesterol.
- **Range.** Total under 200 mg/dL, triglycerides under 150, HDL above 40 to 50, LDL under 100.
- **Loop.** Regulated at the cell level rather than the blood level. Intracellular sterol content controls SREBP, which sets both LDL receptor expression and cholesterol synthesis.
- **Anatomy.** Lipoproteins are transport vehicles because lipid does not dissolve in plasma. Chylomicrons carry dietary fat from enterocytes, VLDL carries hepatic triglyceride out, LDL delivers cholesterol to tissue, HDL brings it back.
- **High LDL means.** More cholesterol is being delivered to peripheral tissue than receptors are clearing.
- **High triglycerides mean.** Triglyceride-rich particles are entering faster than lipoprotein lipase is stripping them, which is directly tied to insulin, since insulin activates that enzyme.
- **High HDL means.** Reverse transport capacity is generous.

### Fecal fat, quantitative
- **Type.** Lab, 72 hour stool collection on a defined fat intake.
- **Measures.** Grams of fat excreted per day.
- **Range.** Under 7 grams per day on a 100 gram fat diet.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It tests the whole fat handling sequence, emulsification, lipolysis, micelle formation, absorption, and lymphatic export.
- **Anatomy.** Bile salts from the liver emulsify, pancreatic lipase digests, brush border absorbs, and chylomicrons leave through lacteals rather than capillaries because they are too large for the endothelium.
- **High means.** One of those four steps failed. Which one requires a different test, but the physiology teaching point is that fat absorption uniquely needs a detergent, an enzyme, a membrane, and a lymphatic.

### Hydrogen breath test
- **Type.** Procedure, timed breath sampling after a sugar load.
- **Measures.** Hydrogen and methane in exhaled breath.
- **Range.** A rise of more than 20 ppm over baseline is the usual threshold.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. Human cells cannot make hydrogen gas, so any hydrogen in breath came from bacterial fermentation of carbohydrate that the small intestine did not absorb.
- **Anatomy.** Unabsorbed sugar reaching colonic bacteria gets fermented, the gas diffuses into portal blood, crosses the lung, and appears in breath. That is a lovely gas transport story in itself.
- **Early rise means.** Fermentation is happening more proximally than it should.
- **Late rise means.** The sugar reached the colon unabsorbed, so brush border disaccharidase capacity or transport is limited.

### Upper endoscopy and colonoscopy
- **Type.** Procedure, direct visualization with optional biopsy.
- **Measures.** Mucosal surface appearance, plus tissue architecture on biopsy.
- **Range.** Descriptive rather than numeric.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It is an anatomy test that answers physiologic questions about surface area and barrier.
- **Anatomy.** Small intestinal absorptive area comes from three nested amplifications, circular folds, villi, and microvilli, together multiplying flat surface area by hundreds of times. Biopsy shows whether that architecture is intact.
- **Blunted villi mean.** Absorptive surface area has dropped, which reduces the capacity for every nutrient that depends on brush border transporters.

### Gastric emptying study
- **Type.** Procedure, scintigraphy after a labeled meal.
- **Measures.** Percent of the meal remaining in the stomach at set time points.
- **Range.** Under 10 percent remaining at 4 hours is typical.
- **Loop.** Sensor is duodenal osmoreceptors, chemoreceptors, and stretch receptors. Integrator is the enteric nervous system with vagal input. Effectors are gastric fundic tone, antral pumping, and pyloric resistance. Regulated variable is the delivery rate of chyme to the duodenum.
- **Anatomy.** Interstitial cells of Cajal set the slow wave frequency, about three per minute in the stomach, which is the pacemaker for the whole process.
- **Delayed means.** Either the pump is weak or the brake is too strong. The duodenal feedback arm exists to keep osmotic and acid load within what the small intestine can neutralize and dilute.
- **Rapid means.** The brake is not being applied, so a hyperosmolar load hits the duodenum and pulls water into the lumen.

## 10. Cardiac function

### Electrocardiogram, 12-lead
- **Type.** Procedure, surface electrodes.
- **Measures.** The summed electrical vector of cardiac depolarization and repolarization over time, viewed from twelve angles.
- **Range.** Rate 60 to 100 beats per minute. PR interval 120 to 200 ms. QRS under 120 ms. QTc under about 440 to 460 ms.
- **Loop.** Not a control loop itself, but rate and conduction are the output of autonomic control on the nodes.
- **Anatomy.** P wave is atrial depolarization. PR segment is the AV nodal delay, which exists so atria finish emptying before ventricles contract. QRS is ventricular depolarization through the His-Purkinje system, fast because those fibers are large and well coupled. T wave is ventricular repolarization, upright because repolarization runs epicardium to endocardium, the reverse of depolarization.
- **Prolonged PR means.** Conduction through the AV node is slower, so the atrial to ventricular handoff is delayed.
- **Wide QRS means.** The ventricle is being activated outside the fast conducting system, so depolarization spreads muscle to muscle.
- **Long QT means.** Repolarization is taking longer, usually because potassium efflux is reduced or calcium influx is prolonged, which lengthens the vulnerable window.
- **ST segment shifts mean.** The ventricle has regions at different resting membrane potentials during what should be an electrically uniform phase, which is what a mismatch between oxygen supply and demand produces.

### Echocardiogram
- **Type.** Procedure, ultrasound of the heart.
- **Measures.** Chamber dimensions, wall thickness, wall motion, valve motion, and Doppler-derived flow velocities. Ejection fraction is calculated from end diastolic and end systolic volumes.
- **Range.** Ejection fraction 55 to 70 percent. Left ventricular wall under 1.1 cm. E to A ratio near 1 or a bit above in healthy adults.
- **Loop.** Reads the mechanical output of the loop that defends arterial pressure. Preload, afterload, and contractility are all visible in what it shows.
- **Anatomy.** The parasternal, apical, and subcostal windows exist because ultrasound cannot cross bone or air, so images are taken between ribs and under the sternum.
- **Low ejection fraction means.** The ventricle is emptying a smaller fraction of what it holds, so either contractility is reduced or afterload is high enough to limit shortening.
- **High ejection fraction with a small cavity means.** The chamber is emptying well but starting small, so stroke volume can still be low even with an excellent percentage. This is the single best argument for teaching fraction and volume as separate ideas.
- **Thick walls mean.** The myocardium added sarcomeres in parallel, which is what chronic pressure load produces, and thick stiff walls fill less easily.
- **Doppler inflow pattern.** Compares early passive filling to atrial contribution, so it reports how compliant the ventricle is during diastole rather than how strongly it squeezes.

### Cardiac troponin I or T
- **Type.** Lab, blood, serial.
- **Measures.** Concentration of a regulatory protein of the cardiac thin filament.
- **Range.** Below the assay's 99th percentile cutoff, which is assay specific and often in the nanograms per liter range.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. It is a structural protein, so its presence in blood means cardiac myocyte membrane integrity was lost.
- **Anatomy.** The troponin complex sits on actin. Troponin C binds calcium, troponin I inhibits the actin myosin interaction at rest, troponin T anchors the complex to tropomyosin. The cardiac isoforms differ from skeletal, which is what makes the test tissue specific.
- **Rising means.** Cardiac myocyte contents are entering the circulation. The rise and fall pattern matters more than a single value, because a changing level indicates an active process rather than a chronic one.
- **Flat and normal means.** Myocyte membranes have been intact over the sampled window.

### B-type natriuretic peptide, BNP or NT-proBNP
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Concentration of a peptide released by stretched ventricular myocardium.
- **Range.** BNP under 100 pg/mL. NT-proBNP thresholds rise with age.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the ventricular myocyte responding to wall stretch. Effectors are natriuresis, vasodilation, and inhibition of renin and aldosterone. Regulated variable is blood volume and pressure. It is the physiologic counterweight to the renin angiotensin aldosterone system.
- **Anatomy.** ANP comes from atrial myocytes on atrial stretch, BNP from ventricular myocytes on ventricular stretch, so the two report different chambers.
- **High means.** Wall stress is elevated, which is a function of pressure times radius over wall thickness, the Laplace relationship. The heart is signaling for volume unloading.
- **Low means.** Wall stretch is not elevated, which is useful mostly as a way to rule stretch out.

### Cardiac stress testing
- **Type.** Procedure, exercise or pharmacologic, with ECG and often imaging.
- **Measures.** Whether the heart's oxygen supply can keep up with a controlled increase in demand.
- **Range.** Interpreted by achieved workload in METs, blood pressure and heart rate response, symptoms, and ECG or wall motion change.
- **Loop.** Exercise raises sympathetic drive and lowers vagal tone, which raises rate and contractility, while local metabolites dilate the coronary bed.
- **Anatomy.** Coronary filling happens mostly in diastole, especially for the left ventricle, because systolic wall tension squeezes the intramural vessels shut. Faster rates shorten diastole, so demand goes up exactly when supply time goes down.
- **Positive response means.** Somewhere in the coronary bed, flow reserve was used up before the workload was, so a region became underperfused relative to demand.
- **Blunted blood pressure rise means.** Cardiac output is not increasing with workload as it should.

### Cardiac catheterization with coronary angiography
- **Type.** Procedure, invasive, with contrast.
- **Measures.** Coronary anatomy and flow, plus chamber pressures and, where measured, cardiac output.
- **Range.** Left ventricular end diastolic pressure 5 to 12 mmHg. Peripheral systolic normally runs 10 to 20 mmHg above central aortic systolic because reflected waves summate distally, while mean pressure is nearly the same at both points.
- **Loop.** Direct measurement of the pressures the whole cardiovascular loop is built around.
- **Anatomy.** Access through radial or femoral artery, retrograde across the aortic valve for left heart pressures. The catheter path itself is a good anatomy review.
- **High end diastolic pressure means.** The ventricle needs a higher filling pressure to reach a given volume, which is a statement about compliance, not about strength.
- **Pressure gradient across a valve means.** Flow is being forced through a smaller effective orifice, so velocity rises and pressure energy converts to kinetic energy, which is the Bernoulli relationship echocardiography uses to estimate the same thing non-invasively.

### Central venous pressure and right atrial pressure
- **Type.** Procedure, central line transduction, or estimated from jugular venous distension.
- **Measures.** Pressure in the great veins entering the right atrium.
- **Range.** 2 to 8 mmHg.
- **Loop.** Sits at the intersection of the two curves students should learn together, the cardiac function curve and the venous return curve. Their crossing point sets both cardiac output and CVP.
- **Anatomy.** The internal jugular column is continuous with the right atrium with no valve between, which is exactly why the neck can be used as a manometer.
- **High means.** Either the heart is not moving forward what it receives, or the venous compartment is full, or intrathoracic pressure is raised. It reports right sided filling pressure, which is not the same as volume.
- **Low means.** Venous filling is reduced relative to the pump's ability to clear it.

### Pulmonary artery catheter, Swan-Ganz
- **Type.** Procedure, invasive, flow-directed balloon catheter.
- **Measures.** Right atrial pressure, right ventricular pressure, pulmonary artery pressure, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, cardiac output by thermodilution, and mixed venous oxygen saturation.
- **Range.** PA systolic 15 to 30 mmHg, PA diastolic 4 to 12 mmHg. Wedge 6 to 12 mmHg. Cardiac output 4 to 8 L/min. Cardiac index 2.5 to 4.0 L/min/m2. Mixed venous saturation 60 to 80 percent.
- **Loop.** Instruments nearly every variable in the cardiovascular control loop at once, which is why it belongs in the hemodynamics program rather than as an isolated test.
- **Anatomy.** The catheter floats from a central vein through right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, pulmonic valve, and into a pulmonary artery branch. Inflating the balloon stops flow in that branch, creating a static column of blood from catheter tip through capillary to left atrium, which is why the wedge estimates left atrial pressure.
- **High wedge means.** Left sided filling pressure is elevated, so the pressure driving fluid out of pulmonary capillaries rises with it.
- **Low mixed venous saturation means.** Tissues are extracting a larger fraction of delivered oxygen, which happens when delivery falls or demand rises. It is the single best global measure of whether output is meeting need.
- **High mixed venous saturation means.** Less oxygen is being extracted than expected, so either delivery is generous or tissue cannot use it.

### Cardiac output and cardiac index
- **Type.** Calculation or measurement by thermodilution, Doppler, or Fick.
- **Measures.** Volume of blood ejected per minute, and the same value normalized to body surface area.
- **Range.** 4 to 8 L/min, index 2.5 to 4.0 L/min/m2.
- **Loop.** Output equals heart rate times stroke volume, and stroke volume is set by preload, afterload, and contractility. This is the equation the whole cardiovascular section builds toward.
- **Anatomy.** Fick method uses oxygen consumption divided by the arteriovenous oxygen difference, which ties cardiac output directly to lung and tissue physiology.
- **High means.** Either rate or stroke volume is elevated, usually because tissue demand or vasodilation is driving it.
- **Low means.** One of the three stroke volume determinants is limiting, or rate is inadequate. Working out which one is the reasoning exercise.

## 11. Blood and body defense

### Complete blood count, red cell indices
- **Type.** Lab, blood, automated.
- **Measures.** Red cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, mean corpuscular volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, and red cell distribution width.
- **Range.** Hemoglobin 13.5 to 17.5 g/dL in men, 12.0 to 15.5 in women. Hematocrit roughly three times hemoglobin. MCV 80 to 100 fL. RDW 11.5 to 14.5 percent.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the renal peritubular interstitial cell reading tissue oxygen tension through hypoxia inducible factor. Integrator is the kidney. Effector is erythropoietin acting on marrow progenitors. Regulated variable is tissue oxygen delivery, not red cell count itself.
- **Anatomy.** Made in red marrow of axial skeleton and proximal long bones in adults. The kidney is the oxygen sensor because its blood flow is high relative to its oxygen use, so its tissue oxygen tension tracks arterial content rather than flow.
- **High hemoglobin means.** Either the marrow is being driven by a real or perceived oxygen shortage, or plasma volume is down so the same cell mass is more concentrated. Viscosity rises steeply with hematocrit, which raises resistance.
- **Low hemoglobin means.** Oxygen carrying capacity is reduced. Content equals 1.34 times hemoglobin times saturation, plus a small dissolved term, so saturation can be perfect while content is poor.
- **MCV and RDW together.** Cell size points at which part of production is limited, and width tells you whether the population is uniform or mixed, meaning whether the process is old or recent.

### Reticulocyte count
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Percent or absolute count of newly released red cells still containing ribosomal RNA.
- **Range.** 0.5 to 2.5 percent, absolute 25,000 to 75,000 per microliter.
- **Loop.** The output arm of the erythropoietin loop, so it reports whether the marrow is answering the signal.
- **Anatomy.** Reticulocytes lose their RNA within about a day in circulation, so the count is a snapshot of very recent production.
- **High means.** The marrow is responding, so it has the substrate and the capacity, and the problem lies in loss or destruction rather than production.
- **Low when hemoglobin is already low means.** The marrow is not answering, so production is the limiting step.

### White blood cell count with differential
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Total leukocytes and the proportion of neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, and basophils.
- **Range.** Total 4,500 to 11,000 per microliter. Neutrophils 40 to 70 percent, lymphocytes 20 to 40 percent.
- **Loop.** Not a single loop. Cytokines including G-CSF drive production and release, while margination and demargination shift cells between the circulating pool and the vessel wall pool without changing total body count.
- **Anatomy.** Neutrophils spend hours in blood and days in tissue. About half the intravascular pool is marginated along endothelium at any moment, which is why stress and catecholamines can raise the count within minutes without any new production.
- **High neutrophils mean.** Release from marrow storage or demargination has increased, which is the innate system mobilizing.
- **High lymphocytes mean.** The adaptive compartment is expanding or redistributing.
- **High eosinophils mean.** IL-5 driven recruitment, which is the branch of the response aimed at large parasites and at allergic signaling.
- **Low neutrophils mean.** Production, release, or survival in the circulation is reduced, which lowers the first line barrier response.

### Platelet count
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Platelets per microliter.
- **Range.** 150,000 to 450,000 per microliter.
- **Loop.** Sensor and effector run through thrombopoietin, which is made constitutively by the liver and removed by binding to platelets and megakaryocytes. Fewer platelets means less removal, so more free thrombopoietin reaches the marrow. That is an elegant sensor-free feedback design worth teaching on its own.
- **Anatomy.** Megakaryocytes extend proplatelet processes through marrow sinusoid endothelium and shed fragments into flowing blood. About a third of platelets are sequestered in the spleen at any time.
- **High means.** Production is up or splenic sequestration is down. Reactive elevation follows inflammatory cytokines, especially IL-6.
- **Low means.** Production is down, destruction is up, or sequestration is increased. Primary hemostasis, meaning the initial platelet plug, is what suffers.

### Prothrombin time and INR
- **Type.** Lab, blood, citrated.
- **Measures.** Time to clot after adding tissue factor and calcium, standardized as INR.
- **Range.** PT 11 to 13.5 seconds, INR near 1.0.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It times the tissue factor pathway plus the common pathway.
- **Anatomy.** Tests factors VII, X, V, II, and fibrinogen. Factor VII has the shortest half life, about 4 to 6 hours, so this is the first test to move when hepatic synthesis or vitamin K availability changes.
- **Prolonged means.** One or more of those factors is insufficient in quantity or function. Because the liver makes almost all of them and vitamin K is required for gamma carboxylation of several, this test doubles as a functional liver synthetic measure.

### Activated partial thromboplastin time
- **Type.** Lab, blood, citrated.
- **Measures.** Time to clot after contact activation.
- **Range.** 25 to 35 seconds.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It times the contact activation pathway plus the common pathway.
- **Anatomy.** Tests factors XII, XI, IX, VIII, X, V, II, and fibrinogen. Running it alongside PT localizes which limb of the cascade is affected, which is the entire pedagogical value of having two tests.
- **Prolonged means.** A factor in that limb is reduced or inhibited.

### D-dimer
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Concentration of a specific cross-linked fibrin degradation fragment.
- **Range.** Under about 500 ng/mL FEU, with age-adjusted thresholds above 50.
- **Loop.** Reports the fibrinolytic arm, meaning plasmin activity acting on fibrin that has already been cross-linked by factor XIII.
- **Anatomy.** The fragment can only exist if fibrin was formed, cross-linked, and then broken down, so it is proof that the full sequence ran somewhere in the body.
- **High means.** Fibrin formation and breakdown are both active somewhere. It says nothing about location, which is why it is used to exclude rather than to confirm.
- **Low means.** No meaningful ongoing fibrin turnover.

### Fibrinogen
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Concentration of the soluble precursor of the fibrin mesh.
- **Range.** 200 to 400 mg/dL.
- **Loop.** Hepatic synthesis, upregulated by IL-6 as an acute phase reactant, consumed during clot formation.
- **Anatomy.** Thrombin cleaves fibrinogen into fibrin monomers, which polymerize and are then cross-linked by factor XIII into a stable mesh.
- **High means.** Acute phase synthesis is up, which also raises red cell rouleaux formation and therefore sedimentation rate.
- **Low means.** Either synthesis is down or consumption is outrunning it.

### Iron studies, serum iron, ferritin, TIBC, transferrin saturation
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Circulating iron, stored iron as ferritin, total binding capacity, and the percent of binding sites occupied.
- **Range.** Iron 60 to 170 mcg/dL. Ferritin 20 to 250 ng/mL in men, 10 to 120 in women. TIBC 240 to 450 mcg/dL. Saturation 20 to 50 percent.
- **Loop.** Sensor and effector are hepcidin, made by the liver in response to iron load and inflammation. Hepcidin degrades ferroportin, the only known iron exporter, which blocks release from enterocytes and macrophages. Regulated variable is plasma iron availability.
- **Anatomy.** There is no regulated route for iron excretion, so the body controls iron entirely at absorption and internal recycling. Macrophages recycling senescent red cells supply most daily iron, far more than diet does.
- **Low ferritin means.** Storage is depleted. Ferritin tracks total stores directly and falls before serum iron drops, before TIBC rises, and well before hemoglobin moves, so it is both the earliest and the most specific single measure of depletion.
- **High ferritin with low saturation means.** Iron exists but is locked in storage rather than released, which is what hepcidin does during inflammatory signaling.
- **High TIBC means.** The liver has upregulated transferrin, which is the response to low iron availability.

### Haptoglobin
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Concentration of the plasma protein that binds free hemoglobin.
- **Range.** 30 to 200 mg/dL.
- **Loop.** Not a set point. The liver makes it continuously, and the haptoglobin hemoglobin complex is cleared by macrophages through the CD163 receptor, so the protein is consumed whenever free hemoglobin appears.
- **Anatomy.** Free hemoglobin in plasma dissociates into alpha beta dimers of about 32 kDa, which are small enough to be filtered. Haptoglobin binds those dimers, and the complex is far too large to pass the glomerulus. So binding does two things at once, it keeps iron away from bacteria and it keeps heme pigment out of the tubule.
- **Low means.** Red cells have been lysing inside the vessels fast enough to consume the binding capacity. Once haptoglobin is saturated, free hemoglobin appears in plasma, turns it pink, and then reaches the urine.
- **Teaching pair.** Run this alongside the urine heme discordance question. Myoglobin needs no carrier and is filtered immediately, hemoglobin has a carrier and reaches urine only after that carrier is used up. Same dipstick result, two different molecular stories.

### Erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** ESR is how fast red cells settle in an hour. CRP is the concentration of a hepatic acute phase protein.
- **Range.** ESR under 20 mm/hr, rising with age. CRP under 3 mg/L.
- **Loop.** Both are downstream of IL-6 signaling to hepatocytes. Neither is a regulated variable.
- **Anatomy.** ESR is indirect, since it depends on fibrinogen and globulins reducing the negative surface charge that normally keeps red cells apart, allowing them to stack and fall faster. CRP is measured directly.
- **CRP high means.** Hepatic acute phase synthesis is running now. It rises within hours and falls within days.
- **ESR high means.** Plasma protein composition has shifted over a longer window. It moves slowly in both directions, so the two together give you a fast signal and a slow one.

### Blood typing and crossmatch, direct antiglobulin test
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** ABO and Rh surface antigens, presence of antibodies in plasma, and whether antibody is already bound to the patient's own red cells.
- **Range.** Reported as type and as positive or negative.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It is applied immunology and membrane biology.
- **Anatomy.** ABO antigens are carbohydrate structures on glycolipids and glycoproteins of the red cell membrane. Naturally occurring antibodies to the missing antigens exist without prior transfusion because similar structures appear on gut flora, which is why ABO mismatch is immediate and severe.
- **Positive antiglobulin means.** Antibody or complement is already sitting on the cell surface, which marks those cells for removal by splenic macrophages.

### Immunoglobulin levels and vaccine titers
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Total IgG, IgA, IgM, IgE, and antigen-specific antibody concentrations.
- **Range.** IgG 700 to 1600 mg/dL, IgA 70 to 400, IgM 40 to 230.
- **Loop.** Not a set point. It reflects the size and history of the plasma cell compartment and the antigens it has met.
- **Anatomy.** IgM is the first response and is a pentamer, so it is efficient at agglutination and complement fixation but stays intravascular. IgG is the memory workhorse, crosses placenta, and is the one that matters for long-term protection. IgA is dimeric with a secretory component and lives on mucosal surfaces. IgE sits on mast cells.
- **Protective titer present means.** Class switching and memory formation happened, so a second exposure will get a faster and higher response.
- **Low IgG means.** The humoral compartment is not producing or maintaining antibody, so opsonization and neutralization are weakened.

### Complement C3 and C4
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Concentration of two central complement proteins.
- **Range.** C3 80 to 180 mg/dL, C4 15 to 45 mg/dL.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. Levels reflect hepatic production minus consumption in cascade activation.
- **Anatomy.** Three activation routes converge on C3. Classical starts with antibody, lectin with mannose binding, alternative with spontaneous hydrolysis. Which protein is consumed tells you which route ran, since the classical route consumes C4 and the alternative does not.
- **Low means.** The cascade has been consuming protein faster than the liver replaces it.

### Flow cytometry, lymphocyte subsets
- **Type.** Lab, blood, fluorescent antibody labeling.
- **Measures.** Counts and proportions of cells bearing specific surface markers, for example CD4 and CD8.
- **Range.** CD4 500 to 1500 cells per microliter. CD4 to CD8 ratio roughly 1 to 4.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It counts populations defined by their surface proteins.
- **Anatomy.** CD4 marks helper T cells, which read antigen on MHC class II presented by professional antigen presenting cells. CD8 marks cytotoxic T cells, which read MHC class I present on all nucleated cells. That difference in MHC class is the reason for the whole division of labor.
- **Low CD4 means.** The helper population that licenses B cell class switching and macrophage activation is reduced, so multiple downstream arms weaken at once.

## 12. Circulation and vessels

### Systemic vascular resistance
- **Type.** Calculation from measured pressures and cardiac output.
- **Measures.** Resistance to flow across the systemic circuit. SVR equals MAP minus right atrial pressure, divided by cardiac output, times 80 for dyne units.
- **Range.** 800 to 1200 dyne times second per cm to the fifth.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the baroreceptor. Integrator is the medulla, with humoral input from angiotensin II, vasopressin, and catecholamines. Effector is arteriolar smooth muscle. Regulated variable is mean arterial pressure.
- **Anatomy.** Arterioles are the resistance vessels because they have the thickest smooth muscle relative to lumen and the steepest pressure drop across them. Poiseuille says resistance varies inversely with the fourth power of radius, so small radius changes are enormously effective.
- **High means.** Arteriolar tone is up, which raises afterload and can maintain pressure while flow falls.
- **Low means.** Arteriolar tone is down, so pressure falls unless cardiac output rises to compensate.

### Pulmonary vascular resistance
- **Type.** Calculation from pulmonary artery catheter data.
- **Measures.** Resistance across the pulmonary circuit. PVR equals mean PA pressure minus wedge, divided by cardiac output, times 80.
- **Range.** 37 to 250 dyne times second per cm to the fifth, roughly a tenth of systemic.
- **Loop.** Local rather than central. Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction is the key mechanism, and it runs opposite to systemic vessels.
- **Anatomy.** Pulmonary vessels are thin walled and highly compliant, and they recruit and distend as flow rises, which is why pressure barely changes when output triples during exercise.
- **High means.** Vessels are constricted, obstructed, or lost, so the right ventricle faces more afterload. The right ventricle is a thin walled volume pump and tolerates pressure load poorly.
- **Hypoxic response teaching point.** Low alveolar oxygen constricts local pulmonary arterioles, diverting blood away from poorly ventilated regions. Systemic arterioles dilate to low oxygen. Those opposite responses both make sense once you ask what each circuit is for.

### Ankle brachial index
- **Type.** Procedure, Doppler pressures at ankle and arm.
- **Measures.** Ratio of ankle systolic to brachial systolic pressure.
- **Range.** 1.0 to 1.4.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It compares pressure at two points along the same arterial tree.
- **Anatomy.** Pressure normally rises slightly as the pulse wave travels distally, because reflected waves summate in the stiffer peripheral arteries. So an ankle pressure lower than the arm is already abnormal.
- **Low means.** Pressure was lost between the two points, which means resistance in the intervening conduit is significant.
- **Very high means.** The vessel wall resists compression by the cuff, so the reading overstates the true intravascular pressure.

### Venous and arterial duplex ultrasound
- **Type.** Procedure, B-mode imaging plus Doppler.
- **Measures.** Vessel wall and lumen appearance, plus flow direction and velocity.
- **Range.** Descriptive plus velocity thresholds.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. Doppler shift reports velocity, and continuity says velocity rises where cross-sectional area falls.
- **Anatomy.** Venous flow is phasic with respiration and augments with distal compression, because thoracic pressure changes and the muscle pump drive it. Arterial flow is pulsatile and shaped by downstream resistance.
- **Loss of respiratory phasicity means.** Something between the probe and the thorax is damping the transmitted pressure change.
- **Absent compressibility means.** The lumen contains something that resists collapse.

### Capillary refill time
- **Type.** Bedside measurement.
- **Measures.** Seconds for color to return after blanching pressure on a nail bed.
- **Range.** Under 2 to 3 seconds.
- **Loop.** Reflects the peripheral end of the pressure-defending loop. Skin is the first bed sacrificed when the baroreflex redistributes flow.
- **Anatomy.** Cutaneous arterioles carry dense sympathetic innervation and abundant alpha receptors, which is what makes skin the earliest and cheapest thing to shut down.
- **Prolonged means.** Cutaneous perfusion is reduced, either from vasoconstriction, low driving pressure, or low temperature.

### Jugular venous pressure assessment
- **Type.** Bedside procedure.
- **Measures.** Height of the internal jugular pulsation above the sternal angle, converted to an estimated right atrial pressure.
- **Range.** Under 3 to 4 cm above the sternal angle, meaning roughly 8 to 9 cmH2O total.
- **Loop.** Same as central venous pressure.
- **Anatomy.** The sternal angle sits about 5 cm above the right atrium regardless of the patient's position, which is the anatomic constant the whole measurement rests on.
- **Elevated means.** Right atrial pressure is high, so either the right heart is not clearing its input or the venous compartment is overfilled.

### Orthostatic vital signs
- **Type.** Procedure, supine then standing pressures and rates.
- **Measures.** Change in blood pressure and heart rate with a posture change.
- **Range.** Systolic drop under 20 mmHg, diastolic under 10, heart rate rise under 20 beats per minute.
- **Loop.** Baroreflex, tested by gravity.
- **Anatomy.** Standing pools blood in dependent veins because venous compliance is roughly twenty times arterial. Venous return falls first, then stroke volume, then pressure, and only then does the reflex correct it.
- **Large drop with a large rate rise means.** The reflex is intact but circulating volume is inadequate for it to work with.
- **Large drop without a rate rise means.** The reflex arc itself is not responding.

## 13. Fluid, electrolyte, and acid base

### Serum sodium
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Sodium concentration in plasma water.
- **Range.** 135 to 145 mEq/L.
- **Loop.** Sensor is hypothalamic osmoreceptors. Integrator is the hypothalamus. Effectors are ADH and thirst, both of which move water, not sodium. Regulated variable is plasma osmolality.
- **Anatomy.** Sodium is the dominant extracellular cation and sets extracellular fluid volume, because water follows it. The collecting duct handles the water, the tubule handles the sodium, and the two are controlled separately.
- **High means.** Water is short relative to sodium. This is almost always a water problem, and the fact that thirst did not fix it says the person could not access water or could not sense the need.
- **Low means.** Water is in excess relative to sodium. Since ADH is the only thing that retains free water, low sodium usually means ADH is present when osmolality says it should not be, because a volume or non-osmotic signal is overriding it.

### Serum chloride
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Chloride concentration.
- **Range.** 98 to 107 mEq/L.
- **Loop.** Follows sodium and bicarbonate handling rather than being independently defended. Renal chloride handling is central to acid base, since the kidney trades chloride and bicarbonate.
- **Anatomy.** Reabsorbed with sodium in the proximal tubule and thick ascending limb, and exchanged for bicarbonate in the collecting duct.
- **High relative to sodium means.** Bicarbonate is proportionally lower, since the two anions balance sodium between them.
- **Low relative to sodium means.** Bicarbonate is proportionally higher, or chloride has been lost selectively from a source that is rich in it.

### Serum bicarbonate, total CO2
- **Type.** Lab, blood, part of the basic metabolic panel.
- **Measures.** Bicarbonate concentration, the metabolic arm of acid base balance.
- **Range.** 22 to 29 mEq/L.
- **Loop.** Sensor is central and peripheral chemoreceptors for the respiratory arm, and renal tubular cells sensing pH and carbon dioxide for the metabolic arm. Effector is proximal tubule bicarbonate reclamation plus distal generation of new bicarbonate through ammonium and titratable acid excretion. Regulated variable is extracellular pH.
- **Anatomy.** Nearly all filtered bicarbonate is reclaimed proximally, not by direct transport but by converting it to carbon dioxide with luminal carbonic anhydrase and rebuilding it inside the cell, which is worth teaching because it explains carbonic anhydrase inhibition.
- **High means.** The metabolic arm holds more buffer base, either because acid was lost or bicarbonate was gained or retained.
- **Low means.** Buffer base has been consumed by acid or lost directly. Whether that is consumption or loss is what the anion gap answers.

### Anion gap
- **Type.** Calculation, sodium minus the sum of chloride and bicarbonate.
- **Measures.** The concentration of anions the panel does not measure, mostly albumin plus phosphate, sulfate, and organic acids.
- **Range.** 8 to 12 mEq/L, adjusted upward by about 2.5 for every 1 g/dL that albumin falls below 4.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. Electroneutrality means total cations equal total anions, so the gap exists only because the panel measures some of each and not all.
- **Anatomy.** Albumin carries most of the normal gap, which is why the correction for albumin matters so much and gets skipped so often.
- **Wide gap with low bicarbonate means.** An unmeasured anion arrived with its acid, and the bicarbonate was consumed buffering it.
- **Normal gap with low bicarbonate means.** Bicarbonate left the body directly and chloride was retained in its place to keep electroneutrality.

### Arterial blood gas
- **Type.** Procedure plus lab, arterial puncture.
- **Measures.** pH, PaCO2, PaO2, calculated bicarbonate, and base excess.
- **Range.** pH 7.35 to 7.45. PaCO2 35 to 45 mmHg. PaO2 80 to 100 mmHg on room air. Bicarbonate 22 to 26 mEq/L. Base excess minus 2 to plus 2.
- **Loop.** Two effector systems defending one variable. The lungs adjust carbon dioxide within minutes, the kidneys adjust bicarbonate over hours to days. Henderson-Hasselbalch ties them together, since pH tracks the ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved carbon dioxide, not either one alone.
- **Anatomy.** Radial artery is standard because it is superficial, has collateral supply through the ulnar artery, and is compressible.
- **Low pH with high PaCO2 means.** Ventilation is not clearing carbon dioxide fast enough for the rate it is being made.
- **Low pH with low bicarbonate means.** Acid load or bicarbonate loss has exceeded what the kidney has replaced, and the low PaCO2 you will also see is the lung compensating.
- **High pH with low PaCO2 means.** Ventilation is exceeding metabolic carbon dioxide production.
- **Compensation teaching point.** Compensation moves pH toward normal but does not overshoot it, so the direction of the pH still tells you which arm started the problem.

### Serum phosphate
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Inorganic phosphate concentration.
- **Range.** 2.5 to 4.5 mg/dL.
- **Loop.** Sensor is bone, through FGF23 from osteocytes, plus PTH responding to calcium. Effector is the proximal tubule sodium phosphate cotransporter. Regulated variable is phosphate, which moves inversely to calcium in the PTH response.
- **Anatomy.** Most body phosphate is in bone as hydroxyapatite. Intracellular phosphate is in ATP, nucleic acids, and 2,3-BPG, so shifts between compartments move the serum value fast.
- **High means.** Renal excretion has not kept up with load or release. It binds calcium, which lowers the ionized fraction.
- **Low means.** Phosphate shifted into cells, was wasted renally, or was not absorbed. ATP generation and 2,3-BPG both suffer, and lower 2,3-BPG shifts the oxygen dissociation curve left.

### Urine electrolytes and fractional excretion of sodium
- **Type.** Lab, urine paired with serum.
- **Measures.** Urine sodium and creatinine, used to compute the fraction of filtered sodium that reaches the urine.
- **Range.** FENa under 1 percent when the kidney is conserving sodium hard, above 2 percent when it is not.
- **Loop.** The output side of the volume loop. It reports what the kidney is currently being told to do about sodium.
- **Anatomy.** Creatinine is used as the reference because it is filtered and barely reabsorbed, so it tracks how much water was removed from the filtrate.
- **Low FENa means.** Aldosterone and angiotensin are acting and the tubule is reclaiming nearly all filtered sodium, which is the appropriate response to a low effective circulating volume.
- **High FENa means.** The tubule is not reabsorbing sodium, either because it is not being told to or because it cannot.

### Urine osmolality
- **Type.** Lab, urine.
- **Measures.** Solute particle concentration in urine.
- **Range.** 50 to 1200 mOsm/kg, and the full range should be available.
- **Loop.** ADH acting on collecting duct aquaporins, working against the medullary gradient.
- **Anatomy.** The gradient is built by the loop of Henle countercurrent multiplier and preserved by the hairpin vasa recta, which acts as a countercurrent exchanger so blood flow does not wash the gradient out.
- **High means.** Water is being reclaimed, so ADH is present and the machinery is working.
- **Low means.** Water is being excreted. Compare it to serum osmolality every time, because the same urine number is appropriate or completely wrong depending on what the plasma is doing.

## 14. Respiration, including mechanical ventilation

### Spirometry
- **Type.** Procedure, forced breathing maneuver into a flow sensor.
- **Measures.** Forced vital capacity, forced expiratory volume in one second, the FEV1 to FVC ratio, and the flow volume loop shape.
- **Range.** FEV1 and FVC reported as percent of predicted for age, height, sex, and ancestry. Ratio 0.70 to 0.80 or higher in adults.
- **Loop.** Not a control loop. It tests the mechanical properties of the system, meaning airway resistance and lung and chest wall compliance.
- **Anatomy.** Most airway resistance sits in medium bronchi, not the smallest ones, because although each small airway is narrow, there are so many in parallel that their combined cross-sectional area is enormous.
- **Low ratio means.** Expiratory flow is limited relative to the volume available, so the limiting factor is resistance or loss of the elastic recoil that holds airways open during expiration.
- **Low FVC with a preserved ratio means.** The system is not filling to a normal volume in the first place, so the limiting factor is on the filling side rather than the emptying side.
- **Flow volume loop teaching point.** The expiratory limb is effort independent over most of its range because of dynamic airway compression, which is why the test is reproducible and why pushing harder does not raise the flow.

### Lung volumes by plethysmography or dilution
- **Type.** Procedure, body box or inert gas dilution.
- **Measures.** Total lung capacity, functional residual capacity, and residual volume, the volumes spirometry cannot reach.
- **Range.** TLC about 6 L, FRC about 2.4 L, RV about 1.2 L in an average adult male, all scaled to size.
- **Loop.** FRC is a mechanical equilibrium rather than a regulated variable. It is the volume where inward lung elastic recoil exactly balances outward chest wall recoil.
- **Anatomy.** At FRC the pleural pressure is about minus 5 cmH2O, which is what keeps the lung inflated against its own recoil. If air enters the pleural space and that negative pressure is lost, the lung recoils inward and collapses.
- **High residual volume means.** Air is being trapped, so the lung is not emptying to its usual endpoint.
- **Low total capacity means.** Something is limiting expansion, whether that is the lung tissue, the pleura, the chest wall, or the muscles.

### Diffusing capacity, DLCO
- **Type.** Procedure, single breath carbon monoxide uptake.
- **Measures.** How much gas crosses the alveolar capillary membrane per minute per unit pressure gradient.
- **Range.** Reported as percent of predicted, with 80 to 120 percent as the usual band.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. It reports surface area, membrane thickness, and the hemoglobin available to bind the gas, which is Fick's law of diffusion made measurable.
- **Anatomy.** Carbon monoxide is used because it binds hemoglobin so avidly that its uptake is limited by diffusion rather than by blood flow, unlike oxygen at rest which is perfusion limited.
- **Low means.** Surface area is reduced, the membrane is thicker, or there is less hemoglobin in the capillary to accept the gas. Correcting for hemoglobin separates the blood factor from the lung factor.
- **High means.** More hemoglobin is available in the alveolar capillaries, which happens with increased pulmonary blood volume or blood inside the alveolar space.

### Peak expiratory flow
- **Type.** Procedure, handheld meter.
- **Measures.** Highest flow achieved during a forced exhalation, in liters per minute.
- **Range.** Interpreted against the person's own personal best rather than a population value.
- **Loop.** Not a loop, but airway caliber is under autonomic and local mediator control, so this is a bedside readout of that tone.
- **Anatomy.** Large airway caliber dominates the peak, which is why it is sensitive to bronchial smooth muscle tone but blind to narrowing in the small airways.
- **Falling from personal best means.** Airway caliber has narrowed, so resistance rose. Because resistance varies inversely with the fourth power of radius, a small caliber change makes a large flow change.

### Arterial oxygen tension and the A-a gradient
- **Type.** Lab plus calculation.
- **Measures.** Arterial PaO2, and the difference between calculated alveolar and measured arterial oxygen tension.
- **Range.** PaO2 80 to 100 mmHg on room air. A-a gradient roughly age divided by 4, plus 4, and generally under 15 mmHg in young adults.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. The alveolar gas equation predicts alveolar oxygen from inspired oxygen, barometric pressure, water vapor, and PaCO2 divided by the respiratory quotient. The gap between prediction and measurement is where the problem lives.
- **Anatomy.** The alveolar capillary barrier is under a micron thick, built for diffusion. Normal gradient exists because of bronchial and thebesian venous drainage bypassing the alveoli.
- **Low PaO2 with a normal gradient means.** The alveolus itself has low oxygen, so either inspired oxygen is low or ventilation is inadequate.
- **Low PaO2 with a wide gradient means.** Oxygen is in the alveolus but is not reaching the arterial blood, so the problem is diffusion, ventilation-perfusion mismatch, or shunt. Whether it corrects with supplemental oxygen separates mismatch from shunt.

### Capnography, end tidal CO2
- **Type.** Procedure, continuous infrared analysis of exhaled gas.
- **Measures.** Carbon dioxide concentration through the breath cycle, reported as the end tidal value and as a waveform.
- **Range.** 35 to 45 mmHg, usually 2 to 5 mmHg below arterial PaCO2.
- **Loop.** Reports the output of the ventilation loop, and does it breath by breath rather than by intermittent sampling.
- **Anatomy.** The plateau represents alveolar gas, so the shape of the waveform reports how uniformly alveoli empty.
- **Falling end tidal with unchanged ventilation means.** Either less carbon dioxide is being delivered to the lung, meaning pulmonary blood flow dropped, or dead space rose. That is why it is the fastest bedside indicator of a sudden fall in cardiac output.
- **Rising means.** Production is up or ventilation is down.
- **Widening gap between end tidal and arterial means.** More lung is being ventilated than perfused.

### Mechanical ventilation: mode
- **Type.** Setting.
- **Measures.** Nothing directly. It defines who initiates a breath and what the ventilator guarantees.
- **Range.** Volume control, pressure control, pressure support, and the combined modes.
- **Physiology.** In volume control you set the volume and the pressure is whatever the respiratory system's compliance and resistance make it. In pressure control you set the pressure and the volume is whatever compliance and resistance allow. You get to fix one and you have to accept the other. That single trade-off is the whole conceptual core of ventilator teaching.
- **Anatomy.** Positive pressure ventilation reverses the normal pressure gradient. Spontaneous breathing lowers intrathoracic pressure and pulls venous return in, while positive pressure raises it and impedes venous return, which is why cardiac output can drop when a patient is put on a ventilator.

### Mechanical ventilation: tidal volume
- **Type.** Setting or measurement.
- **Measures.** Volume delivered per breath.
- **Range.** Commonly set at 6 to 8 mL per kilogram of predicted body weight, based on height rather than actual weight.
- **Physiology.** Alveolar ventilation equals respiratory rate times the difference between tidal volume and dead space. Because dead space is fixed, raising rate at a small tidal volume is far less efficient at clearing carbon dioxide than raising volume.
- **Anatomy.** Predicted body weight is used because lung size scales with height, not with fat mass.
- **Higher volume means.** More carbon dioxide cleared per breath, at the cost of higher alveolar distension.
- **Lower volume means.** Less alveolar stretch per breath, and carbon dioxide will rise unless rate rises to compensate.

### Mechanical ventilation: respiratory rate
- **Type.** Setting.
- **Measures.** Mandatory breaths per minute.
- **Range.** Commonly 12 to 20, higher when carbon dioxide production is high or tidal volumes are small.
- **Physiology.** Rate is the direct lever on carbon dioxide. Doubling alveolar ventilation roughly halves PaCO2, so this is where students practice the inverse relationship.
- **Anatomy.** Each breath needs time to exhale. Raising rate shortens expiratory time, so at high rates the lung may not return to its resting volume before the next breath arrives.
- **Too high means.** Incomplete exhalation, so volume and pressure accumulate breath over breath.

### Mechanical ventilation: PEEP
- **Type.** Setting.
- **Measures.** Positive pressure maintained in the airway at the end of expiration.
- **Range.** 5 cmH2O as a common starting point, titrated upward as needed.
- **Physiology.** PEEP holds alveoli open at end expiration, which raises functional residual capacity, recruits collapsed units, and improves the match between ventilation and perfusion. It works on oxygenation, not on carbon dioxide.
- **Anatomy.** Surfactant from type II pneumocytes normally lowers surface tension more in small alveoli than large ones, which stabilizes them against Laplace collapse. When surfactant function is compromised, PEEP is doing mechanically what surfactant was doing chemically.
- **Higher means.** More recruitment and better oxygenation, but also higher mean intrathoracic pressure, which lowers venous return and can lower cardiac output. Oxygen content can go up while oxygen delivery goes down.

### Mechanical ventilation: FiO2
- **Type.** Setting.
- **Measures.** Fraction of inspired oxygen, from 0.21 on room air to 1.0.
- **Range.** Titrated to the lowest value that maintains adequate saturation.
- **Physiology.** Raising FiO2 raises alveolar oxygen tension directly through the alveolar gas equation. It fixes low inspired oxygen and ventilation-perfusion mismatch well, and fixes true shunt poorly, because blood bypassing ventilated alveoli never meets the extra oxygen.
- **Anatomy.** Oxygen delivery equals cardiac output times content, and content is dominated by hemoglobin-bound oxygen, not dissolved. Once saturation is near 100 percent, more FiO2 adds only the small dissolved term.
- **Requiring high FiO2 means.** Either a large shunt fraction or severely mismatched ventilation and perfusion.

### Mechanical ventilation: inspiratory to expiratory ratio
- **Type.** Setting.
- **Measures.** How the breath cycle is divided between inspiration and expiration.
- **Range.** 1 to 2 conventionally, lengthened toward 1 to 3 or 1 to 4 when expiration needs more time.
- **Physiology.** Expiration is passive and driven by elastic recoil, so the time it needs is set by the time constant of the respiratory system, which is compliance times resistance. High resistance means a long time constant and a need for more expiratory time.
- **Anatomy.** Airways narrow during expiration as pleural pressure rises, which is exactly when a high resistance system needs the most time.
- **Too little expiratory time means.** Air stacks up breath over breath, raising end expiratory pressure above what was set and impeding venous return.

### Mechanical ventilation: peak and plateau pressures
- **Type.** Measurement from the ventilator.
- **Measures.** Peak pressure during flow delivery, and plateau pressure during an inspiratory pause when flow is zero.
- **Range.** Plateau under 30 cmH2O is the usual target. Peak is normally a few cmH2O above plateau.
- **Physiology.** With flow present, pressure has to overcome both resistance and elastance. With flow stopped, resistance drops out and the remaining pressure reflects compliance alone. Comparing the two separates an airway problem from a lung tissue problem, which is the most useful single piece of reasoning at the bedside.
- **Anatomy.** Plateau pressure is the pressure the alveoli actually see, which is why it is the number tied to alveolar overdistension.
- **High peak with normal plateau means.** Resistance rose, so the problem is in the airway or the tube.
- **High peak and high plateau together mean.** Compliance fell, so the problem is in the lung tissue, the pleural space, or the chest wall.

### Static compliance and driving pressure
- **Type.** Calculation from ventilator measurements.
- **Measures.** Compliance equals tidal volume divided by plateau minus PEEP. Driving pressure is plateau minus PEEP.
- **Range.** Compliance 50 to 100 mL per cmH2O in healthy lungs. Driving pressure under about 15 cmH2O as a general target.
- **Physiology.** Compliance is the slope of the pressure volume curve, meaning how much volume you get per unit pressure. Driving pressure is tidal volume normalized to the size of the lung that is actually participating, which makes it a better measure of stretch than tidal volume alone.
- **Anatomy.** When alveoli are collapsed or flooded, the remaining open lung is small, so a normal tidal volume overstretches whatever is still open.
- **Low compliance means.** The lung or chest wall is stiffer, so the same volume costs more pressure.
- **High driving pressure means.** The tidal volume is large relative to the aerated lung available to receive it.

### Six minute walk test
- **Type.** Procedure, timed corridor walk with saturation monitoring.
- **Measures.** Distance covered in six minutes, plus heart rate and saturation response.
- **Range.** Roughly 400 to 700 meters in healthy adults.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It is a submaximal integrated test of the whole oxygen transport chain in a functional setting.
- **Anatomy.** Exercise raises oxygen extraction and cardiac output together, so any limitation in ventilation, diffusion, circulation, or muscle shows up as either a shorter distance or a falling saturation.
- **Desaturation on walking means.** Diffusion or matching cannot keep up once transit time through the pulmonary capillary shortens, which is a limitation that resting measurements will miss entirely.

## 15. Renal and urinary

### Serum creatinine
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Concentration of the spontaneous breakdown product of muscle phosphocreatine.
- **Range.** 0.7 to 1.3 mg/dL in men, 0.6 to 1.1 in women.
- **Loop.** Not regulated at all. Production is roughly constant and proportional to muscle mass, so concentration is set almost entirely by how fast the kidney removes it.
- **Anatomy.** Freely filtered at the glomerulus, with a small amount secreted by the proximal tubule. Because production is steady, it works as a natural marker for filtration.
- **High means.** Filtration has fallen, or muscle mass and turnover are unusually high. The relationship is a rectangular hyperbola, so early filtration loss barely moves creatinine while later loss moves it steeply. That non-linearity is the most important teaching point on this test.
- **Low means.** Little muscle mass generating it, so a normal-looking value can hide substantial filtration loss in a small or frail person.

### Blood urea nitrogen
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Nitrogen carried as urea.
- **Range.** 7 to 20 mg/dL. BUN to creatinine ratio roughly 10 to 20 to 1.
- **Loop.** Not a set point. Urea is the liver's disposal form for ammonia from amino acid catabolism, filtered by the glomerulus and then variably reabsorbed depending on tubular flow rate and ADH.
- **Anatomy.** Urea is recycled into the medullary interstitium through UT-A transporters under ADH control, so it actively contributes to the concentrating gradient rather than just being waste.
- **High with a high ratio means.** Tubular flow is slow and ADH is acting, so more urea is being reabsorbed. That happens when effective circulating volume is low, which is why the ratio is read as a volume clue.
- **High with a normal ratio means.** Filtration itself has fallen, so both markers rise together.
- **Low means.** Reduced protein intake or reduced hepatic urea production.

### Estimated glomerular filtration rate and creatinine clearance
- **Type.** Calculation from serum creatinine, or measured with a timed urine collection.
- **Measures.** Volume of plasma cleared of creatinine per minute, normalized to body surface area.
- **Range.** Above 90 mL/min/1.73 m2.
- **Loop.** Filtration rate is autoregulated across a mean arterial pressure range of roughly 80 to 180 mmHg by the myogenic response of the afferent arteriole and by tubuloglomerular feedback from the macula densa.
- **Anatomy.** Filtration depends on the balance of glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure pushing out, Bowman space pressure pushing back, and plasma colloid osmotic pressure pulling in. Afferent and efferent arteriolar tone are the two independent controls, and they move filtration in opposite directions, which is worth working through carefully.
- **Low means.** Either fewer functioning nephrons or a lower filtration pressure per nephron. The estimate assumes a steady state, so it is unreliable while creatinine is actively changing.

### Urinalysis
- **Type.** Lab, urine, dipstick plus microscopy.
- **Measures.** Two separate tests reported together. The dipstick is a chemistry panel reading pH, specific gravity, protein, glucose, ketones, blood, leukocyte esterase, nitrite, urobilinogen, and bilirubin. The microscopic exam is a look at what is physically suspended in the spun sediment, meaning cells, casts, crystals, bacteria, and yeast.
- **Range.** pH 4.5 to 8.0. Specific gravity 1.005 to 1.030. No protein, glucose, ketones, blood, leukocyte esterase, or nitrite on the stick. Under 3 red cells and under 5 white cells per high power field. Occasional hyaline casts only. No bacteria in a clean specimen.
- **Loop.** Each analyte reports a different filtration or tubular function, which makes this the most physiology-dense cheap test in medicine. Almost nothing on the panel is a regulated variable in itself. Each one is a threshold being crossed somewhere along the nephron.
- **Anatomy.** The filtration barrier has three layers, fenestrated endothelium, glomerular basement membrane, and podocyte slit diaphragms, and all three are negatively charged. That charge is why albumin is held back even though it is small enough to fit through the pores. Everything downstream of the barrier is tubule, and everything downstream of the ureter is plumbing, which is the anatomical split the whole interpretation rests on.

**Dipstick findings**

- **Glucose present means.** Filtered glucose exceeded the transport maximum of the proximal SGLT2 and SGLT1 transporters, which happens near a plasma glucose of 180 to 200 mg/dL unless the transporter itself is altered or inhibited. This is the cleanest clinical demonstration of transport maximum that exists, and it is worth having students sketch the titration curve with its splay.
- **Protein present means.** Either the charge or size barrier is leaking, or the proximal tubule's capacity to reclaim normally filtered small proteins has been exceeded. The pad is far more sensitive to albumin than to other proteins, so a light chain or a small globulin can flood the urine and leave the stick nearly blank. That is a detection chemistry limitation, not a physiologic one.
- **Ketones present mean.** Fatty acid oxidation is outrunning carbohydrate availability, so the liver is exporting acetoacetate and beta hydroxybutyrate as an alternative fuel. The pad detects acetoacetate well and beta hydroxybutyrate poorly, and beta hydroxybutyrate is the dominant species when the shift is severe, so the stick can understate exactly the situation it is meant to catch.
- **Nitrite present means.** Bacteria capable of reducing dietary nitrate to nitrite have been sitting in bladder urine long enough for the reaction to run. Human cells do not do this, so the finding is bacterial by definition. It needs several hours of bladder dwell time, which is why a first morning specimen is the one to test and why frequent voiding makes a real infection read negative.
- **Leukocyte esterase present means.** Neutrophils have arrived in the urinary tract and released their granule enzyme. This detects the enzyme, not the intact cell, so it stays positive even when the cells have lysed in dilute or alkaline urine and disappeared from the microscope slide.
- **Blood, meaning heme positive.** The pad detects the peroxidase-like activity of the heme group, not the red cell. Intact red cells, free hemoglobin, and free myoglobin all trigger it identically, which is why this single pad cannot be interpreted without the microscope. See the discordance section below, because this is the most useful teaching pattern on the entire panel.
- **pH extremes mean.** The distal nephron is either excreting or conserving acid. Read it against plasma pH, since urine pH is only informative in the context of what the blood is asking for. Urine that stays alkaline while the blood is acidemic says the distal acidification machinery is not answering.
- **Specific gravity mismatch.** Three methods, three different answers, and comparing them is one of the better measurement lessons in the course. The dipstick pad reads ionic strength only, so it is blind to any non-ionic solute, and heavy glucosuria barely moves it while adding real osmoles. A refractometer reads density, so it over-reads heavy high molecular weight solutes and gives famously inflated values after iodinated contrast. Freezing point osmolality counts particles, which is why protein contributes almost nothing to it, since one large molecule is still just one particle. Have students predict all three for the same specimen before they measure.
- **Bilirubin and urobilinogen.** Only conjugated bilirubin is water soluble enough to be filtered, so bilirubin on the stick means the conjugation step ran and the product entered blood rather than bile. Urobilinogen comes from gut bacteria acting on bilirubin that did reach the intestine, so it reports that the enterohepatic route is open.

### Urine microscopy
- **Type.** Lab, spun sediment under light microscopy, phase contrast where available.
- **Measures.** What is physically present in the urine, counted per high power field and described by morphology.
- **Range.** Under 3 red cells and under 5 white cells per high power field. Hyaline casts occasional and normal. No other cast type, no bacteria, no dysmorphic cells.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. Microscopy answers a question chemistry cannot, which is where along the tract the material entered the stream.
- **Anatomy.** This is the anatomical localization test of nephrology. Casts form only inside a tubular lumen, so anything trapped in a cast came from at or above the tubule. Anything free floating without a cast could have joined the urine anywhere from Bowman space to the urethral meatus.
- **Casts, the general rule.** Casts are cylindrical molds of the tubular lumen, formed when Tamm-Horsfall protein, which is secreted by the thick ascending limb, gels around whatever is present. Their shape is literally a cast of the tubule. Their presence is proof of renal origin. Whatever is embedded in them names the process.
  - Hyaline casts are protein alone and are normal, especially in concentrated urine or after exercise.
  - Red cell casts mean red cells were inside a tubular lumen, which means they crossed the glomerular barrier. This is the single most specific finding in urinalysis.
  - White cell casts mean neutrophils were inside a tubular lumen, so the inflammation is in the kidney itself rather than in the bladder.
  - Granular and waxy casts are degenerating cellular material, so they say the process has been running long enough for cells inside the cast to break down.
  - Renal tubular epithelial cell casts mean tubular cells themselves sloughed into the lumen.
  - Fatty casts and oval fat bodies mean lipid crossed the barrier alongside heavy protein loss.
- **Red cell morphology.** Isomorphic red cells look like normal biconcave discs and joined the urine downstream of the glomerulus, from anywhere in the collecting system. Dysmorphic red cells, especially acanthocytes with membrane blebs, were deformed squeezing through gaps in the glomerular basement membrane and then further stressed by the osmotic gradient down the tubule. Morphology is therefore a localization tool, not just a description.
- **White cells and bacteria.** Neutrophils with bacteria and no casts point at the lower tract. Neutrophils with white cell casts point at the renal parenchyma. Squamous epithelial cells in quantity mean the specimen picked up material from the distal urethra and perineum, so the sample is contaminated and everything else on it is suspect.
- **Crystals.** Formed when a solute exceeds its solubility at the urine's pH, temperature, and concentration. Which crystal appears depends on pH, so uric acid forms in acidic urine and calcium phosphate and struvite in alkaline urine. Struvite specifically requires urease-producing bacteria to raise the pH, so a struvite crystal is telling you about bacterial metabolism, not just chemistry.

### Reading the dipstick against the microscope
This is the highest-yield reasoning exercise in the renal unit, and it works because the two methods detect different things. The dipstick reads chemistry. The microscope reads structure. When they disagree, the disagreement is the finding.

**Heme positive on the stick, red cells present on microscopy**
Intact red cells are in the urine. The next question is where they entered, and morphology plus casts answer it. Isomorphic cells with no casts means somewhere in the collecting system, ureter, bladder, or urethra. Dysmorphic cells with red cell casts and protein means they crossed the glomerular filtration barrier.

**Heme positive on the stick, no red cells on microscopy**
The pad is detecting free heme protein in solution rather than cells. Two sources produce this.

- Myoglobin, released when skeletal muscle cell membranes fail on a large scale. Myoglobin is a small monomer of about 17 kDa, it is not bound to a carrier protein, and it is freely filtered, so it reaches the urine fast and in quantity. The plasma stays clear because myoglobin leaves the circulation quickly. Look for a very high creatine kinase alongside it, plus potassium and phosphate rising as they leave the same broken cells and calcium falling as it binds to the phosphate released. That whole cluster is one mechanism told from four different samples, which is exactly the kind of integration worth asking students to assemble themselves.
- Free hemoglobin, released when red cells lyse inside the vessels. Hemoglobin is a 64 kDa tetramer and it is bound by haptoglobin first, so it only reaches the urine once haptoglobin is saturated. The plasma looks pink, and haptoglobin falls. The size difference between the two proteins is what produces every downstream difference in how they behave.

The teaching point is that a chemistry pad detects a chemical group, and the same chemical group can arrive in three completely different physical forms. Students who understand that will never again read a dipstick as if it were a cell count.

**Heme negative on the stick, red cells reported**
The usual cause is ascorbic acid in the urine, since ascorbate is a reducing agent and blocks the peroxidase-driven color reaction on the pad. High specific gravity does it too, because concentrated urine crenates the cells and blunts the reaction. Contamination of the specimen after the stick was read will also produce it. Worth teaching, so students learn to distrust a result rather than talk themselves into reconciling it.

**Protein negative on the stick, heavy protein by other measures**
The pad is albumin weighted, so non-albumin proteins slip past it. A sulfosalicylic acid test precipitates total protein and catches what the stick missed. This is a good example of choosing a method to match the question.

**Nitrite negative with clear signs of infection**
Either the organism does not reduce nitrate, or the urine did not sit in the bladder long enough for the reaction to complete. Absence of a chemical reaction product is not absence of bacteria.

**Glucose present with a normal plasma glucose**
Filtration and plasma concentration are fine, so the transport maximum was lowered rather than the load raised. This separates a delivery problem from a transporter problem, which is the whole point of teaching transport maximum in the first place.

**Three patterns to have students build from mechanism**
Give them the sediment and the stick and let them reason to the compartment, not the diagnosis.

| Pattern | Stick | Microscopy | Where the material entered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lower tract infection | Nitrite positive, leukocyte esterase positive | Neutrophils, bacteria, no casts | Bladder or urethra, below the kidney |
| Glomerular barrier breach | Heme positive, protein positive | Dysmorphic red cells, red cell casts | Across the glomerular filtration barrier |
| Muscle breakdown | Heme positive, protein often trace | No red cells, sometimes pigmented granular casts | Not the urinary tract at all, the protein came from muscle |

### Urine albumin to creatinine ratio
- **Type.** Lab, spot urine.
- **Measures.** Albumin excretion normalized to creatinine so a random sample can substitute for a timed collection.
- **Range.** Under 30 mg albumin per gram creatinine.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It is a sensitive test of the filtration barrier's charge selectivity.
- **Anatomy.** Normalizing to creatinine works because creatinine excretion per unit time is nearly constant, so it corrects for how dilute or concentrated the sample happens to be.
- **High means.** Small amounts of albumin are crossing a barrier that should be holding it back, detectable long before total protein rises.

### Cystatin C
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** Concentration of a small protein produced at a constant rate by all nucleated cells.
- **Range.** 0.5 to 1.0 mg/L.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. Freely filtered and then fully metabolized by the proximal tubule, so it never returns to the blood.
- **Anatomy.** Because production does not depend on muscle mass, it avoids the main weakness of creatinine in people at either extreme of body composition.
- **High means.** Filtration has fallen.

### Urodynamic studies
- **Type.** Procedure, pressure and flow measurement during filling and voiding.
- **Measures.** Bladder pressure during filling, detrusor pressure, urine flow rate, and post-void residual volume.
- **Range.** Peak flow above 15 mL per second, post-void residual under 50 mL.
- **Loop.** Sensor is bladder wall stretch receptors. Integrator is the pontine micturition center with cortical override. Effectors are the detrusor through parasympathetic S2 to S4, the internal sphincter through sympathetic fibers, and the external sphincter through the somatic pudendal nerve. Regulated variable is bladder volume, with voluntary override built in.
- **Anatomy.** The detrusor is smooth muscle arranged so contraction shortens the bladder in every direction at once. The external sphincter is skeletal muscle, which is precisely why voiding can be voluntarily deferred.
- **High filling pressure means.** The bladder is not accommodating volume, so compliance is reduced and pressure transmits back toward the ureters.
- **Low flow with high detrusor pressure means.** The bladder is generating force against resistance.
- **Low flow with low detrusor pressure means.** The pump itself is weak rather than obstructed.

### Renal ultrasound
- **Type.** Procedure, imaging.
- **Measures.** Kidney size, cortical thickness, corticomedullary differentiation, and whether the collecting system is dilated.
- **Range.** Kidney length 9 to 13 cm in adults.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It answers structural questions that change how you interpret the functional numbers.
- **Anatomy.** Cortex holds the glomeruli and proximal and distal convoluted tubules. Medulla holds the loops and collecting ducts, so cortical thinning and medullary changes point at different parts of the nephron.
- **Dilated collecting system means.** Urine is not leaving freely, so pressure is transmitting back toward Bowman space, which lowers net filtration pressure.

## 16. Cross-cutting tests

These do not belong to one system. They come up in every unit, and each one is worth teaching once as a physical principle rather than repeatedly as a test.

### Radiography, plain X-ray
- **Type.** Procedure, imaging.
- **Measures.** Differential absorption of X-ray photons, which tracks tissue density and atomic number.
- **Physiology and anatomy.** Five densities in order, air, fat, soft tissue and water, bone, and metal. Everything you can read on a plain film comes from an interface between two of those. Air next to water is why a chest film works at all.

### Computed tomography
- **Type.** Procedure, imaging.
- **Measures.** The same X-ray absorption, reconstructed into cross sections and reported in Hounsfield units, with water at 0 and air at minus 1000.
- **Physiology and anatomy.** Cross sectional reconstruction removes the superimposition problem of plain film. Contrast follows blood flow, so timing the scan after injection turns a structural test into a perfusion test.

### Magnetic resonance imaging
- **Type.** Procedure, imaging.
- **Measures.** Behavior of hydrogen nuclei in a magnetic field after a radiofrequency pulse, mostly the hydrogen in water and fat.
- **Physiology and anatomy.** Contrast comes from how quickly protons in different tissues return to alignment, so it distinguishes soft tissues that look identical on CT. Functional MRI takes this further by reading the magnetic difference between oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin, which makes local blood flow a proxy for neural activity.

### Ultrasound
- **Type.** Procedure, imaging.
- **Measures.** Reflection of high frequency sound at interfaces between tissues of different acoustic impedance, plus Doppler frequency shift for motion.
- **Physiology and anatomy.** Sound will not cross air or bone, which is why the windows are where they are. Doppler shift is proportional to velocity and to the cosine of the insonation angle, so the angle has to be right or the number is wrong.

### Nuclear medicine and PET
- **Type.** Procedure, imaging after radiotracer administration.
- **Measures.** Where a labeled molecule accumulates, which reports function rather than structure.
- **Physiology and anatomy.** FDG is a glucose analog that enters cells through the same transporters but cannot be fully metabolized, so it accumulates in proportion to glucose uptake. Brain, heart, and any tissue with high glycolytic rate light up. It is a physiology image, not an anatomy image.

### Dual energy X-ray absorptiometry, DXA
- **Type.** Procedure, imaging.
- **Measures.** Bone mineral density in grams per square centimeter, reported as T and Z scores.
- **Range.** T score above minus 1.0 is the reference band.
- **Loop.** Sensor is the osteocyte network reading mechanical strain, plus PTH and calcitriol responding to calcium. Effectors are osteoblasts and osteoclasts, coupled through RANK, RANKL, and osteoprotegerin. Regulated variable is bone mass, defended against mechanical demand and calcium need at the same time.
- **Anatomy.** Trabecular bone in vertebrae and femoral neck turns over faster than cortical bone, so it responds first to any change in the balance.
- **Low means.** Resorption has been outrunning formation, so mineral has been withdrawn from the reservoir. Note that the same mechanism serves calcium homeostasis, which means the skeleton is regularly sacrificed to defend plasma calcium.

### Vitamin D, 25-hydroxyvitamin D
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** The storage form of vitamin D, which reflects total availability.
- **Range.** 30 to 60 ng/mL is the commonly cited sufficient band.
- **Loop.** Skin makes cholecalciferol from 7-dehydrocholesterol under UVB. The liver adds the 25 hydroxyl group without much regulation, so that form reflects supply. The kidney adds the 1 hydroxyl group under PTH control, so calcitriol reflects demand.
- **Anatomy.** Calcitriol acts on intestinal enterocytes to raise calcium and phosphate absorption, which is the only route by which new calcium enters the body.
- **Low means.** Supply from skin and diet has been inadequate, so intestinal calcium absorption falls and PTH rises to hold plasma calcium by pulling from bone instead.

### Serum uric acid
- **Type.** Lab, blood.
- **Measures.** The end product of purine metabolism in humans.
- **Range.** 3.4 to 7.0 mg/dL in men, 2.4 to 6.0 in women.
- **Loop.** Not regulated. Balance of purine turnover against renal excretion, which involves both secretion and reabsorption in the proximal tubule.
- **Anatomy.** Most mammals convert uric acid further to allantoin. Humans lost that enzyme, so uric acid is the endpoint and it is near its solubility limit at body temperature, which is why cooler peripheral joints are where it precipitates.
- **High means.** Production exceeds excretion. Renal handling shares transporters with organic anions, so volume status and competing anions both shift it.

### Audiometry
- **Type.** Procedure, tones at graded frequency and intensity.
- **Measures.** Hearing threshold in decibels across frequencies, by air conduction and bone conduction.
- **Range.** Thresholds within 25 dB of the reference across speech frequencies.
- **Loop.** Not a loop, though the acoustic reflex and outer hair cell electromotility both provide gain control.
- **Anatomy.** Air conduction tests the whole chain, ear canal through middle ear ossicles through cochlea through nerve. Bone conduction bypasses the outer and middle ear and goes straight to the cochlea. Comparing them separates a conduction problem from a sensorineural one, which is a clean anatomical dissection done with sound.
- **Air worse than bone means.** Sound energy is being lost before it reaches the cochlea.
- **Both equally reduced means.** The cochlea or the nerve is the limiting element.

### Tonometry
- **Type.** Procedure, applanation or non-contact.
- **Measures.** Intraocular pressure.
- **Range.** 10 to 21 mmHg.
- **Loop.** Aqueous humor is secreted by the ciliary epithelium at a fairly constant rate and drains through the trabecular meshwork into Schlemm canal. Pressure is set by the balance of production and outflow resistance.
- **Anatomy.** The eye is a closed fluid-filled sphere, so its pressure behaves like any other closed compartment. The optic nerve head is the structure that pays when pressure exceeds what perfusion can overcome.
- **High means.** Outflow resistance has risen or production exceeds drainage, so perfusion pressure at the nerve head falls.

### Allergy skin prick testing
- **Type.** Procedure, intradermal or epicutaneous antigen with a wheal and flare read.
- **Measures.** Wheal diameter at 15 to 20 minutes compared with positive and negative controls.
- **Range.** A wheal 3 mm or more larger than the negative control counts as a response.
- **Loop.** Not a loop. It is a live demonstration of an immediate hypersensitivity response.
- **Anatomy.** Antigen cross-links IgE already bound to dermal mast cells, which degranulate and release histamine. Histamine causes local arteriolar dilation, which is the flare, and increased venular permeability, which is the wheal. Both parts of the response are visible microcirculatory physiology happening on the skin in real time.
- **Positive means.** Specific IgE is present and bound to mast cells in that person's skin.

## Calculations students work by hand in this lab

Pulled out here so they are easy to find and assign. Each one is derived, not looked up.

| Calculation | Formula | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| Mean arterial pressure | Diastolic plus one third of pulse pressure | Why diastole dominates the average |
| Pulse pressure | Systolic minus diastolic | Stroke volume against arterial compliance |
| Cardiac output | Heart rate times stroke volume | The two levers, and what happens when one rises at the other's expense |
| Cardiac index | Cardiac output divided by body surface area | Why raw output cannot be compared between people |
| Fick cardiac output | Oxygen consumption divided by arteriovenous oxygen difference | Ties the heart to the lung and the tissue |
| Systemic vascular resistance | MAP minus right atrial pressure, over cardiac output, times 80 | Pressure, flow, and resistance as one relationship |
| Pulmonary vascular resistance | Mean PA pressure minus wedge, over cardiac output, times 80 | Why the right side runs at a tenth the pressure |
| Oxygen content | 1.34 times hemoglobin times saturation, plus dissolved | Why a perfect saturation can still mean poor delivery |
| Oxygen delivery | Cardiac output times oxygen content | Content and flow are two separate problems |
| Alveolar gas equation | Inspired oxygen tension minus PaCO2 over the respiratory quotient | Predicts alveolar oxygen from things you can measure |
| A-a gradient | Calculated alveolar minus measured arterial oxygen | Localizes the problem to the alveolus or past it |
| Alveolar ventilation | Rate times tidal volume minus dead space | Why rapid shallow breathing moves less air than it looks like |
| Static compliance | Tidal volume over plateau minus PEEP | Stiffness separated from resistance |
| Driving pressure | Plateau minus PEEP | Stretch normalized to the lung that is actually open |
| Anion gap | Sodium minus chloride plus bicarbonate, corrected for albumin | Electroneutrality made visible |
| Serum osmolality estimate | 2 times sodium, plus glucose over 18, plus BUN over 2.8 | What actually holds water where |
| Corrected calcium | Total calcium plus 0.8 times 4 minus albumin | Bound versus free, and why the active fraction is the one that matters |
| Estimated GFR and creatinine clearance | Urine creatinine times volume, over plasma creatinine and time | Clearance as a volume of plasma, not a mass |
| Fractional excretion of sodium | Urine and plasma sodium and creatinine, as a percent | What the tubule is being told to do right now |
| Corrected reticulocyte count | Reticulocyte percent times hematocrit over 45 | Correcting a proportion for the size of its denominator |

## What this list deliberately leaves out

- Diagnoses. Every high and low line stops at what the body is doing.
- Disease-specific test panels, tumor markers, genetic panels, and drug levels. Those are real, but they teach classification rather than mechanism.
- Reference ranges as things to memorize. They are here so students can see where the regulated variable sits, and so they can reason about direction and magnitude. The interactive version will show them on request rather than making them a memorization target.

## Where this goes next: the interactive build

The plan is one self-contained page per body system, or one page with system tabs, built the way your other tools are built.

How it works for a student:

1. Pick a body system, then pick a test from the card grid.
2. The card opens with the test name and its type only. Nothing else is visible yet.
3. The student writes what they think is being measured, in physiology terms, into a text field. Nothing is graded and nothing leaves the browser.
4. They reveal the answer one layer at a time. Measures first, then the control loop, then anatomy, then high and low.
5. A "build the loop" step asks them to place sensor, integrator, effector, and regulated variable before the loop is shown.
6. A chart mode hands them a curve, waveform, or panel with no label and walks the four questions: axes and units, shape, what produces the shape, what would move it.
7. A calculation mode gives them the inputs and makes them work the formula, then shows what happens to the answer when one input is changed. Seeded values so no two students get the same numbers.
8. A mixed review mode pulls random tests across systems, since real charts do not arrive sorted by chapter.

That structure keeps the retrieval effort on the student instead of letting them read the card and feel like they knew it. It also drills all four lab skills in one place, since measuring shows up as what the method detects, calculating and chart reading get their own modes, and data collection shows up as the conditions attached to every value. It also feeds directly into the patient file term project, since a weekly control loop drawing is exactly what step 5 rehearses.
