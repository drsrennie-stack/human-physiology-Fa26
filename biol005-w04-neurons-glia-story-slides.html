01

The baseline story

Neurons and glia, told as a story

We take the resting cell we built in Story 1 and turn it into a cell that can carry a message. We start with a plain problem of distance: your foot is about a meter from your spinal cord, and our cell can only affect what is right beside it.

  • Each part adds one new piece of information and then asks you a question you can already answer.
  • You draw along on your own sheet: the arrows, the doors and the labels.
  • A scientific name arrives only after you understand the idea it belongs to. There are no numbers today.

What we are building

One cell stretches until it can reach a meter. It puts the right door in each of its regions, gets a support crew and a layer of insulation, and runs a supply line to its far end. By the end you can say what every part is for.

02

Part 1

A message has a long way to go

Here is the resting cell we built in Story 1. Put it in your spinal cord. It needs to tell a muscle in your foot to contract, and that muscle is about a meter away. So far our cell can only affect what is right beside it.

Ask

How could one cell carry a message that far?

Answer

The cell reaches the whole way itself. It grows one long, thin extension of its own membrane and cytoplasm, all the way to the muscle. Then the message can travel the whole distance inside one cell.

It is still one cell, with one nucleus. A cell built to carry messages like this gets its own name.

Target far away → the cell grows one long extension → the message travels inside the cell.

Now name it: neuron

03

Part 2

One cell, four jobs

Here is our cell after it has stretched. It also grew short branches around the cell body. Follow one message through it. The message has to come IN somewhere. The cell has to DECIDE whether to send it on. It has to TRAVEL the distance. And it has to be HANDED ON to the next cell.

Ask

Four jobs, and four numbered places on the cell. Which place does which job?

Answer

Place 1, the branches and the cell body, is where messages come in. Place 4 sits beside the next cell, so that is where the message is handed on. Place 3, the long extension, carries it.

That leaves place 2, where the long extension begins. Everything that came in arrives there, so that is where the cell decides whether to send the message on.

The message goes one way only, from 1 to 4.

In → decide → travel → hand on.

Now name it: input zone, trigger zone, conducting zone, output zone

04

Part 3

Give the parts their names

Now the parts themselves get names. Dendrite comes from the Greek word for tree. Axon comes from the Greek word for axle, a long straight rod. A terminal is the end of a line.

Ask

Which part of our neuron is the dendrites, which is the axon, and which is the axon terminal?

Answer

The short branches look like a tree, so they are the dendrites. The long extension is the axon. The swollen far end is the axon terminal. The part that holds the nucleus keeps its plain name, the cell body.

The funnel where the cell body narrows into the axon is the axon hillock. A hillock is a small hill. The trigger zone is the axon hillock and the first stretch of the axon.

Dendrites and cell body → axon hillock → axon → axon terminal.

Now name it: dendrites, cell body, axon hillock, axon, axon terminal

05

Part 4

Back to the nucleus: a door where messages arrive

A message reaches the dendrites as a chemical. Another cell releases it, and it crosses a very narrow gap to our neuron. Story 1 ended with three kinds of doors: the leak door, the door that needs a chemical key, and the door that senses an electrical change.

Ask

Which of the three doors belongs in the membrane of the dendrites and the cell body, and why?

Answer

The ligand gated door, because the message arrives as a chemical. The chemical is the key. It binds to the binding spot, the door changes, and ions move through. Now the message is inside our neuron, as a small electrical change.

Back to the nucleus: gene, protein, membrane. The neuron builds ligand gated channels and places them in the input zone.

In a sensory neuron the input doors are opened by a physical stimulus, such as touch. The full lecture covers those.

Chemical arrives → key binds → door changes → a small electrical change in the input zone.

06

Part 5

Back to the nucleus: doors along the axon

Inside the neuron the message is electrical: a change in the charge across the membrane. It has to travel the whole axon, and there are no chemical keys inside an axon.

Ask

Which kind of door can respond to an electrical change? Where along the neuron would you put it?

Answer

The voltage gated door. Back to the nucleus: the neuron builds voltage gated channels for Na+ and for K+ and places them all along the axon. An electrical change at one spot can then open the doors at the next spot.

They are packed most closely at the trigger zone, and that is what makes it the deciding spot. If the electrical change that arrives there is big enough, those doors open and the message is sent down the axon. If it is too small, nothing is sent.

How those doors make the message is the next story.

Electrical change → voltage gated doors open → the next spot feels the change.

Now name it: voltage gated Na+ channels and voltage gated K+ channels

07

Part 6

A new ion and a new door at the far end

At the axon terminal there is a gap between our neuron and the next cell, and the electrical change cannot cross it. The message has to become a chemical again. This part has a new ion: calcium, Ca2+. There is lots of Ca2+ outside and almost none inside. Back to the nucleus: the neuron builds a voltage gated door for calcium and places it in the terminal.

Ask

The electrical message arrives and this door opens. Which way does calcium move? You can answer from Story 1.

Answer

In. Lots outside and almost none inside, so the concentration gradient pushes Ca2+ in. Calcium is positive and the inside is negative, so the electrical pull is also in. Two reasons to enter, the same as sodium.

The Ca2+ that enters is the signal to the packets. They release their chemical key into the gap, and the message is handed on. The packets are called synaptic vesicles, and the chemical key now gets its real name.

Message arrives → Ca2+ doors open → Ca2+ moves IN → chemical key released.

Now name it: voltage gated Ca2+ channel, neurotransmitter

08

Part 7

Three neurons, one reflex

You step on a tack, and your leg pulls back before you have thought about it. Three neurons in a row did that. Your brain and spinal cord together are called the central nervous system, the CNS.

Ask

Follow the message from the tack to the muscle. Compared with the spinal cord, which way does each neuron carry it?

Answer

Neuron 1 carries it TOWARD the spinal cord. Neuron 2 lies entirely INSIDE the spinal cord and passes the message from one neuron to another. Neuron 3 carries it AWAY from the spinal cord, to the muscle.

Afferent means carrying toward the CNS. Efferent means carrying away from it. Most of the neurons you own are interneurons.

Toward the CNS → inside the CNS → away from the CNS.

Now name it: sensory (afferent) neuron, interneuron, motor (efferent) neuron

09

Part 8

Sort neurons by shape: count the extensions

Neurons also come in different shapes. To sort them by shape you do one thing: find the cell body and count the extensions that leave it.

Ask

How many extensions leave the cell body of each of these four neurons?

Answer

Neuron 1: many. Neuron 2: two. Neuron 3: one, which then splits in two. Neuron 4: many, but none of them is a long axon.

Look back at the reflex. The sensory neuron had its cell body off to the side, which is shape 3. The interneuron and the motor neuron were shape 1, and shape 1 is by far the most common.

Find the cell body → count what leaves it.

Now name it: multipolar, bipolar, pseudounipolar, anaxonic

10

Part 9

A busy cell needs a support crew

Every message lets a little K+ out of the neuron into the very narrow space around it. Used chemical keys are left in that space as well. The neuron also needs fuel from the blood, and debris has to be cleared away.

Ask

From Story 1: what would happen to our resting cell if K+ piled up just outside it?

Answer

With more K+ outside, the push on K+ to leave gets weaker. Less positive charge leaves, so the inside becomes less negative. The resting cell is disturbed, and the neuron could send messages that nothing asked for. Somebody has to mop up that K+.

Cell 1, astrocytes, mop up K+ and used chemical keys, and pass fuel from the blood to neurons. Cell 2, microglia, clean up debris and microbes. Cell 3, ependymal cells, line the fluid filled spaces and move the fluid.

K+ builds up outside → the resting cell is disturbed → astrocytes mop it up.

Now name it: glia: astrocytes, microglia, ependymal cells

11

Part 10

A hose with holes

The axon membrane is full of leak doors, the ones you built in Story 1. As the electrical change spreads along the inside of the axon, charge leaks out through them. So the change reaches only a short way, and the voltage gated doors have to make the message again at every short step. Each time takes time.

Ask

Turn on the tap. How far along the hose does a strong flow reach? Without touching the tap, how would you make it reach farther?

Answer

Not far, because water leaks out at every hole. Wrap the hose so the holes are covered. The axon gets the same fix: wrap it in insulation, and the electrical change reaches much farther before it has to be made again. Fewer stops, so the message arrives sooner.

Here is where the comparison stops. Water in a hose is never made again. In an axon the wrapping has short gaps, and at each gap voltage gated doors make the message again, full size. The neuron keeps its voltage gated doors packed at those gaps.

Bare axon leaks → many short steps → slow. Wrapped axon → long steps from gap to gap → fast.

Now name it: myelin, nodes of Ranvier

12

Part 11

Who does the wrapping

Myelin is made by support cells. A cell winds its own membrane around the axon again and again. Membrane is mostly fat, which scientists call lipid, and ions cannot cross it. So many layers of membrane make good insulation. Two different cells do this, in two different places.

Ask

Cell 1 wraps pieces of several axons. Cell 2 wraps one piece of one axon. If one cell of each kind dies, which loss affects more axons?

Answer

Losing cell 1. Every axon it served loses a piece of its wrapping. Losing one cell 2 strips one piece from one axon.

Cell 1 works in the brain and spinal cord, the CNS. Cell 2 works in the nerves, which are the peripheral nervous system, the PNS. The PNS has one more support cell, cell 3, which surrounds neuron cell bodies and looks after them the way astrocytes do in the CNS.

That makes six kinds of glia: four in the CNS and two in the PNS.

CNS: one cell wraps many axons. PNS: one cell wraps one piece of one axon.

Now name it: oligodendrocytes (CNS), Schwann cells and satellite cells (PNS)

13

Part 12

Strip the insulation

Suppose the myelin is stripped from one stretch of a myelinated axon. Look at what is left there: bare membrane with its leak doors, and very few voltage gated doors, because the neuron had packed those at the gaps.

Ask

The message reaches the stripped stretch. What happens to it? Use the hose.

Answer

It leaks away. The electrical change fades across the bare stretch, and there are too few voltage gated doors there to make it again. If enough is left to open the doors at the next gap, the message arrives, but late. If not, it does not arrive at all.

There are two places this happens. When myelin is lost in the CNS, it is multiple sclerosis. When it is lost in the PNS, it is Guillain-Barré syndrome. The full lecture follows a patient.

Myelin lost → charge leaks out → the message is late, or lost.

Now name it: demyelination: multiple sclerosis (CNS), Guillain-Barré syndrome (PNS)

14

Part 13

The supply line

Back to the nucleus, one more time. Every door in this story came from a gene, and all the protein factories are in the cell body. The axon terminal is a meter away and has none. Proteins wear out, so the far end needs new ones all the time. If a new protein had to drift there on its own, it would take longer than a lifetime.

Ask

How do the new doors get to the far end?

Answer

They are carried. It works like deliveries on a railway. A track runs the whole length of the axon. An engine picks up a packet of new proteins and moves along the track toward the far end. It costs fuel, which is ATP.

Here is where the comparison stops. The engine has no wheels. It is a protein with two feet, and it walks along the track one step at a time. Every step uses ATP.

Gene → protein in the cell body → carried along the track → far end. Every step costs ATP.

Now name it: axonal transport: microtubule (track), kinesin (engine), anterograde (outward)

15

Part 14

The return trip

Worn out parts are not thrown away at the far end. They go back to the cell body to be broken down and reused. News from the far end travels back the same way. A second engine walks the same tracks in the opposite direction, and it also uses ATP for every step.

Ask

The return engine picks up its load at the axon terminal, which is out in the body. What else could get on it?

Answer

Anything that gets into the terminal and can attach to the engine. Some viruses do this, for example the rabies virus and herpes simplex virus. Tetanus toxin does it too.

They ride back to the cell body. That is how something that enters at a wound in the skin can reach the nervous system.

Far end → carried back along the same track → cell body. Every step costs ATP.

Now name it: dynein (return engine), retrograde (back toward the cell body)
