The whole idea in a few sentences

Read this before you record. If these sentences make sense, the rest is detail.

  1. At rest Na+ has two reasons to come in and almost no doors, so giving it doors makes the inside less negative, and with enough doors, positive.
  2. A chemical key opens ligand gated doors at the input end, a little Na+ comes in, and the inside becomes a little less negative right there. More keys make a bigger change, and the change fades as it spreads. That is a graded potential.
  3. The trigger zone is packed with voltage gated sodium doors that open when the inside becomes less negative, so Na+ coming in opens more of them. That loop is positive feedback, and it starts only if the graded potential is still big enough when it arrives, which is threshold.
  4. Each sodium door has a fast gate that opens and a slow gate that closes a moment later, so the door shuts itself and cannot reopen until the inside is negative again.
  5. Slow voltage gated potassium doors open next, K+ leaves, and the inside becomes negative again, dips a little below rest while those doors close, and returns to rest on the leak doors.
  6. The action potential is all or none because its size comes from the Na+ difference and the doors, and so few ions cross that the differences are still there afterward. The pump tidies up slowly and does not end the signal.

Part 1. Sodium is waiting outside

75 sec

Here is our cell, right where we left it. I moved the leak doors and the pump down to the bottom edge, only so the top edge is clear for today. They are all still working. And from the neuron story, this cell has an input end and a trigger zone, marked along the top.

Remember sodium. The inside is negative, and Na+ has two reasons to come in. There is lots of it outside, and the negative inside pulls on it. But it has almost no doors.

So here is my question. What would happen to the inside if we suddenly gave sodium a lot of doors?

Na+ would come in, and every one brings a positive charge with it. So the inside becomes less negative. Give it enough doors and the inside even becomes positive for a moment. That is the whole idea of today. The rest of the story is how the cell opens those doors, and how it shuts them again.

Part 2. Back to the nucleus: a door with a key

70 sec

Our cell needs a new protein, so where do we go? Back to the nucleus. Gene, protein, membrane. The cell builds a door with a key, and it puts copies at the input end. It stays shut until a chemical key fits into this little cup on top. This one lets Na+ through.

A key binds and the door opens. Which way does Na+ go, and what does that do to the inside right there?

Na+ comes in, for both of its reasons. A little positive charge comes in with it, so the inside becomes a little less negative, right there under the door.

You already know the name of this door from last time: a ligand gated channel. Ligand is just the scientific word for the key. And now the change gets a name too. Any time the inside becomes less negative, we call it a depolarization.

Part 3. More keys arrive

40 sec

One key opened one door, and we got a small change. Now a stronger signal arrives, and a stronger signal means more keys.

If more keys arrive, is the change the same size, smaller or bigger? And tell me why.

Bigger. More keys open more doors, more Na+ comes in, and the inside becomes less negative by a larger amount. So this kind of change does not have one fixed size. A small signal gives a small change, and a large signal gives a large change.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, put a key in the cup of the second door, swing its gate open, and draw a second arrow in. At the right write the chain: more keys, more doors, more Na+ in, a BIGGER change.
05

Part 4. The change spreads, and it fades

70 sec

Think about dropping a pebble in a pond. The ripple is tall where the pebble landed, and it gets smaller as it spreads. A bigger pebble makes a bigger ripple.

The positive charge that came in does not stay under the door. It spreads along the inside of the cell, in both directions. As it spreads toward the trigger zone, does it stay the same size?

No. It gets smaller with distance, and if it goes far enough it is gone.

This is where the pond comparison stops working. Nothing travels through the cell as a wave. Positive charge spreads along the inside, and on the way some of it leaks back out through the leak doors. So there is less and less of it left.

A change whose size depends on the size of the signal, and that fades as it spreads, is called a graded potential.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, just under the top membrane, draw a thick arrow pointing right from the open doors and write big here. Continue with a thinner arrow and write smaller. Finish under the trigger zone with a very thin arrow and write almost gone. Add one short arrow pointing left from the first door. Then write the name: graded potential.
06

Part 5. Back to the nucleus: a door that senses voltage

80 sec

Back to the nucleus again. Gene, protein, membrane. This is the other door from the end of the first story. The little paddle with the plus signs is a sensor. This door opens when the inside becomes less negative. It lets only Na+ through, and the cell packs lots of them into the trigger zone. I have drawn two.

I have opened the first one for you, and Na+ is coming in. Take your time with this one. What does that Na+ do to the door next to it?

The Na+ that came in makes the inside less negative there too. And less negative is exactly what the next door senses. So it opens too. More Na+ comes in, the inside becomes even less negative, and even more doors open.

You just found a loop. The result feeds its own cause, so once it starts it goes faster and faster. A loop like that is called positive feedback. And this door is a voltage gated sodium channel.

DoPoint to the inset: gene, protein, membrane. Go slowly. Ask the question and let them work out the loop out loud before you open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, redraw the second door with its paddle up and its gate open, and draw an arrow in through it. Draw a curved arrow under the membrane from the first door to the second. Then draw the loop as a circle of words in any empty space: less negative, doors open, Na+ in, even less negative. Write: positive feedback.
07

Part 6. What decides whether the loop starts?

70 sec

The loop does not start by itself. Something has to open the first few doors, and that something is our graded potential, arriving from the input end. But it has been fading the whole way here. And the K+ leak doors are open, as always, with K+ leaving and pulling the inside back toward rest.

So what decides whether the loop starts?

How big the change still is when it arrives. If it is too small, only a few sodium doors open. More K+ is leaving than Na+ is entering, so the inside goes back to rest, and all we had was a graded potential.

If it is big enough, so many sodium doors open that more Na+ enters than K+ leaves. Now the loop feeds itself, and it cannot be stopped partway.

The level the inside has to reach for the loop to start is called threshold. No value today.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, draw a dashed box around the two trigger zone doors. At the right, in navy, write the two outcomes: too small, doors stay shut, it fades. Big enough, doors open, loop starts. Under them write THRESHOLD in rust.
08

Part 7. What stops a runaway loop?

95 sec

Now we have a problem. A loop that feeds itself will not stop by itself.

So look at this sodium door up close. It has two gates. There is a fast gate, this bar. And there is a slow gate, this ball on a chain. The same change in voltage starts both of them. The fast gate opens right away. The slow gate closes a moment later.

Look at the three drawings. In drawing 1 the door is closed. In drawing 3 the door is also closed. Which of those two can be opened by a new signal?

Only drawing 1. There the fast gate is shut and the slow gate is open. The door is closed and ready. In drawing 3 the fast gate is already open, and the slow gate is plugging the door. It is shut and not ready, and nothing opens it until the inside is negative again. Then both gates go back to where they started. So each sodium door opens only for a moment, and that is what ends the entry of Na+.

Now the grown-up names. The fast gate is the activation gate. The slow gate is the inactivation gate. And a door stuck in drawing 3 is inactivated.

DoPoint to the bar and then the ball on drawing 1 before you ask. Ask the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, label the bar fast gate and the ball slow gate on drawing 1. In navy, draw an arrow in through drawing 2 and write Na+ in. In rust, put an X over drawing 3. Under the three drawings write: closed and ready, open, shut and NOT ready. Draw arrows 1 to 2 to 3, and a long arrow from 3 back to 1 labeled: only after the inside is negative again. Then add the names: activation gate, inactivation gate, inactivated.
09

Part 8. Back to the nucleus: a slow potassium door

90 sec

The sodium doors have shut themselves. But look at the charges at the trigger zone. The inside is still positive. Something has to bring it back. One more time, back to the nucleus. Gene, protein, membrane.

This door also senses voltage, and it also starts to open when the inside becomes less negative. It lets only K+ through. And it is slow.

You can answer this from the first story. When this door opens, which way does K+ go, and what does that do to the inside?

Out. There is lots of K+ inside, and right now the inside is positive, so both forces push K+ out. Positive charge leaves, and the inside becomes negative again. Going back toward negative is called repolarization. And the door is a voltage gated potassium channel.

One more question. Why does it matter that this door is slow? Suppose it were as fast as the sodium door. K+ would leave at the same moment Na+ came in. The two would cancel, and the inside would hardly change. Because it is slow, sodium goes first and potassium goes second.

DoPoint to the inset: gene, protein, membrane. Ask the first question, wait, open Show the answer, then ask the second question out loud.
DrawIn rust, draw an arrow out of the cell through the new door. Beside it write: K+ OUT, positive leaves, inside negative again. Write SLOW above the door. Then write the name: repolarization.
10

Part 9. Put it in order: one trace

95 sec

You have every piece now, so let's put them in order as one line over time. Up on this graph means the inside is more positive. The solid line is rest. The dashed line is threshold. There are no numbers.

First a few keys arrive. Then, later, many keys arrive. Draw what the inside does each time, and tell me which door is responsible for each part of your line.

A few keys: a small bump. It does not reach the dashed line, and it fades. That is a graded potential.

Many keys: the bump reaches threshold. Now up, steeply, because Na+ is coming in through the voltage gated sodium doors. It stops at the top because the slow gates are shutting and the K+ doors are opening. Then down, because K+ is going out. Then a small dip below rest, because the K+ doors are slow to close. And then back to rest. The extra K+ doors close, and the leak doors, which were open this whole time, hold the inside at rest again.

Now the names, in order. Rising phase. Peak. Falling phase. After-hyperpolarization, which only means more negative than rest, afterward. And the whole event is an action potential.

DoDraw the line yourself, slowly, before you open the answer. Say which door is working as your pen moves.
DrawIn navy, start on the rest line. After the first arrow draw a low bump that stays under the dashed line and comes back to rest. After the second arrow climb to the dashed line, then go up steeply to a peak near the top. Switch to rust at the peak: come down steeply, go a little below the rest line, and curve back up to rest. Label in navy: rising phase, Na+ in. Label in rust: falling phase, K+ out, and after-hyperpolarization. Write peak at the top and action potential across the whole event.
11

Part 10. All or none

80 sec

Think about flushing a toilet. If you push the handle a little, nothing happens. Push it past a certain point and you get a full flush. Push it really hard, and you get the same flush.

Three signals arrive at our cell: a weak one, one that is just enough, and one that is much stronger. Draw the response to each. Then tell me why, in cell terms, the stronger signal does not make a bigger action potential.

The weak one gives a graded potential and nothing more. The one that is just enough gives a full action potential. And the much stronger one gives the same action potential. Same height, same shape.

Why? The size of a flush comes from the water stored in the tank. The handle only starts it. The size of an action potential comes from the Na+ difference that the pump built, and from how the doors open and shut. The signal only has to get the trigger zone to threshold. This is called all or none.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy and rust as before: a low bump after the weak arrow and write NONE. A full action potential after the second arrow and another after the third. In rust, draw a dashed line across the two peaks and write: same height, same shape. Write ALL beside each one.
12

Part 11. Did the cell just lose its differences?

65 sec

Here the toilet comparison stops working. A flush empties the tank. And in our cell, Na+ came in and K+ went out. So it is fair to ask: did our cell just empty its tank? After one action potential, does it still have lots of Na+ outside and lots of K+ inside?

Yes, it does. Remember the first story. Only a very small number of ions has to cross to change the charge at the membrane. The differences are almost exactly what they were.

And the pump? It keeps working in the background, and over time it moves those few Na+ back out and those few K+ back in. But the pump is not what ended the action potential. The slow gates and the K+ doors did that. The pump tidies up afterward.

Write ATP beside the pump, and under it: tidies up slowly.
