Three ideas carry the whole week

Blood flows because there is a pressure difference, and it flows less when there is resistance. Resistance lives almost entirely in the arterioles, and it depends on the radius of the vessel to the fourth power, which is why a small change in arteriole diameter is the body's biggest lever. Mean arterial pressure is what the arterioles and the heart make together: cardiac output times total peripheral resistance.

Radius to the fourth power

Flow through a tube equals the pressure difference across it divided by its resistance. Resistance rises with the length of the tube and the thickness of the fluid, and falls with the radius raised to the fourth power. Length and viscosity hardly change minute to minute. Radius changes constantly, and because it is raised to the fourth power it dominates everything.

Radius × 1.00 → resistance × 1.00, flow × 1.00 at the same pressure gradient.

Four numbers from one cuff

Systolic is the peak when the ventricle ejects. Diastolic is the floor the elastic arteries hold while the ventricle refills. Pulse pressure is the difference, and it tells you about stroke volume and how stiff the big arteries are. Mean arterial pressure is the average pressure driving blood into the organs over the whole cycle; because diastole lasts longer than systole at rest, it sits closer to diastolic.

MAP is also the product of what the heart puts in and what the arterioles hold back. Every person below has a different story told by which of those two is out of place. Each one comes with a question; you decide which numbers settle it.

The one third rule is for resting heart rates. In a fast heart diastole shortens and MAP moves toward the middle.

What the cuff is doing

  1. Cuff above systolic. The brachial artery is squeezed shut for the whole cycle. No flow, no sound.
  2. Cuff between systolic and diastolic. At the top of each beat the artery pops open for a moment, blood squirts through a narrowed, deformed segment, and the flow is turbulent. Turbulent flow makes noise: the Korotkoff sounds. The first tap you hear is systolic.
  3. Cuff below diastolic. The artery stays open all cycle, flow is smooth (laminar) again, and the sound disappears. The pressure where it goes quiet is diastolic.

So the sounds are not the heartbeat. They are the sound of turbulence in a half-crushed artery, and they exist only while the cuff pressure sits inside the pulse pressure.

Standing up is an experiment on yourself

When you stand, gravity pulls roughly 500 mL of blood into the veins of the legs and belly. Less blood returns to the heart, end diastolic volume falls, stroke volume falls, and pressure dips. The carotid sinus and aortic arch feel the drop, fire less, and the medulla answers within a few seconds: sympathetic up, parasympathetic down. Heart rate rises, veins and arterioles constrict, and pressure recovers. If the reflex is slow (dehydration, some blood pressure drugs, old age) you feel lightheaded for a moment: orthostatic hypotension.

Walking fixes the venous side in a different way: every calf contraction squeezes the deep veins and the valves only let the blood go one direction, toward the heart. Standing still is the worst case: the pooling without the pump.

Predict the whole system

Each scenario changes one thing first. Follow it through: what happens to the heart, what happens to the arterioles, what the baroreflex does about it, and what that leaves mean arterial pressure doing. Choose up, down or about the same for each variable, then explain the chain. Guess first; the explanation after the check is where the learning is.

You stand up quickly

Key. Venous pooling: SV down, CO down a little, baroreflex raises HR and TPR, MAP roughly held after a few seconds. Muscle flow unchanged.

Moderate exercise, ten minutes in

Key. HR and SV both up (sympathetic contractility plus muscle-pump venous return), CO up three to four times, TPR down because active muscle arterioles dilate on local metabolites, MAP up modestly, muscle flow up many times while gut and kidney flow are trimmed by sympathetic constriction.

Losing one liter of blood, first thirty minutes

Key. Volume lost: SV and CO fall. Baroreflex: HR up, TPR up (pale cool skin, gut and kidney and muscle beds constricted) to protect brain and heart. MAP falls less than CO because TPR rose, but it still falls. Compensation fails when the loss is too fast or too large for constriction to cover: then MAP collapses, the heart itself is underperfused, and shock becomes irreversible.

Anaphylaxis, five minutes after a bee sting

Key. Histamine dilates arterioles everywhere (TPR down) and makes capillaries leak (plasma lost, SV and CO down). MAP crashes from both sides. The baroreflex drives HR up but cannot constrict arterioles that histamine is holding open. Epinephrine works because it does what the reflex cannot: alpha-1 constriction of the arterioles and beta-2 relaxation of the airway. Skin and muscle flow are up because the vessels are open, which is why the patient is flushed.
