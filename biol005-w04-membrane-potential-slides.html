The resting membrane potential

Every cell in your body has a voltage across its membrane. It is small, about 70 thousandths of a volt, and the inside of the cell is the negative side. Today we build that voltage starting from zero and add one membrane protein at a time, so you can see what each protein contributes.

  • We start with a cell that has no voltage at all. We add the sodium potassium pump, then potassium leak channels, and we read a voltmeter after each step.
  • Then we read two equations, the Nernst equation and the Goldman equation, in plain words and use them to predict which way ions move. Four reasoning problems are worked on the slides. Try each one before you open the answer.
  • Last, we change the potassium and the sodium in the blood and predict what happens to the voltage. That explains what happened to the patient.

Silverthorn chapter 5 (the resting membrane potential) and chapter 9. Week 4 competencies 1 to 5.

The problem for today

A construction worker is pinned under a collapsed wall for five hours with both legs crushed. He is awake and talking the whole time. The rescue team lifts the wall, and within minutes his heart rhythm turns chaotic and his heart stops. Nothing new injured him. The only thing that changed is that blood began flowing through his legs again. What was in those legs that could stop a heart?

03

Two terms

Resting potential and action potential are different things

  • The resting membrane potential is a steady voltage across the membrane of a cell that is not being stimulated. Almost every cell in the body has one: liver cells, fat cells, skin cells, neurons and muscle cells alike.
  • An action potential is a brief, rapid change in that voltage that travels along the membrane. Only excitable cells can produce one, which means neurons and muscle cells (skeletal, cardiac and smooth).
  • The resting potential is today's lecture. The action potential is the next one. You need today's lecture to understand it, because an action potential is a short, temporary change away from the resting potential.
Side by side
Resting potentialAction potential
Which cellsAlmost all cellsNeurons and muscle cells
Over timeSteadyRises and falls in a few milliseconds

Typical size−70 mV in a neuron, −90 mV in skeletal and heart muscleFrom rest up to about +30 mV and back

Yes to the first, no to the second

A liver cell has a resting potential, because it has the sodium potassium pump and potassium leak channels, which are covered in the next few slides. It cannot fire an action potential, because it does not have voltage gated sodium channels. Neurons and muscle cells do. A cell can have a membrane voltage without being able to change it quickly.

04

Measuring it

What −70 mV actually means

  • A membrane potential is always a comparison. By agreement the outside is called zero, so −70 mV means the inside is 70 millivolts more negative than the outside.
  • A millivolt is one thousandth of a volt. A AA battery is 1,500 mV, so a cell holds about one twentieth of that across a membrane only 8 nanometers thick.
  • The cell is not filled with negative charge. The fluid inside and the fluid outside are each electrically neutral. The only place the charges are unequal is a very thin layer on the two faces of the membrane.
  • This matters later in the lecture: a cell only has to move a very small number of ions to produce its voltage.
05

Where the proteins come from

The nucleus decides which proteins are in the membrane

  • Every pump and channel in this lecture is a protein, and every protein is built from a gene. All of your cells contain the same genes. What differs from one cell type to another is which genes are switched on.
  • All cells switch on the genes for the sodium potassium pump and for potassium leak channels, which is why almost every cell has a resting potential. Neurons and muscle cells also switch on the genes for voltage gated sodium and potassium channels, which is why only they can fire action potentials.
  • A cell can change how many copies of a channel are in its membrane by adding more or taking some out. More open channels for an ion means a higher permeability to that ion. Keep this in mind for the rest of the lecture, because permeability decides what the membrane potential will be.
  • Transcription and translation are on the review page, so the steps inside the nucleus and the ribosome are not repeated here.

Mainly where that gene is used

Mainly in the heart. Every cell has the mutated gene, but only the cells that switch that gene on build the faulty channel. One inherited form of long QT syndrome works this way: a potassium channel in heart muscle does not work normally, heart cells take longer to repolarize, and the person is at risk of dangerous rhythms. Diseases caused by a faulty ion channel gene are called channelopathies. Cystic fibrosis is another one, caused by a faulty chloride channel.

Build step 1

Start with a cell that has no voltage

  • This cell is imaginary. It has a lipid membrane and no membrane proteins. Ions cannot cross the oily middle of a bilayer, which you saw last week, so nothing moves.
  • Inside there are large negative molecules that can never leave: proteins, and phosphate groups on ATP and nucleic acids. Every one of them is paired with a positive ion, so the inside is neutral.

07

Build step 2

Add the sodium potassium pump

  • The sodium potassium pump (Na+/K+ ATPase) is in the membrane of every cell you have. Each cycle moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in, and splits one ATP to pay for it, because both ions are being moved toward the side where they are already more concentrated.
  • The pump uses a lot of energy: roughly a fifth to a third of the ATP a resting body uses, and more than that in neurons.
  • Three positive charges leave for every two that enter, so the pump does make the inside slightly negative. A pump that moves net charge like this is called electrogenic.
  • Now look at the voltmeter. Even with the pump running at full speed, the cell is only a few millivolts negative. A real neuron is at −70 mV, so the pump alone does not explain the resting potential.

Nursing

Some heart drugs act on this pump, and the blood potassium level changes how strong their effect is.

Example: Digoxin works by slowing this pump in heart muscle. Potassium and digoxin compete for the same spot on the pump, so a patient whose potassium has dropped gets a stronger digoxin effect from the same dose. That is why a potassium level is checked alongside digoxin.

Radiologic technology

Some heart scans use a tracer that the pump transports as if it were potassium.

Example: In a nuclear stress test, thallium-201 or rubidium-82 is carried into heart muscle cells by the sodium potassium pump. Living muscle with good blood flow takes it up and shows on the image. Muscle that is dead or starved of blood does not.

Medicine

You can lower a dangerous blood potassium by making the pump work faster.

Example: Insulin stimulates the pump, which pulls potassium out of the blood and into cells within minutes. It is given with glucose so the blood sugar does not fall. The potassium is still in the body, so this lowers the blood level for a few hours and does not remove the cause.

Respiratory therapy

A common breathing treatment also speeds up the pump.

Example: Albuterol stimulates the pump through beta-2 receptors. Repeated nebulizer treatments for asthma can pull blood potassium down as a side effect, and the same effect is used on purpose in a patient whose potassium is too high.

09

Build step 3

Add potassium leak channels

  • A leak channel is a protein pore with no gate. It is open all the time. This one is lined so that K+ fits through and Na+ does not. The channel does not choose a direction. It only makes movement possible.
  • Potassium is 35 times more concentrated inside, so it diffuses out.
  • Each K+ that leaves carries one positive charge out and leaves behind the negative partner it was paired with. Those large anions are too big for any channel.
  • So every potassium ion that leaves makes the inner face of the membrane a little more negative. A voltage produced this way, by an ion diffusing down its gradient, is called a diffusion potential. The voltmeter reading is now changing quickly.
10

Build step 3

Why potassium stops leaving

  • Two forces act on every potassium ion. The chemical force comes from the concentration gradient and pushes K+ out. The electrical force comes from the voltage: a negative interior attracts a positive ion and pulls K+ back in. Together they are the electrochemical gradient.
  • At the start the electrical pull is very small, so potassium leaves quickly. Each ion that leaves makes the inside more negative, and that makes it harder for the next one to leave.
  • At one particular voltage the two forces are exactly equal and opposite. Potassium ions still cross in both directions, but just as many enter as leave, so there is no net movement. The channels are still open.
  • That voltage is the equilibrium potential for potassium, written EK. It is also called the Nernst potential. With 140 mM inside and 4 mM outside it is about −95 mV.
11

A common mistake

Almost no ions had to move

Many students picture potassium leaving until the cell is nearly empty. That is not what happens.

  • Setting up −95 mV across the membrane of a typical cell takes far fewer than one potassium ion out of every thousand in the cell.
  • The concentrations in the table on slide 8 do not measurably change. Potassium is still 140 mM inside after the voltage has formed.
  • This is why the voltage can change in a thousandth of a second, which you will need for action potentials, and why a cell can do it over and over without running down.

Because the charges stay at the membrane

The charges that separate do not spread through the cell. The K+ ions that left stay pressed against the outer face of the membrane, held there by the negative charges they left behind on the inner face, which is only 8 nanometers away. Opposite charges held that close together across a thin insulator produce a large voltage from a small number of charges. It is the same way a capacitor works in a circuit.

This is the thin layer of + and − signs from Figure 1. The bulk fluid on both sides stays neutral.

Say it in one sentence

The voltage comes from a small number of ions changing sides at the membrane, and the concentrations are what decide where the voltage settles.

12

A second cell

Now let only sodium cross

  • Take a second imaginary cell. Give it the same pump, so it has the same gradients, but close the potassium channels and open channels that only Na+ fits through.
  • Sodium is concentrated outside, so the chemical force pushes it in. At the start the inside is slightly negative, so the electrical force pulls it in as well. Both forces point the same way, so sodium enters quickly.
  • Each Na+ that enters brings a positive charge with it. The inside goes from slightly negative, through zero, to positive. Once it is positive, the electrical force reverses and starts pushing Na+ back out.
  • The two forces balance at about +61 mV. That is the equilibrium potential for sodium, ENa. The same rules gave the opposite sign, because the gradient points the opposite way.
13

Putting them together

Each ion moves the membrane potential toward its own equilibrium potential

  • Whenever an ion can cross the membrane, its movement shifts the membrane potential toward that ion's equilibrium potential: −95 mV for potassium and +61 mV for sodium.
  • The more open channels an ion has, the larger its effect. The ion with the highest permeability has the most influence on the membrane potential.

It is mostly permeable to potassium

−70 mV is 25 mV away from EK and 131 mV away from ENa. The resting membrane must be far more permeable to potassium than to sodium, and it is: at rest the membrane is roughly 25 to 40 times more permeable to potassium than to sodium, because it has many more open potassium leak channels.

It is not exactly at EK, which means a small amount of another ion is also crossing. That ion is sodium, and slides 17 and 18 show how much difference it makes.

14

The equation

The Nernst equation, read in plain words

Eion = (61.5 mV ÷ z) × log10 ( [ion] outside ÷ [ion] inside )

Eion is the equilibrium potential, the inside compared with the outside, in mV.

z is the charge on the ion: +1 for K+ and Na+, −1 for Cl−, +2 for Ca2+.

61.5 mV bundles the gas constant, the Faraday constant, the conversion to log base 10 and body temperature, 37 °C (98.6 °F). Many books round it to 61 or 60.

What it says: the equation converts a concentration ratio into the voltage that would exactly cancel it.

  • You will learn to read this equation, not calculate with it. The full form is E = (RT ÷ zF) × ln(out ÷ in), and the short form above is the same equation with the constants already multiplied out for a human body.
  • Only the ratio of outside to inside matters. The steeper the gradient, the bigger the voltage needed to cancel it. Every tenfold difference is worth about 61 mV for an ion with a single charge.
  • The sign comes from the direction of the gradient and the charge together. A positive ion that is more concentrated inside needs a negative interior to hold it in, so its E is negative. A negative ion flips the sign.
  • An ion with a double charge feels the voltage twice as strongly, so it needs only half the voltage to balance the same ratio. That is the z in the denominator.

Worked problem 1

Reason out the equilibrium potential for potassium

Potassium is 4 mM outside the cell and 140 mM inside. Without calculating anything, predict whether EK is positive or negative and explain why. Then say which way potassium moves when its channels are open in a neuron resting at −70 mV, and which way that moves the membrane potential.

EK = (61.5 ÷ z) × log10 ( [K+] out ÷ [K+] in )

Read it, do not calculate with it. Reason it out with the two forces before you open the work.

Solved

  1. Start with the chemical force. Potassium is 35 times more concentrated inside, so its concentration gradient pushes it out.
  2. Ask what electrical force would balance that push. Potassium is a positive ion, so the force that holds it back has to pull it in, and a positive ion is pulled in by a negative interior. That is why EK has to be negative.
  3. The equation says the same thing in its own way. Outside over inside is 4 over 140, a ratio smaller than 1, and for an ion with a positive charge that always gives a negative answer. For these concentrations EK is about −95 mV. Books that use slightly different concentrations say −90 mV, and either value is fine.
  4. Read that number as a sentence: if potassium were the only ion that could cross, the inside would settle about 95 mV negative, and at that voltage potassium would stop moving on net.
  5. Now the direction. The neuron is at −70 mV, which is not negative enough to hold potassium in. The outward push from concentration is still larger than the inward pull from voltage, so potassium leaks out, slowly and continuously. Each potassium ion that leaves carries a positive charge out, so the membrane moves down, toward −95 mV.
  6. The rule to keep: an ion whose channels are open always moves the membrane toward its own equilibrium potential. The gap between the membrane potential and EK, here about 25 mV, is potassium's driving force, and it is small.
16

Worked problem 2

Now sodium, and then chloride

Sodium is 145 mM outside and 15 mM inside. Chloride is 110 mM outside and 10 mM inside. Predict the sign of each equilibrium potential from the direction of the gradient and the charge on the ion. Then, using ENa of about +61 mV and ECl of about −64 mV, say which way each ion moves when its channels open in a neuron resting at −70 mV, and rank potassium, sodium and chloride by the size of their driving force.

Both gradients point inward. Watch the charge on chloride. It is the step that most often goes wrong.

Solved

  1. Sodium. It is about 10 times more concentrated outside, so the chemical force pushes it in. To hold back a positive ion that is being pushed in, the inside has to repel it, which means the inside has to be positive. ENa is positive, about +61 mV.
  2. Chloride. It is also more concentrated outside, so the chemical force pushes it in as well. But chloride carries a negative charge, and a negative ion is pushed back out by a negative interior. So ECl is negative, about −64 mV, even though its gradient points the same way as sodium's. The charge flips the sign, which is what z = −1 does in the equation.
  3. Direction for sodium. The cell is at −70 mV and sodium's equilibrium potential is +61 mV. Concentration and voltage both point inward, so when sodium channels open, sodium rushes in and the membrane moves up toward +61 mV. Very little gets in at rest only because very few sodium channels are open.
  4. Direction for chloride. A cell at −70 mV is only about 6 mV more negative than ECl, so chloride is nearly balanced. Opening chloride channels moves very little of it, and the small net movement, outward at this voltage, nudges the membrane toward −64 mV.
  5. Rank the driving forces by the gap between −70 mV and each equilibrium potential: sodium about 131 mV, potassium about 25 mV, chloride about 6 mV. Sodium has by far the largest driving force, and at rest it is held back only by closed channels.
  6. What the three say together: at rest, potassium is slightly off balance, chloride is close to balanced, and sodium is far off balance. That is why opening sodium channels, the subject of the next lecture, changes the voltage so quickly.
17

The real cell

A real resting cell leaks a little sodium too

  • A real membrane has many K+ leak channels and a few Na+ leak channels. The small inward sodium leak brings positive charge in and raises the potential from −95 mV to about −70 mV.
  • At −70 mV neither ion is at its own equilibrium. Potassium keeps leaking out and sodium keeps leaking in, all the time.
  • The voltage is steady because the two leaks carry equal and opposite charge, and the pump returns the ions that leaked. This is a steady state and not an equilibrium, because it costs ATP to hold.
  • An equilibrium needs no energy to stay where it is. A steady state stays constant only while energy keeps being supplied. If the pump stops, the gradients in this cell slowly disappear.

Worked problem 3

The Goldman equation: several ions at once

Vm = 61.5 × log10 ( (PK[K+]out + PNa[Na+]out) ÷ (PK[K+]in + PNa[Na+]in) )

Vm is the membrane potential. P is the permeability of the membrane to that ion, which in practice means how many of its channels are open.

What it says: the membrane potential is a blend of the equilibrium potentials, with each ion weighted by how easily it can cross. You will read it, not calculate with it. It is also called the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz or GHK equation. Chloride can be added as a third term. It is left out here because it is already close to balanced at rest.

At rest the membrane is about 25 times more permeable to potassium than to sodium. Without calculating, predict whether Vm sits nearer EK (about −95 mV) or ENa (about +61 mV), and roughly where. Then predict Vm if PNa fell to zero, and if PNa became very large.

Solved

  1. Read the equation as a tug of war. Each ion pulls the membrane toward its own equilibrium potential, and its pull is weighted by its permeability, P, which in practice means how many of its channels are open.
  2. Potassium's weight is about 25 times sodium's, so the membrane sits close to EK and nowhere near halfway between the two. Halfway would be about −17 mV, which is nothing like a resting cell.
  3. Sodium's small weight still counts. Sodium is far from its own equilibrium potential and there is a lot of it outside, so the few open sodium channels let in enough positive charge to lift the potential about 24 mV above EK. That puts rest at about −70 mV. The equation gives about −71 mV for these values, which matches what an electrode measures in a real neuron.
  4. Check the first extreme. If PNa fell to zero, potassium would be the only ion with any weight, the equation would become the Nernst equation for potassium, and Vm would sit at −95 mV. That was cell 1.
  5. Check the other extreme. If PNa became very large, sodium's weight would swamp potassium's and Vm would head for +61 mV. That was cell 2, and the next lecture shows a neuron doing something close to it for a thousandth of a second.
  6. The sentence to keep: the resting potential is a blend of the equilibrium potentials, weighted by permeability, and it sits near EK because the resting membrane is far more permeable to potassium than to anything else.
19

The pump again

The pump contributes in two ways, one small and one large

What the sodium potassium pump contributes to the resting potential
Direct contributionIndirect contribution
HowMoves 3 positive charges out for every 2 it brings inBuilds and maintains the K+ and Na+ concentration gradients
SizeA few millivoltsAll of the rest, because without the gradients the leak channels have nothing to work with
If the pump stopsLost at onceLost slowly, over minutes to hours, as the gradients run down

The resting potential is produced mainly by potassium leaking out through leak channels. The pump makes that possible by keeping the cell full of potassium.

Two very different answers

After one second: the potential becomes a few millivolts less negative, perhaps −70 to −67 mV, and then holds. Only the pump's small direct contribution has been lost. The gradients are still there, so potassium is still leaking out and still producing nearly all of the voltage.

After several hours: sodium has been leaking in and potassium leaking out with nothing to return them. The gradients shrink, both equilibrium potentials drift toward zero, and the membrane potential follows them. With more and more sodium inside, water follows by osmosis and the cell swells. A cell with no gradients has no voltage, and a neuron with no voltage cannot signal.

It depolarizes

Permeability changed

Opening a channel raises the permeability, P, for whichever ion fits through it. In the Goldman equation that gives the ion a bigger weight, so the membrane potential moves toward that ion's equilibrium potential. Open sodium channels and the potential moves toward +61 mV. Open more potassium channels and it moves toward −95 mV. The concentrations do not need to change at all.

22

Vocabulary

Four words for a membrane potential that moves

  • A resting membrane is polarized: it has a negative side and a positive side. Depolarization is any change that makes the inside less negative than rest, so the trace moves up toward zero.
  • Overshoot is the part of a depolarization that goes past 0 mV, where the inside is briefly positive. Define it by the zero line and not by the height of the peak.
  • Repolarization is the return toward the resting potential after a depolarization.
  • Hyperpolarization is any change that makes the inside more negative than rest. A hyperpolarized cell is farther from threshold, so it is harder to excite. A depolarized cell is closer to threshold, so it is easier to excite, at least at first.

Worked problem 4

Change the blood potassium and predict

Plasma potassium is the outside potassium for every cell in the body, and inside potassium stays at 140 mM. Predict which way EK and the resting potential move if plasma potassium rises to 7 mM, and if it falls to 2.5 mM. Say whether each change moves the cell toward threshold or away from it.

Solved

  1. High, 7 mM. More potassium outside makes the gradient less steep, so less negative charge inside is needed to balance it. EK becomes less negative, moving from about −95 toward about −80 mV. The resting potential follows EK because potassium has most of the permeability, so the cell depolarizes, from about −71 to about −64 mV, and moves closer to threshold.
  2. Low, 2.5 mM. Less potassium outside makes the gradient steeper, so a more negative interior is needed to hold potassium in. EK becomes more negative, about −108 mV, and the resting potential follows it down to about −76 mV. The cell hyperpolarizes and moves farther from threshold.
  3. Why the direction surprises people: adding a positive ion outside makes the inside less negative, not more. The reason is not the charge on the added potassium. A weaker push out means less potassium leaves, so less negative charge is left behind inside.
  4. With low potassium, rest moves away from threshold. Nerve and skeletal muscle cells become harder to excite, and the patient has muscle weakness and cramps. Heart muscle responds to low potassium in a more complicated way, which we take up in the cardiac weeks.
  5. With high potassium, rest moves toward threshold, and for a short time cells fire more easily. Then a second effect becomes more important. Voltage gated sodium channels that stay at a depolarized potential for too long switch to an inactivated state and cannot reopen until the membrane repolarizes. Cells become unable to fire. In skeletal muscle that is weakness. In the heart it is slowed, disorganized conduction.

Permeability. The resting membrane is about 25 times more permeable to potassium than to sodium, so the resting potential follows EK closely and mostly ignores ENa.

Ratio. The Nernst equation works on the ratio of outside to inside. Going from 4 to 7 mM nearly doubles the outside potassium. Going from 145 to 125 mM changes sodium by only 14 percent. This answers slide 2

A change of 3 mM in potassium reaches the heart because every cell's resting potential is tied to the potassium ratio across its membrane. The same 3 mM change in sodium has almost no effect.

The patient, explained

What was in his legs

  1. Muscle is where the body keeps its potassium

    Most of the potassium in the body is inside skeletal muscle cells, at 140 mM. Five hours of crushing killed a large mass of muscle, and dead cells release their contents.

  2. The wall worked like a tourniquet

    While he was pinned, no blood flowed through his legs, so the released potassium stayed there. When the wall was lifted, blood flow returned and carried that potassium into his circulation all at once, and plasma potassium rose from 4 to 7 mM.

  3. Every cell in his body depolarized

    EK moved from −95 to −80 mV, and resting potentials followed it upward by about 7 mV. You predicted this on slide 23.

  4. His heart could no longer fire in an orderly way

    Heart cells held at a depolarized potential have many of their sodium channels held in the inactivated state. The electrical signal crosses the heart slowly and unevenly, the rhythm breaks down, and the heart stops pumping.

Treat it as an experiment

The variable that failed is plasma potassium, and through it the gap between rest and threshold. Before you open the box, name one way to fix the voltage problem quickly and one way to actually get potassium out of the body.

Three different targets

Calcium given into a vein does not change potassium at all. Raising the calcium outside the cell moves threshold to a less negative value, which restores the gap between rest and threshold. It works within minutes and protects the heart while the other steps take effect.

Insulin with glucose, and albuterol, both speed up the sodium potassium pump. Potassium moves out of the plasma and into cells, and plasma potassium falls within the hour. The potassium is still in the body.

Dialysis, or the kidneys if they are working, is the only step that removes potassium from the body. Crush injuries often damage the kidneys too, which is why dialysis is common in these patients.

The same physiology shows up whenever many cells break open at once, as in large burns, and whenever the kidneys cannot excrete potassium, as in kidney failure.

Nursing

Potassium given into a vein goes in slowly, diluted and on a pump, because a sudden rise outside the cells depolarizes the heart.

Example: Concentrated potassium chloride is never pushed straight into an IV line. A patient receiving a potassium infusion is kept on a heart monitor, and a lab value that looks too high is rechecked, because red cells that break open in the sample tube release potassium and give a falsely high result.

Radiologic technology

A patient whose kidneys have failed may arrive for imaging with a high potassium, and the first sign is on the heart monitor.

Example: A dialysis patient who missed a session comes for a CT scan. Tall, pointed T waves or a widening rhythm on the monitor are a reason to stop and call the nurse or physician before going on with the scan.

Medicine

Treatment follows the physiology in order: protect the membrane, move potassium into cells, then remove it from the body.

Example: In a patient with a potassium of 7 mM and ECG changes, calcium goes in first because it works in minutes, insulin with glucose comes next, and dialysis is arranged to remove the potassium.

Respiratory therapy

One of the drugs used to relax muscles for intubation raises blood potassium.

Example: Succinylcholine opens channels on skeletal muscle that let potassium out, which normally raises plasma potassium by about 0.5 mM. In a patient with major burns or a crush injury from days earlier the rise can be far larger, so a different drug is chosen.
