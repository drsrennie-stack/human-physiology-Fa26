The whole idea in a few sentences

Read this before you record. If these sentences make sense, the rest is detail.

  1. Each synapse gives the receiving cell only a small, brief change, and the membrane leaks, so the change fades with time and with distance, like one cup of water in a bucket with a hole.
  2. A small depolarization is an EPSP, and EPSPs reach threshold only by adding: one input firing fast (temporal summation) or many inputs at the same moment (spatial summation).
  3. Other synapses open key hole doors for K+ or Cl−. K+ leaving makes the inside more negative, and open Cl− doors hold it near rest, so both are IPSPs and both keep the cell away from threshold.
  4. The trigger zone compares only the total of all EPSPs and IPSPs with threshold. That is synaptic integration. Inputs close to the trigger zone count for more, and inhibition on the cell body acts on everything.
  5. The rules can be changed: a third neuron on one terminal lets less Ca2+ in and turns down that one input (presynaptic inhibition), and a strongly used synapse gains key hole doors and counts for more (synaptic plasticity, long term potentiation).
  6. Gap junctions join two cells directly, which is fast and works in both directions but allows very little control, and a neuron that loses its inhibition fires to ordinary input when it should not.

01

Integration at the synapse, told as a story

45 sec

This is the last story of the week. In the last story one neuron sent a chemical across a gap, and the membrane of the second cell changed a little. But a real neuron does not have one input. It has a great many, and at every moment it has one decision to make: fire, or stay silent. Today we work out how it decides.

We are going to start with a bucket that has a small hole in it. No vocabulary yet, and no numbers. Get your draw along sheet, or blank paper, and two colors.

DoShow the link to the draw along sheet. Give them a moment to get paper and two colors.
DrawNothing yet. Say your pen code out loud: navy for everything that raises the level (the cups, excitatory inputs, sodium coming in, plus signs). Rust for everything that lowers it or holds it down (the leak, the scoops, potassium leaving, chloride, inhibitory inputs).
02

Part 1. The bucket

50 sec

Here is a bucket. It has a small hole near the bottom, so the water in it sits at the height of the hole. Near the top, someone has drawn a line. And you have one small cup of water.

You tip in one cup. Does the water reach the line? And what happens to that water if you wait?

No. One cup raises the water a little, and the line is far above it. And then the extra water runs out through the hole, and the level slowly goes back down to where it started. One cup, a small rise, and it leaks away.

DoAsk both questions, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw a new water level a little above the old one and write: one cup, far below the line. In rust, draw three drops falling from the hole and an arrow from the new level back down to the hole. Write: it leaks away.
03

Part 2. Only small cups

45 sec

Now the rules. You may use small cups only. And you are not allowed to plug the hole.

How could you get the water up to the line? I want two different ways.

Way one: one person adds cup after cup, quickly, so each cup arrives before the last one has leaked away. Way two: many people each add one cup at the same moment. What does not work is one cup now and another much later, because the first has leaked away before the second arrives. I am not naming anything yet. Just keep those two ways.

DoLet them find both ways. If they only find one, ask: what if you had friends? Then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy. Over the bucket marked Way 1, draw one tipped cup with a looping arrow and write: one pourer, cup after cup, fast. Over Way 2, draw four tipped cups and write: many pourers, same moment. Draw the water up to the line in both.
04

Part 3. Turn the bucket into a neuron

115 sec

Now let's turn our bucket into a neuron. This is the receiving neuron from the last story: dendrites, cell body, nucleus, axon, and the trigger zone. Four other neurons end on it, A, B, C and D. Input B has just sent one message.

You know what happens from the last story. The chemical key fits a key hole door, positive ions come in, and the inside becomes a little more positive at that spot. And from the story before that, you know the small change fades as it spreads, and the cell fires only if the change is big enough when it reaches the trigger zone.

So, in the neuron, what is the cup? What is the leak? What is the line?

The cup is one small change from one synapse. The leak is the fading: the membrane has leak channels, so the extra positive charge leaks back out. The line is threshold at the trigger zone. And one cup did not fill the bucket, so one input does not fire this cell.

I have to be honest about where the bucket stops working. In a neuron the water is electrical charge. And a bucket has one level everywhere, but a neuron does not. The level that gets compared with the line is the level at one spot, the trigger zone.

Now the small change can have its name: an excitatory postsynaptic potential, EPSP. Excitatory, because it moves the cell toward firing. Postsynaptic, because it happens in the receiving cell.

DoAsk the three questions one at a time. Then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, write two small plus signs inside the dendrite under B and draw an arrow along the dendrite toward the cell body that gets thinner as it goes. Above B write: the cup. Beside the trigger zone write: the line. In rust, under the dendrite write: the leak, it fades. On the small bucket, draw the level up a little and write: not enough. Then write EPSP beside B.
05

Part 4. One input is not enough

45 sec

One EPSP is far too small to reach threshold. But you have already solved this problem with the bucket.

What are the two ways this neuron could reach threshold using only small EPSPs?

Way one: one input sends message after message, fast. Each EPSP arrives before the last one has faded, so each one starts from a higher level. That is temporal summation. Temporal means in time. Way two: many inputs each send one message at the same moment, and their EPSPs arrive at the trigger zone together. That is spatial summation. Spatial means in space. Real neurons use both at once.

DoPoint back to the two buckets in their notes. Ask the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy. On the axon of input C draw four short bars close together and write: one input, fast, temporal summation. On A, B and D draw one bar each and write: many inputs, same moment, spatial summation. On the small bucket draw the water up to the line, and draw an arrow along the axon: fires.
06

Part 5. Someone scoops water out

75 sec

Back to the bucket for a moment. While people add cups, someone else is scooping water out. A neuron can do that too.

Back to the nucleus. Gene, protein, membrane. The receiving cell builds a different key hole door. This one lets K+ through, and it sits under a different input. This is a close look at the membrane, and it is the same cell we have drawn all week.

From the first story: when this door opens, which way does K+ go? And what does that do to the inside?

K+ goes out, because there is lots of K+ inside and less outside. Positive charge leaves, so the inside becomes more negative. That is farther from threshold, so the cell is less likely to fire. Its name is an inhibitory postsynaptic potential, IPSP.

One honest note about the bucket. Water scooped from below the hole would stay gone. In a neuron, the level drifts back to its resting level from either direction.

DoPoint to the inset: gene, protein, membrane. Ask the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, draw an arrow out of the cell through the new door and write: K+ leaves, inside more NEGATIVE. In navy, add two extra minus signs inside the membrane beside the door. On the small bucket, in rust, draw the level below the hole and write: farther from the line. Then write IPSP.
07

Part 6. A second kind of scoop: chloride

100 sec

Back to the nucleus once more. The cell also builds a key hole door for chloride, Cl−. Chloride is the gray ring. It is negative, and there is much more of it outside than inside.

At rest, chloride's tug of war is nearly tied. Concentration pushes Cl− in, and the negative inside pushes it back out. So when this door opens at rest, the level hardly moves.

Now a cup arrives, and the inside starts to become more positive. Which way does Cl− move, and what does that do to the cup?

The inside is less negative now, so it pushes back on chloride less, and the concentration push is the stronger one. Cl− comes in. Negative charge coming in cancels the positive charge the cup brought. So open chloride doors hold the inside down near where it already is, and every cup counts for less.

In the bucket, it is like cutting a wide opening in the wall right at the resting water level. Whatever you add runs straight out. This is also an IPSP, even though the level hardly moves. In some neurons these doors make the inside a little more negative and in others a little less. Either way they hold it below threshold.

DoGo slowly here. Ask the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, draw an arrow into the cell through the chloride door and write: if the inside rises, Cl− comes IN and cancels it. On the small bucket, in rust, draw a tall opening in the wall at the water level with an arrow out of it, and write: held here.
08

Part 7. The cell adds everything

45 sec

Now stand back and look at the whole neuron again. Three cups and one scoop arrive at the same moment, at different places.

What does the trigger zone compare with the line: each input one at a time, or something else?

Only the total. The EPSPs and the IPSPs spread to the trigger zone and add together there. Cups count up, scoops count down. If the total reaches threshold, the cell fires. If it does not, the cell stays silent. A great many inputs, one answer. The name for this is synaptic integration.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, write a plus sign inside the dendrite under A, B and C. In rust, write a minus sign above the inhibitory input. At the top write: cup + cup + cup, minus scoop, equals ONE total. On the small bucket draw the level partway up, below the line, and write: not enough. Then write: integration.
09

Part 8. Where the cup comes from matters

65 sec

If you carry a cup from far away, you spill some on the way. Inputs A and C release exactly the same amount. But A is far out on the dendrite, and C is close to the cell body.

Which one counts for more at the trigger zone? And where would you put a scoop so that it has the most effect?

C counts for more. The change from A fades as it spreads, because charge leaks out through the membrane all along the dendrite, so only a little of it arrives.

And the best place for a scoop is on the cell body, right before the trigger zone. Every EPSP from every dendrite has to pass that spot, so one inhibitory input there can cancel many excitatory ones. Inhibitory inputs are often found exactly there.

DoAsk the two questions one at a time. Then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw a long arrow inside the dendrite from A to the cell body that thins to almost nothing, and a short thick arrow from C. Write: far away, most is lost. In rust, draw a new inhibitory terminal on the cell body beside the trigger zone and write: acts on everything that passes.
10

Part 9. Turn down one hose without touching the bucket

80 sec

Here is a more selective kind of control. A third neuron ends on the terminal of input A. Not on our receiving cell: on the terminal. Its chemical key fits a key hole on that terminal. And back to the nucleus: neuron A built that key hole protein and sent it down to its own terminal.

When the third neuron is active, less Ca2+ comes into terminal A when a message arrives. From the synapse story, what does less Ca2+ do to the message from A? And does the receiving cell now respond differently to B and C?

Less Ca2+ in, fewer bags empty, fewer chemical keys released, a smaller EPSP from A. And no, nothing about the receiving cell has changed, so B and C count exactly as before. Compare that with a scoop on the cell body, which acts on every input. This is presynaptic inhibition. The opposite exists too, where a third neuron makes a terminal release more. That is presynaptic facilitation.

DoPoint to the third neuron and trace where it ends. Ask the two questions one at a time, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, cross out one of the two Ca2+ diamonds beside terminal A and two of the three keys under A. Write: less Ca2+, fewer bags, fewer keys. In navy, draw a thin arrow in the dendrite under A and thick arrows under B and C. Write: only A is turned down.
11

Part 10. An input that is used a lot gets a bigger cup

70 sec

Synapses are not fixed. When one input is used strongly, again and again, the receiving cell changes that synapse so that the same message counts for more.

Think about what makes the change in the first place. What could the receiving cell put more of at this synapse?

More key hole doors. The first extra doors come from a store the cell keeps near the synapse, and keeping the change takes a trip back to the nucleus. Gene, protein, membrane. Now the same chemical message opens more doors, more positive charge comes in, and the EPSP is bigger. The cup has grown.

The name for synapses changing with use is synaptic plasticity, and this lasting increase is long term potentiation, LTP. The reverse happens too. Most neuroscientists think changes like these are part of how learning and memory work. How a particular memory is stored is still being worked out.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw two more key hole doors under the wide terminal, one on each side of the first, each with an arrow into the cell. Write: same message, more doors, BIGGER change. On the small bucket draw a higher level and write: bigger cup.
12

Part 11. A different kind of connection: a tunnel

80 sec

Everything so far used a chemical crossing a gap. Some cells are joined in a completely different way. Back to the nucleus, in both cells. Each cell builds half of a tunnel protein, and the two halves dock, so the inside of one cell opens into the inside of the next.

Positive charge has just come into cell one. What happens in cell two, and how fast? And then: what do you gain with a tunnel, and what do you give up?

The charge spreads straight through the tunnels, so cell two becomes more positive almost at once, and it usually works in either direction. You gain speed, and cells that fire together. Heart muscle is joined this way, and that is why the wall of the heart contracts as one.

What you give up is control. No scoops. No turning down one input. No bigger cups. The tunnels are gap junctions, and a connection made of them is an electrical synapse. What we used all week is a chemical synapse.

DoAsk the first question, take answers, then ask the second. Then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw an arrow from cell 1 through each tunnel into cell 2 and write plus signs in cell 2. Write: straight through, almost no delay, either direction. In gray or navy, draw an arrow from each nucleus to its own half of a tunnel and write: each cell builds half.
13

Part 12. Take away all the scoops

50 sec

One last question. A motor neuron in your spinal cord receives ordinary excitatory input all the time, from touch, from sound, from the brain. Inhibitory inputs normally keep it below threshold unless the excitation is strong.

Now suppose the inhibitory terminals cannot release their chemical at all. Nothing has been added to the excitatory side. What happens when the ordinary inputs arrive?

The IPSPs are missing from the total, so the same ordinary EPSPs now reach threshold. The motor neuron fires when it should not, and its muscle contracts when it should be quiet. That is only the direction. The full lecture has a patient in whom exactly this has happened.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, put a large X over both inhibitory terminals and write: no scoops. In navy, draw the arrows from A, B and C toward the cell body, draw the small bucket over the line, and draw an arrow along the axon: fires when it should not.
