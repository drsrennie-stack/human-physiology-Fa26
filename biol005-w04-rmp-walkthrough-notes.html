  • Values vary slightly between textbooks, but the pattern is the same.
  • Potassium is the only ion in the table that is more concentrated inside than outside. Sodium, chloride and calcium are all more concentrated outside.
  • The large anions are too big to cross the membrane. They are the negative charge left behind when potassium leaves.
  • Ions cannot cross the lipid bilayer on their own. They need channels, which are proteins built from the instructions in genes.

How the inside becomes negative

  1. The membrane has many potassium leak channels, which are always open.
  2. Potassium is more concentrated inside, so it diffuses out through them, down its concentration gradient.
  3. Each potassium ion carries a positive charge out and leaves an unpaired negative charge behind, so the inside becomes negative. The separated charge collects in a very thin layer along the membrane, and only a tiny fraction of the cell's potassium has to leave to produce it.
  4. The negative inside attracts potassium back in, so the electrical gradient on potassium acts inward. It grows as more potassium leaves.
  5. When the electrical gradient is equal and opposite to the concentration gradient, potassium moves in and out at equal rates, with no net movement. That membrane potential is potassium's equilibrium potential, about −90 mV.
  6. A few open sodium channels let a small amount of sodium in, carrying positive charge in and making the inside slightly less negative. The resting potential is about −70 mV.
  • Permeability is how easily an ion crosses the membrane, and it depends mainly on how many of that ion's channels are open. The ion with the highest permeability has the most influence on the membrane potential. At rest a neuron is roughly 25 times more permeable to potassium than to sodium.
  • If every potassium channel were blocked, potassium could no longer carry positive charge out, but sodium would keep leaking in, so the inside would become less negative. The potassium leak channels are the main reason the inside is negative at all.
Resting membrane potential
The voltage across the membrane of a cell at rest, inside relative to outside, about −70 mV in a neuron.
Concentration gradient
The difference in an ion's concentration across the membrane, also called the chemical gradient. It depends only on concentration, not on charge.
Electrical gradient
The effect of the membrane potential on a charged ion. It depends only on the ion's charge and the membrane potential, not on concentration.
Electrochemical gradient
The concentration gradient and the electrical gradient together. It determines the direction of an ion's net movement.
Equilibrium potential
The membrane potential at which the electrical gradient on an ion is equal and opposite to its concentration gradient, so the ion has no net movement.
Permeability
How easily an ion crosses the membrane, set mainly by how many of its channels are open.

  • The difference between the membrane potential and an ion's equilibrium potential is the driving force on that ion.
  • At −70 mV, potassium is 20 mV from its equilibrium potential and sodium is 130 mV from its, so the driving force on sodium is much larger.
  • The membrane potential is measured with a microelectrode inside the cell and a reference electrode outside, and it is always stated as the inside relative to the outside.
Driving force
The difference between the membrane potential and an ion's equilibrium potential.

Which way will it move?

  • When an ion's channels open, net movement of that ion drives the membrane potential toward that ion's equilibrium potential.
  • When its channels are closed, the ion cannot cross, however large its electrochemical gradient.
  • At −70 mV, if sodium channels open, sodium moves in, because its concentration gradient and the electrical gradient both act inward. The membrane depolarizes toward +60 mV.
  • At +30 mV, if potassium channels open, potassium moves out with a large driving force: 120 mV from its equilibrium potential, compared with 20 mV at rest. Potassium carries positive charge out and the membrane potential falls. This is how the membrane repolarizes during an action potential.

The Nernst and Goldman equations

  • In this course you read and interpret these equations; you do not calculate with them.
  • The Nernst equation calculates one ion's equilibrium potential from its concentrations outside and inside the cell. Potassium at 150 mM inside and 5 mM outside gives about −90 mV.
  • If outside potassium rises from 5 to 10 mM, potassium's equilibrium potential becomes less negative, about −72 mV, because the concentration gradient driving potassium out is smaller. This is why high blood potassium depolarizes cells.
  • The Goldman equation calculates the membrane potential from the concentrations of several ions and the membrane's permeability to each. It works much like a weighted average of the equilibrium potentials, with each ion weighted by its permeability.
  • Potassium's permeability is by far the largest, so the resting potential lies near −90 mV, and sodium's small permeability shifts it to about −70 mV.
  • Opening more potassium leak channels moves the membrane potential closer to −90 mV (hyperpolarization). Opening many sodium channels makes sodium permeability exceed potassium permeability, so the membrane potential rises toward +60 mV. This rapid depolarization is the rising phase of an action potential.

What the pump does and does not do

  • The sodium-potassium pump (Na+/K+ ATPase) uses energy from ATP to move ions against their concentration gradients: for each ATP it hydrolyzes, it moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in.
  • Its main job is to maintain the concentration gradients over minutes to hours, because sodium leaks in and potassium leaks out continuously.
  • Because each cycle moves 3 positive charges out and only 2 in, the pump also makes the inside slightly more negative directly, but only by a few millivolts.
  • It does not set the −70 mV resting potential from moment to moment; the leak channels do that. If the pump stops, the membrane potential changes by only a few millivolts at first. Over hours it drifts toward 0 mV as the concentration gradients run down.

Terms for voltage changes

Depolarization
The membrane potential becomes less negative than rest (moves toward zero or above).
Overshoot
The part of an action potential where the inside is positive.
Repolarization
The membrane potential returns toward its resting value, even while the inside is still positive.
Hyperpolarization
The membrane potential becomes more negative than rest.
  • These terms describe the direction of the change relative to the resting potential, not whether the inside is positive or negative.
  • Channels come in four kinds: leak channels (no gate, always open), voltage-gated, ligand-gated (chemically gated) and mechanically gated. The channel gating walkthrough covers these in detail.

Resting potential and excitability

  • A neuron fires an action potential when its membrane is depolarized to threshold, about −55 mV. The difference between the resting potential and threshold is the depolarization needed to fire, and it determines how excitable the cell is.
  • A cell that is depolarized is usually more excitable. If a cell stays depolarized for a long time, the inactivation gates of its voltage-gated sodium channels stay closed, fewer channels are available to open, and the cell becomes less excitable.
