The patient analysis: your chart walk

Two patients, two analyzes. Each one is a recorded chart walk: you on camera, your handwritten chart in front of you, explaining what it shows.

Part 1, Camila due Sunday, November 1, 10:00 pm, with Midterm 1 Part 2, Dale due Wednesday, December 16, 10:00 pm, with Midterm 2 Worth 100 points in all, 50 for each part, the 5 percent capstone inside Use It Hand in the Canvas Studio video, its transcript, and that patient’s chart pages

What this is

You follow two people this term, one at a time. Camila Reyes is nineteen, well in August, and has one system in trouble at a time. She carries Weeks 1 to 8. Dale Whitcomb is sixty-eight, already carrying a bad heart and a damaged kidney, and has several systems in trouble at once. He carries Weeks 9 to 15. Each week you copy that week’s numbers into that patient’s chart by hand.

Both charts are in your chart book from the start, but you only work on Camila until Midterm 1. When her file closes in Week 8, you record her chart walk and turn it in with her chart pages. Then you pick up Dale in Week 9, and his chart walk comes in at the end of the term.

An analysis is not a summary of what happened to the patient, which is the thing I am least interested in. It is an argument about physiology, with the patient’s numbers as the evidence, and you make it out loud, the same way you answer a midterm question. Midterm 1 also includes a question about Camila, answered from memory like every midterm question, so keep her chart current every week.

How the chart walk works

  1. Record in Canvas Studio, the whole time, with your face and your chart both in view. A phone or laptop camera is fine.
  2. Only your handwritten chart is in front of you. No notes, no script, no screen, nothing typed. Your chart is the only thing you are allowed to read from.
  3. Point at your own pages as you talk: the week, the row, the number. Hold a page up to the camera when you use it.
  4. Work through the sections in order and say each heading out loud as you start it.
  5. Draw the drawing on camera, on a whiteboard or on paper, while you explain it.
  6. If you lose your place, pause and keep going, the way you would in front of a class. One continuous video; do not edit it.
  7. Turn in the transcript too. In Canvas Studio, open your video, go to the Captions tab and choose Request, with English as the language. When the captions are ready, open them with Review and Publish, fix only the words the machine got wrong, such as drug names and physiology terms, and publish. Then choose Download from the same menu and upload that caption file with your video. Do not reword what you said; the transcript has to match the video.

The point of the format is the same as the midterm: I want to hear you reason from your own chart. Reading a script aloud is easy to see and hear, and it scores as if the section were not there.

The one rule about sources

Every number you use has to come out of your own chart, and you have to say where: the week, and the flowsheet row or the results table it came from, while you point at it. A number with no source does not count as evidence, and a number that does not match what the chart actually says costs you the point twice, once for the error and once for not checking.

While you prepare, you may use anything you like to look up a mechanism, check a definition, or work through an explanation you did not follow the first time. What you cannot do is have something else build the argument, because it is built out of your own weekly decisions. In the last section, say out loud what you used, the way you have logged it all term.

Part 1: Camila’s analysis

Due Sunday, November 1 at 10:00 pm, the same moment Midterm 1 closes, so do not leave it for that morning. 50 points. A video of 10 to 15 minutes, with one drawing made on camera. Work through the six sections in order and say each heading as you start it.

1

Your claim

5 points

Say one idea that is true across Camila’s file, in two or three sentences. It has to be a claim about physiology, not a claim about her. “Camila got very sick” is not a claim. “In Camila, the number that looked reassuring was a concentration, not a content” is a claim, and it is one you can argue.

Comes from: your Week 8 page, which closes her file.

2

Camila, traced

15 points

Trace your claim through her file. Use three numbers from three different weeks, give the week and the row each one came from, and say what each is evidence of. Then name the one system in her file that was never actually the problem, even though its numbers moved, and say how you know.

Comes from: your Camila flowsheets and your Week 1 to Week 8 pages.

3

Where the treatment did the harm

10 points

Something that was given to help Camila created a new problem. Say what was given, what it was meant to change, the exact mechanism by which it caused the new problem, and the number in your flowsheet that caught it. Then say what you would have done instead, and what that alternative would have cost.

Comes from: her medication and intervention log, and the last column you filled in for her.

4

Where you changed your mind

10 points

Point at a week where you got it wrong. Hold that page up to the camera and read what you wrote at the time. Say which week you noticed, and what specifically changed it: a number, a question, a drawing that would not come out right. Then say what you would write on the earlier page now.

Real reasoning has wrong turns in it. An analysis that was never wrong anywhere is either not looking or not telling me, and both of those score below one that says plainly where it went off.

Comes from: your own handwriting, on a page you filled in before you knew how it turned out.

5

The drawing

5 points

Draw one picture on camera that holds Camila’s file: a control loop, a compartment diagram, a curve with her values marked on it, or a pathway with the break shown. Label it fully and explain it as you draw, because the explanation is graded with the drawing.

Comes from: the loops you drew in Weeks 1 to 8. This one may be a redrawing of one of them, done better.

6

The measurement nobody took, and your AI log

5 points

Name one measurement that was never taken in Camila’s file and that you now think should have been, and say what question it would have answered and when. Then say what you used AI for while preparing, if anything: what you asked, what you got, and what you did with it.

Comes from: Question 5 on your week pages, which asked this every week for a reason.

Every section is scored on the same three things: is the physiology right, is it evidenced from your own chart, and does it explain rather than assert. A correct number with no explanation earns half credit, the same rule as every week this term. A section read aloud from a script scores as if it were not there.
