The patient analysis

One analysis. Both patients. Written from the chart you have been keeping by hand since September, and from nothing else.

Due Wednesday, December 16, 10:00 pm Worth 100 points, the 5 percent capstone inside Use It Length 1,500 to 2,000 words, plus one drawing Hand in with your chart book, photographed or scanned

What this is

You have followed two people this term. Camila Reyes was nineteen, well in August, and had one system in trouble at a time. Dale Whitcomb was sixty-eight, already carrying a bad heart and a damaged kidney, and had four systems in trouble at once. You read fifteen entries between them, and you copied every number into your own flowsheets by hand.

This is where you say what all of that added up to. Not a summary of what happened to them, which is the thing I am least interested in. An argument about physiology, with their numbers as the evidence.

There are eight sections. Each one tells you what it is asking for and where in your chart book the material comes from. Write them in order, use the headings, and keep the whole thing between 1,500 and 2,000 words.

The one rule about sources

Every number you use has to come out of your own chart book, and you have to say where: the week, and the flowsheet row or the results table it came from. A number with no source does not count as evidence, and a number that does not match what the chart actually says costs you the point twice, once for the error and once for not checking.

You may use anything you like to look up a mechanism, check a definition, or work through an explanation you did not follow the first time. What you cannot do is ask something else to make the argument for you, because the argument is built out of fifteen weeks of your own decisions and there is no version of it sitting anywhere to be found. Log what you used in Section 8, the way you have logged it all term.

The eight sections

1

Your claim

10 points

Write one idea that is true of both patients, in two or three sentences. It has to be a claim about physiology, not a claim about either person. "Both patients got very sick" is not a claim. "In both patients, the number that looked reassuring was the one that was concentration rather than content" is a claim, and it is one you can argue.

Comes from: the closing sheet at the back of your chart book, where you drafted this.

2

Camila, traced

15 points

Take your claim and trace it through her file. Use three numbers from three different weeks, give the week and the row each one came from, and say what each is evidence of. Then name the one system in her file that was never actually the problem, even though its numbers moved, and say how you know.

Comes from: your Camila flowsheets and your Week 1 to Week 8 pages.

3

Dale, traced

15 points

Do the same through his file, with three numbers from three different weeks, sourced the same way. Then pick any one of his problems and show it talking to another one: name both, name the direction, and say which of his numbers records the conversation.

Comes from: your Dale flowsheets, your problem list, and your Week 9 to Week 15 pages.

4

Where they are different, and why

10 points

Both patients had a low bicarbonate, a raised heart rate and a kidney that was not doing its job. One of them walked out and went back to playing soccer. Name the physiological reason the two outcomes were different, and be specific: reserve, age and severity are labels, not reasons. Say what reserve actually is in the organ you choose, and point at the number in each file that measures it.

Comes from: both baseline columns, and any week where you recorded a capacity rather than a level.

5

Where the treatment did the harm

15 points

In both files, something that was given to help created a new problem. Name one in each patient. For each, say what was given, what it was meant to change, the exact mechanism by which it caused the new problem, and the number in your flowsheet that caught it. Then say what you would have done instead, and what that alternative would have cost.

Comes from: your medication and intervention log, and the last column you filled in for each patient.

6

Where you changed your mind

20 points

Point at a week where you got it wrong. Say what you thought at the time and quote your own page. Say which week you noticed, and what specifically changed it: a number, a question, a conversation, a drawing that would not come out right. Then say what you would write on the earlier page now.

This is the largest section in the assignment and it is the one I read first. Real reasoning has wrong turns in it. An analysis that was never wrong anywhere is either not looking or not telling me, and both of those score below one that says plainly where it went off.

Comes from: your own handwriting, on a page you filled in weeks before you knew how it turned out.

7

The drawing

10 points

One drawing, by hand, that holds both patients. A control loop, a compartment diagram, a curve with both of them marked on it, a pathway with the break shown in two places. Label it fully and write a short paragraph describing it in words, because the description is graded with the drawing and because a drawing you cannot describe is a drawing you did not understand.

Comes from: the fifteen loops you drew during the term. This one is allowed to be a redrawing of one of them, done better.

8

The measurement nobody took, and your AI log

5 points

Name one measurement that was never taken in either file and that you now think should have been, and say what question it would have answered and when. Then log your AI use for this assignment the way you have all term: what you asked, what you got, and what you did with it.

Comes from: Question 5 on several of your week pages, which asked this every week for a reason.

How it is graded

Every section is scored on the same three things: is the physiology right, is it evidenced from your own chart, and does it explain rather than assert. A correct number with no explanation earns half credit, the same rule as every week this term.

Before you hand it in

Check every one of these

  • Every number carries its week and the row or table it came from.
  • You have checked at least five of your numbers back against the chart book, not against memory.
  • Both patients appear in Sections 1, 4, 5 and 7, not only in the two sections named after them.
  • Section 6 points at a specific page, not at a feeling.
  • The drawing is labeled and the description matches it.
  • You are between 1,500 and 2,000 words.
  • Your chart book is photographed or scanned, all pages, in order, and uploaded with the analysis.
  • Your AI log is honest. It is 5 points and it has never once cost anyone more than that.

How to hand it in

Two files in the Week 15 assignment: the analysis as a PDF or a Word document, and the chart book as a single PDF. If you photograph the chart book, put the pages in order in one file rather than uploading twenty images. Both are due Wednesday, December 16 at 10:00 pm, which is the same day the second midterm closes, so do not leave them for that morning.

If something has gone wrong with your chart book, a lost binder, a flooded bag, a semester that got away from you, tell me before December 10 and we will work out what you can still hand in. Tell me on December 16 and there is much less I can do.

This is the last thing you write for me in this course, and it is the one I will remember. Take the two people seriously and say what you actually think. Dr. Sharilyn Rennie
