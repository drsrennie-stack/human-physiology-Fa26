The baseline story

How action potentials carry information, told as a story

In Story 3 our cell fired an action potential at one spot. Today we send it down the axon. We start with a row of dominoes, and there is no new vocabulary to learn first.

  • Each part adds one new piece of information and then asks you a question you can already answer.
  • You draw along on your own sheet: the arrows, the plus and minus signs and the labels.
  • A scientific name arrives only after you understand the idea it belongs to. There are no numbers today.

What we are building

The same cell and the same doors, now along a whole axon. By the end you can explain how the message travels without fading, why it goes one way, how it says strong or weak, what makes it fast, and three ways to stop it.

02

Part 1

A row of dominoes

Start with a row of dominoes. Push the first one hard enough and the whole row falls, one after another.

Ask

Does the last domino fall more gently than the first?

Can a domino that has fallen fall again right away?

Answer

No. The last one falls just as hard as the first. Your push only tipped the first domino. After that, each domino falls with its own stored energy, which it got when somebody stood it up.

And no. A fallen domino cannot fall again until it has been stood back up.

One more thing you already know: a push that is too weak knocks nothing over. It is the whole row or nothing.

Each one falls with its own energy → the last falls as hard as the first.

03

Part 2

Turn the dominoes into an axon

Our cell has a long axon, with the doors from Story 3 all the way along it. Patch 1 has just fired. Na+ came in, so the inside of patch 1 is positive. Next door, in patch 2, the inside is still negative.

Ask

Inside the axon, positive charge now sits right beside a negative region. Which way does that positive charge move?

Answer

Toward the negative region. Opposite charges attract, and nothing blocks the way along the inside of the axon. So positive charge spreads from patch 1 into patch 2.

That makes the inside of patch 2 less negative than it was.

Na+ comes in at patch 1 → positive charge spreads along the inside → patch 2 becomes less negative.

Now name it: local current

04

Part 3

The next patch fires

Patch 2 has the same voltage sensing sodium doors that you built in Story 3. You know what opens them.

Ask

The inside of patch 2 is becoming less negative. What do its voltage sensing sodium doors do?

Answer

When patch 2 reaches threshold they open. Na+ comes in through patch 2's own doors, and patch 2 fires a full action potential of its own.

Now the inside of patch 2 is positive, so its local current spreads on to patch 3, and patch 3 does the same thing. The action potential is made again, patch after patch.

Local current → next patch reaches threshold → its own sodium doors open → a new action potential.

Now name it: the action potential is regenerated (conduction)

05

Part 4

Why it arrives full size

In Story 3 a little ripple spread along the membrane, and it became smaller with distance and faded away.

Ask

Draw the little ripple in the middle and at the far end. Then draw the action potential in the middle and at the far end. Why are they different?

Answer

The ripple fades because nothing makes it again. The charge spreads out and leaks away, so it is smaller in the middle and gone at the far end.

The action potential is the same size everywhere. Every patch makes it again with its own doors and its own Na+ difference, the way each domino falls with its own stored energy. The one that reaches the far end is a fresh copy.

Made again at every patch → full size at the far end.

06

Part 5

Why it only goes forward

The local current from patch 2 spreads both ways along the inside: toward patch 3 and back toward patch 1. The sodium door in patch 1 is in the state we called shut and not ready.

Ask

Can the current flowing backward open the sodium doors of patch 1 again?

Answer

No. A door that is shut and not ready cannot open, however strongly it is pushed. It has to reset first, and it resets only after the inside has gone back to negative.

Patch 3 is closed and ready, so it opens next. The action potential can only go forward, away from where it started.

This is the fallen domino. Here is where the dominoes stop being a good comparison: nobody has to come and reset an axon. Each patch stands its own dominoes back up within a moment, and it can do that again and again.

Just fired → sodium doors shut and not ready → cannot fire again yet → one way only.

Now name it: refractory period

07

Part 6

Two kinds of not ready

A moment later. Patch 2: the sodium door is still shut and not ready. Patch 1: the sodium door is closed and ready again, but the slow potassium door is still open and K+ is still leaving.

Ask

Patch 2: would any push fire it now?

Patch 1: would it take the usual push, a smaller push or a bigger push?

Answer

Patch 2: no push of any size works, because there are no ready doors to open.

Patch 1: a bigger push than usual. K+ leaving carries positive charge out as fast as the push brings it in, and the inside is a little more negative than at rest, so it is farther from threshold.

An honest everyday comparison is a toilet that has just been flushed. While the tank is empty, nothing happens when you press the handle. While it is refilling, you can flush again, but you get a weaker flush.

First: no push works. Then: only a bigger push works. Then: back to normal.

Now name it: absolute refractory period, then relative refractory period

08

Part 7

Hard press or light touch?

Every action potential is the same size. A doorbell is like that too: it makes the same sound however hard you press the button.

Ask

With a doorbell that always sounds the same, how could you tell the people inside that it is urgent? Find two ways. Then draw the action potentials for the light touch and the hard press.

Answer

You ring it again and again, quickly. And more people can each ring their own bell.

An axon does the first: a stronger stimulus makes it fire more action potentials each second, all of them the same size. The nervous system does the second: a harder press reaches more endings, so more axons fire.

The brain reads both: how often each axon fires, and how many axons are firing.

Stronger stimulus → more action potentials per second, and more axons firing. Never bigger ones.

Now name it: frequency coding and recruitment

09

Part 8

What sets the fastest firing?

Go back to the two kinds of not ready. They follow every single action potential.

Ask

With an ordinary push, where is the earliest place the next action potential can start? With the strongest possible push?

Answer

With an ordinary push, not until both blocks are over. With a bigger push it can start inside the dashed block, and it may come out a little smaller, because only some of the sodium doors are ready.

Even the strongest push cannot start one inside the shut and not ready block. So that block sets the closest that two action potentials can ever be, and that sets the fastest an axon can fire. The refractory period also keeps action potentials separate. They never pile on top of each other.

The doors have to become ready again → there is a fastest possible firing.

10

Part 9

Make it faster: a wider axon

Some messages have to travel fast. Local current flows through the fluid inside the axon. A wide pipe carries water more easily than a narrow pipe, because there is more room for the flow.

Ask

In which axon does the local current reach farther along? What does that do to the speed of the message?

Answer

In the wide axon. There is more room inside, so there is less resistance to the flow of charge, and the current reaches farther before it fades.

Doors farther along reach threshold sooner, so the action potential travels faster.

Wider axon → less resistance inside → current reaches farther → faster.

Now name it: axon diameter

11

Part 10

The leaky hose

A hose with holes along it loses water before the far end. Our membrane has holes too: the leak doors from Story 1. Positive charge that is flowing along the inside can go out through them.

Ask

What happens to the local current on its way to the next sodium doors? What could you do to a leaky hose, and to an axon?

Answer

Some of the current leaks out at every leak door, so less of it reaches the next sodium doors, and they take longer to reach threshold.

You would wrap the hose. The axon is wrapped too. Story 2 covered the glial cells that wrap their own membrane around an axon, layer on layer.

Leaks along the way → less current arrives. Wrap the axon → the current stays inside.

Now name it: myelin

12

Part 11

Wrapped, with gaps

The myelin covers the leak doors, so very little current can leak out under it. The voltage sensing sodium doors are gathered in the short bare gaps between one stretch of myelin and the next.

Ask

Draw the path of the local current from gap 1. Where is the next place an action potential can be made?

Answer

The current flows along the inside, under the myelin, and reaches gap 2 still strong. Gap 2 is the next place with sodium doors, so the action potential is made again there, and then at gap 3.

The name comes from the Latin word for jumping, but nothing jumps. Current flows quickly under the wrapping, and the slow step, opening doors, happens only at the gaps. There are far fewer stops, so the message arrives much sooner.

Myelin → no leak → made again only at the gaps → far fewer stops → much faster.

Now name it: nodes of Ranvier, saltatory conduction

13

Part 12

Strip the wrapping from one stretch

In some diseases the myelin is lost from a stretch of axon. The membrane that was under it has leak doors, and it has almost no sodium doors of its own.

Ask

What happens to the current in the bare stretch? Does gap 3 still get enough?

Answer

The current leaks out through the uncovered leak doors, as it did in the leaky hose. Less of it reaches gap 3.

Gap 3 takes longer to reach threshold, so the message is late. If too little arrives, gap 3 never reaches threshold and the message stops. The bare stretch cannot help, because it has almost no sodium doors.

Myelin lost → current leaks out → the next gap fires late, or not at all.

Now name it: demyelination

14

Part 13

Change the fluid outside: more potassium

Go back to Story 1. The inside of the resting cell is negative mostly because K+ leaks out. Now put more K+ than normal in the fluid outside the cell.

Ask

Does more or less K+ leak out now? So is the inside more negative or less negative? Is the cell closer to threshold or farther from it?

Answer

Less K+ leaks out, because the difference between inside and outside is smaller, so the push out is weaker. Fewer positive charges leave, so the inside at rest is less negative.

Less negative is closer to threshold. At first the cell is easier to fire.

More K+ outside → less K+ leaks out → inside less negative → closer to threshold.

Now name it: hyperkalemia (more K+ than normal in the blood)

15

Part 14

Held there for a long time

In Story 3 the slow gate of the sodium door could reset only after the inside went back to its negative resting level. This cell is held less negative, and it stays there.

Ask

What state do its sodium doors end up in? Can the cell fire? And what would less K+ outside do?

Answer

Shut and not ready. The slow gates shut and cannot reset, so there are no ready doors to open, and the cell cannot fire. Easier to fire at first, then not able to fire at all.

Less K+ outside is the reverse of part 13. More K+ leaks out, the inside is more negative, and the cell is farther from threshold, so it is harder to fire.

Held less negative → sodium doors shut and not ready → cannot fire.

Now name it: hypokalemia (less K+ than normal in the blood)

16

Part 15

Numbing medicine

This is the plainest experiment of all. The medicine blocks the voltage sensing sodium doors, and only in the stretch where it was put. Patch 1 has just fired.

Ask

Where does the message stop? Is anything wrong with patch 3?

Answer

It stops at patch 2. Local current still flows into patch 2, but no sodium doors can open there, so no new action potential is made. The current fades like the little ripple.

Nothing is wrong with patch 3. Its doors are closed and ready, but no push ever reaches it. One blocked stretch is enough, because the message has to be made again at every patch on the way. When the medicine is carried away, everything works again.

Sodium doors blocked in one stretch → not made again there → the message stops there.

Now name it: local anesthetic
