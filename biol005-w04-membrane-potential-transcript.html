The whole idea in six sentences

Read this before you record. If these six sentences make sense, the rest is detail.

  1. The pump uses ATP to make potassium high inside the cell and sodium high outside. It makes almost no voltage itself.
  2. The resting membrane has many open potassium channels and very few open sodium channels.
  3. Potassium diffuses out, and each potassium that leaves strands one negative charge inside, so the inside of the membrane becomes negative.
  4. That negative charge pulls potassium back, so the leaving slows down and stops when the pull in equals the push out. For potassium that is about −95 mV.
  5. A small sodium leak brings a little positive charge in and raises the resting potential to about −70 mV.
  6. Change the potassium outside the cell and you change the push out, so every resting potential in the body shifts. That is why blood potassium matters so much.

The resting membrane potential

03

Resting potential and action potential are different things

55 sec

Two terms sound alike and students mix them up, so let us separate them now. The resting membrane potential is a steady voltage across the membrane of a cell that is not being stimulated. Almost every cell in your body has one. A liver cell has one. A fat cell has one.

An action potential is different. It is a very fast change in that voltage that travels along the membrane, and only neurons and muscle cells can make one. So every cell has a resting potential, and only some cells can fire action potentials. Today is only the resting potential. Next lecture is the action potential, and it will be much easier if today makes sense.

04

What minus 70 millivolts actually means

95 sec

Before we build a voltage, let us be clear about what we are measuring. A voltage is always a comparison between two places. Here the two places are the inside of the cell and the outside. We put one electrode inside, we leave one outside, and the meter tells us the difference.

By agreement we call the outside zero. So when the meter says minus 70 millivolts, it means the inside is 70 millivolts more negative than the outside. A millivolt is a thousandth of a volt. A AA battery is 1,500 millivolts, so this is small, about one twentieth of a battery.

Now the part that is very easy to get wrong. The inside of the cell is not full of negative charge. If you took a sample of the fluid inside the cell, it would be neutral, the same number of positives and negatives. The same is true outside. The only place the charges are unequal is right at the membrane: a very thin layer of extra negative charge on the inner face, and a very thin layer of extra positive charge on the outer face. Look at the plus and minus signs in the figure. They are only along the membrane. That is the whole voltage.

DrawWith the pen, trace the path of the two wires to the meter. Then run the pen along the row of minus signs inside and the row of plus signs outside, and write: only here. Write NEUTRAL in the middle of the cell.
05

The nucleus decides which proteins are in the membrane

100 sec

Everything we add to the membrane today is a protein: the pump is a protein, and every channel is a protein. So where do they come from? From genes. The gene for a channel is copied into mRNA in the nucleus. Ribosomes on the rough ER read that mRNA and build the protein right into the ER membrane. The Golgi finishes it, a vesicle carries it to the surface, and when the vesicle fuses with the cell membrane, the channel is now part of that membrane.

Here is the important idea. Every one of your cells has the same genes. What makes a neuron different from a liver cell is which genes are switched on. Every cell switches on the gene for the sodium potassium pump and the gene for potassium leak channels. That is why every cell has a resting potential. Neurons and muscle cells also switch on the genes for voltage gated sodium channels, and that is why only they can fire action potentials.

One more thing to keep in mind for the rest of the lecture. A cell can put more copies of a channel into its membrane, or take some out. More open channels for an ion means that ion crosses more easily. We call that permeability, and permeability is going to decide everything today.

Start with a cell that has no voltage

65 sec

Here is our starting cell. It is imaginary. It has a lipid membrane and no proteins in it at all. Ions cannot cross the oily middle of a membrane, so nothing moves, and the voltmeter reads zero. For a voltage to appear, two things have to happen. 07

Add the sodium potassium pump

85 sec

The first protein we add is the sodium potassium pump. Every cell in your body has it. Each time it cycles, it moves three sodium ions out of the cell and two potassium ions in, and it uses one ATP to do it. It needs the ATP because it is moving both ions toward the side where there is already more of them. That is uphill, and uphill costs energy.

This pump uses a lot of energy, somewhere between a fifth and a third of all the ATP your body uses at rest. So it must be doing something important.

Now, does the pump make a voltage? A little. Three positives go out for every two that come in, so the inside loses a little positive charge and becomes slightly negative. But look at the voltmeter: about minus 3 millivolts. A real neuron is at minus 70. So the pump by itself does not explain the resting potential. Remember that, because a lot of students leave this topic believing the pump makes the voltage. It does not, at least not directly.

DrawTrace the arrow for 3 sodium out in navy and 2 potassium in in rust. Write 3+ out, 2+ in, then: net 1+ lost each cycle.
DrawCircle the voltmeter reading and write beside it: not enough.

08

The pump creates two steep concentration gradients

65 sec

So what is the pump really doing? It is creating concentration differences. After the pump has been running, potassium is about 140 inside the cell and only 4 outside. That is 35 times more concentrated inside. Sodium is the opposite: about 145 outside and 15 inside.

Learn these four numbers. Half of this course is easier if you know them. Potassium is high inside. Sodium is high outside.

A concentration difference is stored energy, like water held behind a dam. The water wants to run downhill, and it will as soon as you open a gate. Right now there is no gate. Potassium is being pushed outward by its concentration difference, sodium is being pushed inward by its concentration difference, and neither one can move. So let us open a gate, for potassium only, and predict what happens.

Add potassium leak channels

70 sec

The second protein is a potassium leak channel. It is a pore that is always open, and it is shaped so that potassium fits through and sodium does not. The channel does not push anything. It just makes it possible for potassium to cross.

So which way does potassium go? There are 140 inside and 4 outside, so it diffuses out, from high concentration to low.

Now follow one potassium ion. It is positive. Before it left, it was paired with one of those large negative molecules, a triangle. The potassium leaves, and the triangle cannot follow, because it is too big for any channel. So every potassium ion that leaves takes one positive charge out and leaves one negative charge behind. The inside of the membrane gets a little more negative with every ion that leaves. That is the voltage, and that is where it comes from: potassium leaving, and its negative partner staying behind.

DrawPick one potassium circle near a channel. Draw its path out through the channel in rust. Then circle a triangle nearby and write: left behind, negative.
DrawWrite beside the voltmeter: every K that leaves makes the inside more negative.
10

Why potassium stops leaving

2 min 10

If nothing opposed it, potassium would keep leaving until it was the same inside and outside. It does not, and this slide is the reason. This is the most important slide today, so take your time with it.

There are two forces on every potassium ion. The first is the concentration difference, which pushes potassium out. I will call that the chemical push. It stays about the same the whole time, because the concentrations barely change.

The second force is electrical. Potassium is positive, and the inside of the membrane is becoming negative. Opposites attract, so the negative inside pulls potassium back in. At the very start the inside is barely negative, so that pull is tiny, and potassium leaves quickly. That is panel one.

But every potassium that leaves makes the inside more negative, so the pull back in gets stronger. In panel two, at minus 50, potassium is still leaving, but more slowly.

Eventually the inside is negative enough that the pull back in is exactly as strong as the push out. That is panel three. Potassium ions are still moving through the channels in both directions, but for every one that leaves, one comes back. There is no net movement. The voltage where that happens is called the equilibrium potential for potassium. For these concentrations it is about minus 95 millivolts.

If it helps, picture a crowded room with one open door. People move out of the crowded room, but each person who leaves is attached by a rubber band to a partner who has to stay inside. The more people leave, the harder the rubber bands pull them back, until the pull back matches the push to get out.

DrawIn each panel, trace the rust arrow and say: same push. Then trace the navy arrow in panel 1, 2 and 3 and say: small, bigger, equal.
DrawUnder panel 3 write: push out = pull in, so no NET movement. Underline NET twice.

11

Almost no ions had to move

70 sec

Here is a mistake almost everyone makes. They imagine potassium draining out of the cell until the cell is nearly empty. That is not what happens. To make minus 95 millivolts, far fewer than one potassium ion in a thousand has to leave. The concentration inside is still 140 afterward. You could not measure the difference.

How can so few ions make a real voltage? Because of where they end up. The potassium ions that leave do not drift away. They stay right against the outside of the membrane, held there by the negative charges they left on the inside, which are only 8 nanometers away. Opposite charges held that close together make a large voltage from very few charges.

So keep two ideas separate. The concentrations decide what the voltage will be. A tiny number of ions moving at the membrane is what actually produces it.

DoOpen the reveal box after you ask the question aloud.
DrawWrite: concentrations decide the voltage. A few ions at the membrane make it.

Now let only sodium cross

60 sec

To check that you understand, let us run the same experiment with sodium. This is a second imaginary cell. Same pump, same gradients, but this time there are no potassium channels, only sodium channels.

Sodium is high outside, so the chemical push is inward. And at the start the inside is slightly negative, which also attracts positive sodium. Both forces point in, so sodium enters quickly.

Every sodium that enters brings a positive charge in. The inside goes from slightly negative, to zero, to positive. And once the inside is positive, it starts to repel sodium. Now the electrical force points out while the chemical push still points in. They balance at about plus 61 millivolts. That is the equilibrium potential for sodium.

Same rules, opposite answer, because the concentration gradient points the opposite way.

Each ion moves the membrane potential toward its own equilibrium potential

65 sec

Now put both cells on one number line. If only potassium can cross, the membrane goes to minus 95. If only sodium can cross, it goes to plus 61. Whenever an ion can cross the membrane, it moves the membrane potential toward its own equilibrium potential.

So what happens in a real cell, where both can cross? The answer depends on permeability, which means how many open channels each ion has. The ion with more open channels has more influence.

A real resting neuron is at minus 70. Look where that is on the line. It is very close to potassium and a long way from sodium. What does that tell you about the membrane at rest? It must be much more permeable to potassium than to sodium, and it is, by about 25 to 40 times.

14

The Nernst equation, read in plain words

70 sec

Now we look at the equation behind those numbers. The Nernst equation answers one question: for this ion, with these concentrations, what voltage would exactly balance the concentration difference? You will learn to read it, not calculate with it.

Here is the form we use at body temperature. The equilibrium potential equals 61.5 divided by z, times the log of the concentration outside over the concentration inside. z is the charge on the ion: plus 1 for potassium and sodium, minus 1 for chloride, plus 2 for calcium.

Three things to notice. First, only the ratio matters, outside over inside, and the steeper the gradient, the bigger the voltage it takes to balance it. Second, the sign comes from two things together, which way the gradient points and the charge on the ion. A positive ion that is crowded inside needs a negative inside to hold it back, so its equilibrium potential is negative. Third, the 61.5 is just physical constants and body temperature multiplied together. You do not need to derive it, and you will never plug numbers into it in this course.

DrawBox the ratio and write: outside over inside. Circle z and write: charge. Circle 61.5 and write: constants at 37 C (98.6 F).
DoSay it plainly before moving on: we read this equation, and every problem today is a prediction, not arithmetic.
15

Reason out the equilibrium potential for potassium

65 sec

Let us do potassium. 4 outside, 140 inside. Pause and try it before I do, and use the two forces, not numbers.

Potassium is crowded inside, so the chemical push is out. What would hold it back? Potassium is positive, so the inside would have to be negative to pull it back in. That is the whole reason the equilibrium potential for potassium is negative. The equation agrees: outside over inside is less than 1, and for a positive ion that gives a negative answer. For these concentrations it comes out at about minus 95 millivolts. Some books say minus 90 because they use slightly different concentrations, and either is fine.

Now read that as a sentence: if potassium were the only ion that could cross, the inside would settle at minus 95, and at that voltage potassium would stop moving on net.

So which way is potassium moving in a real neuron at minus 70? Minus 70 is not negative enough to hold potassium in. The push out is still bigger than the pull in, so potassium leaks out, and every ion that leaves pulls the membrane a little further toward minus 95. That is the rule for every ion: open its channels and it drags the membrane toward its own equilibrium potential.

DoOpen the work and go through it one line at a time.

16

Now sodium, and then chloride

70 sec

Same reasoning for sodium: 145 outside, 15 inside. Sodium is crowded outside, so the chemical push is in. To hold a positive ion out, the inside would have to be positive, so the equilibrium potential for sodium is positive, about plus 61. The neuron is at minus 70, a long way below that. Both forces point in, so the moment sodium channels open, sodium rushes in and drags the membrane up toward plus 61. The only reason that is not happening right now is that almost all of its channels are closed.

Chloride is the one where people lose the sign. 110 outside and 10 inside, so chloride is also crowded outside and also pushed in. But chloride is negative. What pushes a negative ion back out? A negative inside. So the equilibrium potential for chloride is negative, about minus 64, even though its gradient points the same way as sodium's. The charge flipped the sign. A neuron at minus 70 is already close to minus 64, so chloride is nearly balanced and hardly moves at rest.

Now rank the driving forces. Measure the gap from minus 70 to each ion's equilibrium potential. Sodium, about 131. Potassium, about 25. Chloride, about 6. Sodium has by far the biggest push, and it is held back only by closed channels.

17

A real resting cell leaks a little sodium too

65 sec

Now the real cell. It has the pump, it has many potassium leak channels, and it also has a few sodium leak channels. That small sodium leak brings a little positive charge in, and it raises the potential from minus 95 up to about minus 70.

At minus 70, neither ion is at its own equilibrium. Potassium keeps leaking out, and a little sodium keeps leaking in. So why does the voltage stay steady? Because the two leaks carry equal and opposite charge, and the pump keeps putting the ions back where they came from.

This is called a steady state, and it is different from an equilibrium. An equilibrium stays put without any energy. A steady state stays put only as long as energy keeps being supplied. Here that energy is ATP, through the pump. If the pump stops, this cell slowly loses its gradients.

DrawTrace potassium out in rust and sodium in in navy, and make the sodium arrow much thinner. Then draw a loop through the pump and write: puts them back.
DrawWrite: steady state = needs ATP. Equilibrium = needs nothing.
18

The Goldman equation: several ions at once

90 sec

The Nernst equation handles one ion. When more than one ion can cross, we use the Goldman equation. It looks worse than it is, and again you will read it, not calculate with it. It takes each ion's concentrations and multiplies them by its permeability, P. Permeability just means how easily that ion crosses, which comes down to how many of its channels are open.

Read it as a tug of war. Each ion pulls the membrane toward its own equilibrium potential, and the ion with more open channels pulls harder. At rest the membrane is about 25 times more permeable to potassium than to sodium. So where should the membrane sit? Close to potassium's minus 95, and nowhere near halfway to sodium's plus 61. The small sodium leak drags it up a little, to about minus 70. The equation gives about minus 71, which is what you measure in a real neuron.

Now try the extremes in your head. Take sodium's permeability to zero, and the membrane sits at potassium's minus 95. That was cell 1. Make sodium's permeability huge, and it heads for plus 61. That was cell 2. A small permeability still counts, because sodium is so far from its own equilibrium potential and there is so much of it outside. That is why the resting potential is minus 70 and not minus 95.

19

The pump contributes in two ways, one small and one large

65 sec

Let us come back to the pump, because this is a common exam question. The pump contributes to the resting potential in two ways. Directly, by moving three charges out for every two in, it adds a few millivolts. That is the small contribution.

Indirectly, it builds the potassium and sodium gradients. Without those gradients the leak channels have nothing to work with, and there is no voltage at all. That is the large contribution.

So if someone asks what makes the resting potential, the answer is potassium leaking out through leak channels. If they ask what the pump does, the answer is that it keeps the cell full of potassium so that leak can happen. Try the question in the box: if a poison blocks every pump, what does the voltmeter show one second later, and what does it show hours later?

21

Four kinds of ion channel, sorted by what opens them

65 sec

We have only used leak channels today, because they are the only channels open in a resting cell. But there are three other kinds, and the only thing that separates them is what makes them open.

Leak channels have no gate, so they are always open. Voltage gated channels open when the membrane potential changes. They run along axons and they make the action potential. Ligand gated channels open when a specific molecule binds to them, like a neurotransmitter. The acetylcholine receptor on skeletal muscle is the classic example. Mechanically gated channels open when the membrane is stretched or pressed, like the touch receptors in your skin.

And the Goldman equation already tells you what opening any channel does. It raises the permeability for that ion, so the membrane potential moves toward that ion's equilibrium potential.

DrawPut a star beside Leak channel and write: the only one open at rest.

22

Four words for a membrane potential that moves

50 sec

Four vocabulary words, all defined by direction compared with rest. A resting membrane is polarized, because it has a negative side and a positive side.

Depolarization means the inside becomes less negative than rest. The trace goes up, toward zero. Overshoot is the part of the trace above zero, where the inside is briefly positive. Repolarization is the return back toward rest. And hyperpolarization means more negative than rest, so the trace dips below the resting line.

Here is why it matters. A depolarized cell is closer to threshold, so it is easier to fire. A hyperpolarized cell is farther from threshold, so it is harder to fire.

DrawTrace the curve with the pen as you say each word. Shade the area above the 0 line for overshoot and the area below the resting line for hyperpolarization.
23

Change the blood potassium and predict

85 sec

Now we change the conditions. The potassium in the blood is the outside potassium for every cell in the body. So what happens to a cell when blood potassium changes? Predict it before I tell you.

Take our patient's value, 7. More potassium outside means a smaller concentration difference, so the push out is weaker and it takes less negative charge inside to balance it. The equilibrium potential for potassium gets less negative, from about minus 95 to about minus 80. The resting potential follows potassium, because the membrane is mostly permeable to potassium, so it moves up too, from about minus 71 to about minus 64. Every cell in the body has depolarized.

Low potassium does the opposite. At 2.5, the gradient is steeper, the equilibrium potential for potassium gets more negative, and the resting potential drops to about minus 76. The cell is hyperpolarized, farther from threshold and harder to excite, and the patient is weak.

High potassium is the tricky one. At first the cells are closer to threshold, so they fire more easily. But if a cell stays depolarized, its voltage gated sodium channels switch into an inactivated state and cannot reopen. Now the cell cannot fire at all. In skeletal muscle that is weakness. In the heart it is slow, disorganized conduction.

Now change the blood sodium by even more

65 sec

Here is the answer to the question from the start. Raise potassium by 3, from 4 to 7, and the resting potential moves 7 millivolts. Now lower sodium by 20, from 145 to 125, which is a serious lab value, and the resting potential moves 2 millivolts.

Two reasons, and both are in the equations. First, permeability. The resting membrane is mostly permeable to potassium, so it follows potassium and mostly ignores sodium. Second, ratios. The Nernst equation uses the ratio of outside to inside. Going from 4 to 7 almost doubles the outside potassium. Going from 145 to 125 changes sodium by only 14 percent.

Low sodium is still dangerous, but for a different reason: water moves into brain cells and they swell. That is osmosis, and it is a different problem from membrane potential.
