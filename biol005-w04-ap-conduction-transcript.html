The whole idea in a few sentences

Read this before you record. If these sentences make sense, the rest is detail.

  1. After a sodium channel opens it inactivates, and it cannot open again until the membrane has repolarized. For about 1 to 2 ms no stimulus can fire that membrane (absolute refractory period). For a few ms more, it takes a bigger stimulus (relative refractory period).
  2. Because every action potential is the same size, a stronger stimulus is signaled by more action potentials per second, up to a ceiling of 1 divided by the absolute refractory period, and by more neurons firing.
  3. An action potential moves because sodium entering at one patch makes the inside positive, and that positive charge spreads to the next patch and brings it to threshold. Each patch makes a new, full size action potential, so the signal does not fade.
  4. It goes one way because the patch behind is refractory.
  5. Wider axons and myelinated axons conduct faster, because the spreading current reaches farther. In a myelinated axon only the nodes fire, which is faster and costs less ATP.
  6. Anything that reduces the number of sodium channels that are closed and ready (high potassium, a local anesthetic) or the current reaching them (lost myelin) slows or stops the signal. Low calcium does the opposite and makes the channels open too easily.

How action potentials carry information

70 sec

Last time we built one action potential. Today we ask what a neuron can do with it. An action potential is all or none, the same size every time. So how does a nerve tell your brain a light touch from a hard press? How does the signal get from your toe to your spinal cord, a full meter, without fading? And how fast does it get there?

Here is the problem we will solve. You are at the dentist. Lidocaine is injected next to the nerve for your lower teeth. In a few minutes your lip is numb and the drill does not hurt. But you can still feel the dentist pushing on the tooth. A few hours later everything is back. The drug never reached the pain receptors in the tooth. They still respond to the drill. So where did the message stop? And why did pain go away when pressure did not?

03

Three states of the voltage gated sodium channel

55 sec

A quick reminder from last time, because everything today depends on it. The voltage gated sodium channel has two gates, so it has three states. Closed and ready: the activation gate is shut, and a depolarization to threshold will open it. Open: both gates open, sodium enters, and this lasts less than a millisecond. And inactivated: the inactivation gate has closed.

Here is the one fact to carry through the whole lecture. An inactivated channel cannot open. It does not matter how hard you stimulate it. It has to reset first, and it only resets after the membrane has gone back down to a negative voltage. So the order is always closed, open, inactivated, and back to closed. It never skips a step.

DrawIn navy, draw a loop beside the table: closed, arrow, open, arrow, inactivated, arrow back to closed. On the last arrow write: only after repolarizing.

04

For a few milliseconds after it fires, the membrane cannot fire normally

80 sec

Now look at what that means for the membrane. Here is one action potential. The shaded band in the middle is the absolute refractory period. It starts at threshold and lasts about one to two milliseconds. During that time the sodium channels are open or inactivated, as the left box shows. There are no closed, ready channels, so a second stimulus, even a huge one, has nothing to open.

The hatched band after it is the relative refractory period, a few more milliseconds. In the right box, some sodium channels have reset to closed, but not all. And the potassium channels are still open, so potassium is still leaving, and that is why the trace dips below the resting line. The membrane is hyperpolarized.

So can the membrane fire during this period? Yes, but it is harder. You need a bigger stimulus than normal. And look at the dashed line: the action potential you get is smaller, because only some of the sodium channels are available to take part.

DrawIn navy, trace the first action potential. In rust, bracket the absolute period on the time axis and write: no ready channels.
DrawIn rust, circle the dip below the resting line and write: K channels still open. Then trace the dashed second action potential and write: smaller.
05

What separates the two periods is the state of the channels

80 sec

Here are the two periods side by side, and what separates them is the state of the channels. Try the question. A second stimulus, twice the normal strength, arrives one millisecond after the first action potential began. In a second trial, it arrives at four milliseconds. What happens each time? Pause and decide.

At one millisecond we are in the absolute period. Nothing happens. A stimulus works by opening closed channels, and there are none. At four milliseconds we are in the relative period. The membrane is farther from threshold, and open potassium channels are letting positive charge leak back out. But a double strength stimulus can get there, and you get a smaller than normal action potential.

For an everyday picture, use a toilet. Right after you flush, the tank is empty, and pressing the handle does nothing, however hard you press. That is the absolute refractory period. While the tank refills, you can flush again, but the flush is weak. That is the relative refractory period.

06

What refractory periods do, and the fastest a neuron can fire

65 sec

So what are refractory periods for? Three things. First, they keep action potentials separate, so they never pile up on each other. Second, they set a top speed for firing. Third, they make the action potential travel one way. We will see that on slide 9.

Let us put a number on the second one. Suppose the absolute refractory period is two milliseconds. Each action potential needs its own two milliseconds. How many fit in one second? Two milliseconds is 0.002 seconds. One divided by 0.002 is 500. So the ceiling is 500 action potentials per second. If the refractory period were one millisecond, the ceiling would be 1,000.

Be careful: it is a ceiling. Real neurons usually fire far below it, often well under a hundred per second. The limit comes from the sodium channel.

DoOpen Show the work and go through it line by line.
DrawIn navy write: 2 ms = 0.002 s. Under it: 1 / 0.002 = 500 per second. In rust write: ceiling, not typical.
07

Every action potential is the same size, so strength is coded by frequency

80 sec

Now the first question. How does a neuron say that a stimulus is stronger? Read this figure from the top down: weak, medium, strong.

Row two is the graded potential at the trigger zone. Graded potentials do change size. A stronger stimulus gives a bigger one that stays above the threshold line longer.

Row three is what the axon does. As long as the graded potential is above threshold, the trigger zone keeps firing, and a bigger graded potential can fire it earlier in the relative refractory period, so the action potentials come closer together. Two, then five, then ten, in the same amount of time. And look at the dashed line across the top. Every one of them is exactly the same height.

So strength is coded by frequency, which means how many action potentials per second. And row four shows why that works. Each action potential releases about the same amount of neurotransmitter at the terminal. More action potentials per second, more neurotransmitter. That is how the next cell gets the message.

DrawIn navy, run the pen down the Strong column: tall bar, big graded potential, many spikes, many dots.
DrawIn rust, trace the dashed line across the tops of all the spikes and write: same height. Then write: count them, 2, 5, 10.
08

A stronger stimulus also recruits more neurons

60 sec

One axon has only that one way to say stronger: fire faster. But the nervous system has a second way. Look at the skin. With a gentle touch, one receptor is firing. Press hard, and the dent is deeper and wider. It reaches more receptors, including ones that need a stronger push. Now five are firing. This is called recruitment.

So the brain reads two things: how fast each axon is firing, and how many axons are firing.

Try the question. An axon with a two millisecond refractory period is already firing at 400 per second, and the stimulus doubles. This axon cannot report that, because its ceiling is 500. But the stronger stimulus recruits neighbors that were silent, and that is how the brain finds out.

DrawIn rust, circle the one filled receptor on the left and the five on the right. Write 1 and 5.

09

Local current fires the next patch of membrane

105 sec

Now the second question. How does the action potential move? The figure freezes one moment. The action potential is traveling to the right, and the membrane is divided into three zones.

Start in the middle, the active zone. Sodium channels are open. Sodium has entered. So the inside of the membrane here is positive. Now look next door, to the right. That membrane is still at rest, so the inside there is negative. You have positive charge sitting right beside negative charge, inside the same axon. Positive charge moves toward the negative region. That movement of charge is called local current.

What does that current do to the resting membrane ahead? It depolarizes it, exactly the way a graded potential would. When that patch reaches threshold, its own sodium channels open, and it fires its own full action potential. Then that patch does the same thing to the next patch. And so on, all the way down.

But the current also spreads backward, to the left. Why does the action potential not go back the way it came? Look at the zone behind. That membrane fired a moment ago. Its sodium channels are inactivated, and its potassium channels are open. It is refractory. The backward current arrives, and nothing can open. By the time that membrane has recovered, the active zone is too far away. So the action potential only goes forward.

DrawIn navy, trace the forward current arrow and write: depolarizes to threshold, fires. In rust, trace the backward arrow and put an X at its tip. Write: refractory.
DrawWrite the chain along the bottom: Na in, inside positive, current spreads, next patch reaches threshold, fires.
DoDo not rush this slide. Say the chain of cause and effect twice if you need to.
10

The action potential is made again at every patch, so it arrives full size

85 sec

Compare this with a graded potential. A graded potential spreads by local current and nothing renews it. Charge leaks out as it goes, and within a few millimeters it is gone.

An action potential does not get smaller, because each patch of membrane makes a brand new one, using its own sodium and potassium gradients. The action potential that arrives at the terminal is not the one that left the trigger zone. It is a fresh copy, and every copy is full size. We say the action potential is regenerated.

A row of dominoes is a fair comparison. Each domino falls with its own energy, so the last one falls just as hard as the first. And once a domino has fallen, it cannot fall again until someone stands it back up. That is its refractory period.

Now the question in the box. In the lab, you stimulate an axon in the middle. The action potential goes both ways, because the membrane on both sides is at rest. In your body it goes one way only because it starts at one end.

DrawIn the table, in rust circle Decreases. In navy circle The same at the far end.

11

Two things decide how fast an axon conducts

75 sec

Third question: how fast? Two things decide it. The first is the diameter of the axon. The local current flows through the inside of the axon. A wider axon is like a wider pipe. There is less resistance, so the current spreads farther ahead, and membrane farther along reaches threshold sooner.

The second is how much current leaks out through the membrane along the way. Current that leaks out is wasted. Myelin is insulation, layer upon layer of glial cell membrane, and it lets very little current leak out. So the current spreads farther and faster.

Which one gives more speed for the space? The squid has no myelin, so it makes a giant axon, half a millimeter across, and gets about 25 meters per second. Your biggest myelinated axons are 20 micrometers across and reach 120 meters per second. You could fit about 625 of yours into the space of one squid axon. Myelin gives far more speed for the space it takes.

DoRead the predict box, pause, then open the reveal.
DrawIn navy write: wide = low resistance inside. In rust write: myelin = little leak. Then write 500 / 20 = 25, and 25 squared = 625.
12

Continuous conduction and saltatory conduction

90 sec

Here are two axons of the same diameter. The top one has no myelin. Its whole membrane is shaded, because every patch has to depolarize and fire in turn. The current arrows are short steps, and at every step channels have to open, which takes time. This is continuous conduction, and it is slow.

The bottom one has myelin. Now only the nodes of Ranvier are shaded. The sodium channels are packed into the nodes. Under the myelin there are almost none, and no action potential happens there. The current arrows are now long steps. Current from one node travels under the myelin, hardly leaks, and arrives at the next node still strong enough to bring it to threshold. The action potential is regenerated only at the nodes.

This is called saltatory conduction, which means jumping. But be careful, because nothing really jumps. What moves between the nodes is local current, and current spreads very fast. The slow part is opening channels, and now that only happens at the nodes.

It is also cheaper. Sodium only enters at the nodes, so far fewer ions cross, and the pump has less work to do afterward.

DrawIn rust, draw many short hops along the top axon. In navy, draw four long hops node to node along the bottom axon.
DrawCircle one node in rust and write: channels here. Put the pen on the myelin and write: no action potential here, only current.

Real axons conduct at anywhere from 0.5 to 120 meters per second

50 sec

Here are real numbers for human nerve fibers. You do not need to memorize the letters. Learn the two ends. The largest myelinated fibers, the ones that run your skeletal muscles, conduct at up to about 120 meters per second. The small unmyelinated C fibers, which carry slow aching pain, conduct at about half a meter to two meters per second. That is walking speed.

Look at the third row. A delta fibers are thin, but they have myelin, and they are still about ten times faster than C fibers. So when you rank axons, myelin comes first, and then diameter. Try the ranking question in the box.

14

From your toe to your spinal cord

65 sec

Let us use those numbers. You stub your toe. It is about one meter from your toe to your spinal cord. Time equals distance divided by velocity. In a large myelinated fiber at 100 meters per second, one meter takes one hundredth of a second, which is 10 milliseconds. In a C fiber at one meter per second, it takes one whole second. A hundred times longer.

You have felt this. Pain from the toe travels in two kinds of fiber. The A delta fibers, at around 20 meters per second, get there in about 50 milliseconds, and the C fibers take about a second. So you feel a sharp pain right away, and then the dull ache arrives. That is called first pain and second pain. Both signals left your toe at the same moment.

DoOpen Show the work and go through it.
DrawWrite large: 1 m / 100 m per s = 10 ms. Under it: 1 m / 1 m per s = 1 s. In rust write: first pain 50 ms, second pain 1 s.

16

Remove the myelin from part of an axon

85 sec

Now we change the conditions, and you predict. First, remove the myelin from part of an axon, which is what happens in multiple sclerosis.

Follow the current arrows from the left. Under healthy myelin the arrow is thick. Where the myelin is gone, the gray arrows show current leaking out through the bare membrane, so the arrow reaching the next node is thinner. That node takes longer to get to threshold, so conduction slows down. And if too little arrives, it never gets to threshold, and conduction stops right there.

Now predict. A person with multiple sclerosis takes a hot bath and their temperature goes up half a degree Celsius. Vision blurs and the legs feel weak, and an hour after cooling down they are back to normal. What happened? Warm channels work faster. The sodium channels inactivate sooner, each action potential is a little briefer, and a little less current goes on to the next node. A healthy node gets several times more current than it needs. A node past a bare stretch was barely getting enough, so now it fails. Cool down, and it works again.

DrawIn navy trace the thick arrows. In rust trace the leak arrows out of the bare membrane and write: leak. At the node after the bare stretch write: late, or never.

17

Change the potassium in the blood

85 sec

Second change: potassium in the blood, which is the outside potassium for every cell. You did the hard part in the resting potential lecture. When it goes up to 7, the resting potential moves from about minus 70 to about minus 64. When it drops to 2.5, rest moves to about minus 76. Threshold stays at about minus 55. So what changes is the distance between rest and threshold.

Low potassium first. The gap grows from 15 millivolts to 21. A graded potential that used to reach threshold now falls short, so neurons and muscles are harder to fire, and the patient is weak.

High potassium has two stages. The gap shrinks to 9 millivolts. At first, cells fire more easily. But now think about the sodium channel. A membrane that sits at a depolarized voltage holds some of its sodium channels in the inactivated state, and an inactivated channel cannot open. With too few ready channels, the cell cannot fire at all. So high potassium gives you more excitable first, then less excitable, and the thing that changed in between is the number of available sodium channels.

DrawIn rust, bracket the three gaps and write 21, 15 and 9.
DrawBeside the hyperkalemia row write in navy: 1. closer to threshold. 2. Na channels inactivate. 3. cannot fire.

18

Change the calcium in the blood

70 sec

Third change: calcium. Calcium ions in the fluid outside the cell bind to the outside of the sodium channel, and while they are there the channel is harder to open. So calcium outside the cell steadies the membrane.

When blood calcium is low, sodium channels open too easily. In the top row, threshold has moved toward the resting potential, and nerves start to fire on their own. The person gets tingling around the mouth and in the fingers, then cramps, then muscles that go into spasm. That is called tetany. High calcium does the reverse. Channels are harder to open, and nerves and muscles are sluggish.

Students always ask: muscle needs calcium to contract, so why does low calcium cause spasm and not weakness? Because the calcium a muscle uses to contract is stored inside the muscle cell. Low blood calcium hardly touches that store. What it changes first is the nerve.

DrawIn rust draw an arrow on the top row from the gray line to the threshold line and write: closer to rest, fires on its own.

19

Block the sodium channel, or lower the sodium outside

80 sec

Fourth change. There are two ways to interfere with sodium, and they do different things. On the left, a normal action potential. On the right, the axon is sitting in fluid with half the normal sodium. It still fires, but the peak is lower. In the middle, the sodium channels are blocked by a local anesthetic. There is no action potential at all. The stimulus makes a small bump, and it dies away.

Why the difference? With low sodium, the Nernst equation with 72.5 outside and 15 inside gives plus 42 millivolts, down from plus 61. The peak of an action potential follows the sodium equilibrium potential, so the peak comes down by about the same amount. But the channels still open, and each patch still fires the next. The message still gets through.

With a blocker, the channels cannot open, so there is no action potential and nothing to regenerate. Lidocaine reaches the channel from inside the cell. Tetrodotoxin, from last time, plugs it from the outside.

20

Where the message stopped

90 sec

Back to the dentist's chair. The pain receptors in the tooth work normally, and action potentials start and travel toward the brainstem. Then they reach the stretch of nerve that is soaking in lidocaine. In that stretch the sodium channels cannot open. Local current flows in from the last working patch, but with no channels to open it fades in a few millimeters. Nothing is regenerated, nothing reaches the brain, and there is no pain.

Now, why pain and not pressure? To stop a myelinated axon, the drug has to knock out about three nodes in a row. Pain travels in thin fibers, and in thin fibers the nodes are close together, so a short stretch of drug covers three easily. Pressure travels in thick fibers, and their nodes are a millimeter or two apart. The same stretch of drug may not cover enough of them. So pain goes first, and pressure and movement go last. That is a simplified rule, but it is the usual one.

And it all comes back in a few hours, because nothing was damaged. The drug diffuses away and the channels work again. The box on the right lays this out as an experiment.
