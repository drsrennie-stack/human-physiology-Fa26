Build the Molecular Toolkit

Your body has one job. Keep the conditions inside you steady enough that your cells can keep working, no matter what is happening outside you or to you.

Last week was about control, how the body senses a change and sends the order to fix it. This week asks what carries those orders out, and then asks the question that runs the rest of the semester: how does the body turn that machinery up and down?

The question for this week

Everything the body does, it does with molecules that stick to other molecules, change shape, and let go again. So what decides whether two molecules stick, how tightly they stick, and what can change that?

The claim on the table

A 24 year old is brought to the emergency department after collapsing during a road race on a hot afternoon. Core temperature is 41.6 °C (106.9 °F). He is confused, his muscles are rigid, and his blood work shows enzymes leaking out of muscle cells into the blood.

The physician on the case tells the family:

"The heat itself is doing the damage. His proteins are coming apart, and that is why his muscles and his brain are not working."

Do not agree or disagree yet. Your job across this week is to learn the mechanism well enough to decide whether the evidence supports that claim, and to name the one test result that would settle it.

Two questions to carry with you. What would have to be true about proteins for the claim to be right? And what else, other than heat, could produce this same picture?

Shape is function

This is the idea the rest of the semester sits on. A protein works because it has a pocket, a groove or a surface that fits one particular partner. Change the shape and you change what fits.

  1. The amino acid sequence is set by DNA and held by covalent bonds, so it does not change.
  2. The chain folds, driven by water pushing nonpolar groups inward, and held by many weak interactions.
  3. The fold creates a binding site with a particular size, shape and pattern of charges.
  4. Only molecules that match that site bind well.
  5. Change the fold and the site changes with it. That is the lever, and almost everything this week is a way of pulling it.

Predict, then check

Every weak interaction holding the fold is easy to break. So why does a protein not simply fall apart at body temperature? Decide before you open this.

Because there are thousands of them at once, constantly breaking and reforming, and any one of them breaking costs nothing while the rest still hold. That also tells you the failure mode. Add enough energy, or change enough charges, and you break enough of them at the same moment that the fold cannot recover. Hold that thought for the fever case.

What breaks a fold

When enough weak interactions give way at once, the protein is . The amino acid chain is still intact and still correct. The binding site is gone.

Heat

Added energy makes the chain vibrate until the weak interactions cannot hold. Most human proteins are stable at 37 °C (98.6 °F) and begin losing function above roughly 40 to 41 °C (104 to 105.8 °F).

pH change

Hydrogen ions change the charges on amino acid side chains, so ionic attractions that were holding the fold stop pulling. No heat required.

Certain chemicals

Heavy metals and some solvents disrupt the fold directly. This is a large part of why they are poisons.

Mild denaturation sometimes reverses. Often it does not, and a cooked egg white is the version of that you can see on a plate.

NursingCooling a patient with a dangerously high fever is not comfort care, it is protecting enzyme and structural proteins that stop working above roughly 40 to 41 °C (104 to 105.8 °F).

What this means: bringing the temperature down protects the body's working parts from being wrecked by heat.

Radiologic technologyRadiofrequency and ultrasound ablation treat tumors by heating tissue until its proteins denature, so the treatment is this mechanism used on purpose.

What this means: some treatments deliberately cook a small area of tissue so it stops working.

MedicineCardiac troponin measured in blood is a structural protein released from injured heart muscle, and its rise and fall over hours is how a heart attack is confirmed.

What this means: when heart muscle cells are damaged, their parts leak into the blood where a test can find them.

Respiratory therapyInhaled gas is warmed and humidified before it reaches the airway, because dry cold gas injures the cells and the protein layer lining it.

What this means: the air going into the lungs has to be warm and wet or it damages the lining.

Ligands and binding sites

A is whatever binds to the protein. That is the whole definition, and it covers more than students expect.

Same idea, four names

  • An enzyme binds its substrate.
  • A receptor binds its hormone or neurotransmitter.
  • Hemoglobin binds oxygen.
  • A transporter binds the solute it is about to move.

Four different chapters of the textbook, one mechanism underneath.

What happens after binding

Binding is not the response. Binding is the trigger. What follows depends on the protein: a reaction is catalyzed, a channel opens, a cell changes what it is doing, or a passenger gets carried somewhere.

Keep those two separate in your head. Knowing what binds tells you nothing yet about what happens next.

Four properties describe how binding behaves, and the next four slides take them one at a time. Once you have them you can predict most of what a drug or a hormone will do.

Property 1: specificity, how choosy

is how narrow the range of ligands a protein will accept. High specificity means it binds one molecule and refuses close relatives. Lower specificity means it accepts a family of similar molecules.

  1. The fold creates a site with a particular size, shape and pattern of charges.
  2. A ligand has to physically fit the space and match those charges.
  3. The better the match, the more of the weak interactions can form at once.
  4. Molecules that do not match cannot make enough contacts to stay.

Why lower specificity is sometimes the design

Liver enzymes that clear drugs are deliberately broad. A liver that only recognized molecules the body makes would be no use against anything you swallowed. Broad specificity is also why two drugs cleared by the same enzyme can interfere with each other, which comes back in the competition slide.

Property 2: affinity, how tightly

is how strongly the protein holds the ligand once it lands. High affinity means it binds readily and lets go slowly. Low affinity means it releases easily.

Affinity is a dial, not a switch

This is the part worth slowing down on. Specificity is largely fixed by the fold. Affinity can be changed on the spot, without building a new protein, and the body changes it constantly.

  1. Hemoglobin picks up oxygen in the lung, where affinity is high.
  2. It travels to working muscle, where carbon dioxide is higher, pH is lower and temperature is higher.
  3. Those conditions change hemoglobin's shape slightly, which lowers its affinity for oxygen.
  4. It releases oxygen exactly where the demand is, then returns to the lung and the conditions reverse.

Nothing was replaced and no signal was sent. The tissue that needed oxygen created the conditions that made hemoglobin let go of it. You will build this into the oxygen dissociation curve in Week 13.

Property 3: saturation, when more stops helping

There is a fixed number of binding sites. Add ligand and more sites fill, so the rate of whatever the protein does climbs. Keep adding, and eventually every site is occupied. is the point where more ligand changes nothing.

Predict, then check

A patient's pain is not controlled. The dose of their medication is doubled and nothing improves, but side effects get worse. Using saturation, explain both halves of that result.

The target receptors were already saturated, so the extra drug had nowhere to bind and could not add any effect. The extra drug is still circulating, though, and it binds other proteins elsewhere with lower affinity, which is where the added side effects come from. Saturation is the reason "more" stops being an answer, and it is the reason a second drug with a different target is often the move.

Property 4: competition, two molecules one site

happens when two different ligands both fit the same site. Whichever is present in greater amount, or binds with higher affinity, takes more of the sites. The loser is shut out even though nothing is wrong with it.

Both factors matter, which is the useful part

A ligand with lower affinity can still win if there is far more of it. A ligand with much higher affinity can win even at low concentration. Knowing that is what lets you predict whether flooding the system will work as a treatment.

Predict, then check

Carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin at the same site oxygen uses, with far greater affinity. A patient is pulled from a fire. Why is giving 100 percent oxygen the treatment, and why does it take time?

Because competition depends on concentration as well as affinity. Carbon monoxide wins on affinity by a wide margin, so the only lever left is to raise oxygen concentration far enough that oxygen starts taking the sites back as carbon monoxide molecules release. It takes time because you are overcoming an affinity difference with sheer numbers, which is slow. Raising the pressure as well, in a hyperbaric chamber, is the same strategy pushed harder.

NursingNaloxone reverses an opioid overdose by competing for the same receptor, and its shorter duration is why the patient can slide back once it wears off.

What this means: the reversal drug pushes the opioid off its parking spot, but it leaves sooner than the opioid does.

Radiologic technologyA pulse oximeter reads carbon monoxide bound hemoglobin as though it were oxygen, so a normal reading does not rule out poisoning and a different test is needed.

What this means: the finger clip can be fooled, because it cannot tell the two apart.

MedicineTwo drugs cleared by the same liver enzyme compete for it, so adding the second raises the blood level of the first, which is a large share of drug interaction warnings.

What this means: two medicines can get stuck in line for the same exit, so one of them builds up.

Respiratory therapyIn carbon monoxide poisoning you deliver the highest oxygen concentration available, because concentration is the only lever that competes against that affinity.

What this means: flooding the lungs with oxygen is how you win back the spots carbon monoxide took.

Modulation: the two routes

A is anything that changes how well a protein binds or how well it works. There are only two broad routes, and every specific mechanism you will meet is one or the other.

Route 1: change the protein you have

Leave the number of copies alone and alter the ones already there, by blocking the site, changing the shape, or attaching something to it.

Speed: seconds to minutes.

Used when the body needs a fast answer: a heartbeat, a reflex, a breath.

Route 2: change how many copies exist

Build more of the protein, or destroy the existing ones faster. This runs through transcription and translation.

Speed: hours to days.

Used when the change needs to last: adapting to altitude, to training, to a chronically high hormone level.

The question to ask every time from here on: how fast does this response need to be? That alone usually tells you which route the body took.

Lever 1: competitive inhibition

A molecule that fits the active site sits in it and blocks the real ligand. Nothing about the protein has changed. The parking space is simply occupied.

  1. The inhibitor binds the same site the normal ligand uses.
  2. While it sits there, the normal ligand cannot bind and the protein does nothing.
  3. The inhibitor eventually releases, because binding is reversible.
  4. Raise the normal ligand concentration far enough and it wins the site back, so the block is beatable.

The example to hold on to

A person swallows methanol. Their body converts it, using the same enzyme that handles drinking alcohol, into a product that causes blindness and organ damage. The treatment is to give ethanol, or a drug that works the same way.

Ethanol competes for the same enzyme and the enzyme prefers it. Filling the sites with ethanol means the methanol is never converted, so the toxic product is never made, and the methanol leaves the body unchanged. Notice what the treatment did not do. It did not destroy anything, repair anything, or speed anything up. It occupied binding sites, and that was enough.

Lever 2: allosteric modulation

An binds somewhere else on the protein entirely, and changes the shape of the active site from a distance. This is the one students find hardest and it is the one worth the most.

  1. The modulator binds a separate site, not the active site.
  2. Binding changes the protein's shape.
  3. That shape change reaches the active site and alters it.
  4. The active site now binds its ligand better, or worse, depending on the modulator.

Why it cannot be outcompeted

Adding more of the normal ligand does not help here, because the modulator is not in the way. It is in a different place, changing the shape of the space the ligand is trying to enter. That difference is the whole reason the distinction between competitive and allosteric is clinically useful rather than a piece of vocabulary.

Predict, then check

Carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions bind hemoglobin away from the oxygen sites and lower its affinity for oxygen. Working muscle produces both. What does that accomplish, and why is it better than sending a signal?

The tissue working hardest produces the most carbon dioxide and acid, so it automatically makes hemoglobin release more oxygen exactly where it is needed, in proportion to how hard that tissue is working. No sensor, no nerve, no hormone, no delay. The demand itself is the signal. That is allosteric modulation solving a delivery problem more elegantly than any control system could.

NursingA febrile, acidotic patient unloads oxygen more readily at the tissue, which changes how you read a saturation number against how the patient actually looks.

What this means: fever and acid change how easily blood hands oxygen over, so the number and the patient can disagree.

Radiologic technologySome contrast reactions and premedication strategies work by changing binding at sites other than the one causing the problem, which is the same idea applied to a different protein.

What this means: a medicine can change how something else behaves without touching it directly.

MedicineBenzodiazepines do not open the chloride channel themselves. They bind elsewhere and make the channel respond more strongly to the body's own signal, which is why they have a ceiling effect.

What this means: the drug turns up the body's own volume knob rather than pressing the button itself.

Respiratory therapyStored blood loses the molecule that normally lowers hemoglobin's oxygen affinity, so older transfused blood holds onto oxygen more tightly at first.

What this means: blood that has been sitting in storage does not hand over oxygen as easily right away.

Lever 3: covalent modulation

The cell attaches a chemical group to the protein, almost always a phosphate group taken from ATP. The group changes the protein's shape, which switches its activity on or off.

Adding the phosphate

An enzyme called a kinase takes a phosphate from ATP and attaches it to the target protein. The phosphate carries charge, so it changes the local charge pattern and the fold shifts.

Removing it

An enzyme called a phosphatase takes the phosphate back off, returning the protein to its previous state. The switch is fully reversible, and the cell controls both directions.

Why the body uses this one so much

  • It is fast, in seconds.
  • It is reversible, and the reversal is also controlled.
  • It works on proteins that already exist, so nothing has to be built.
  • One hormone arriving at the surface can trigger a cascade that phosphorylates thousands of target proteins, which is how a small signal produces a large response.

That last point is the bridge into Week 8. When we get to hormone signaling, nearly every pathway ends in something being phosphorylated.

Levers 4 and 5: permanent changes

Irreversible inhibition

The inhibitor binds and does not let go. That protein molecule is finished, and the only way back is for the cell to build a new one, which takes hours to days.

  • Organophosphate pesticides and nerve agents permanently block acetylcholinesterase, so acetylcholine is never cleared from the synapse.
  • Aspirin permanently blocks cyclooxygenase in platelets, and platelets cannot make new protein, so the effect lasts the life of the platelet.

This is why aspirin is stopped days before surgery rather than hours.

Proteolytic activation

The protein is built in an inactive form, then a piece is cut off to reveal the working site. The cut cannot be undone.

  • Pepsinogen is cut to pepsin only once it reaches stomach acid.
  • Clotting factors sit inactive in the blood until the cascade cuts them.

It looks inefficient until you ask the design question: how do you store a protein whose job is to destroy things, inside the cell that made it?

Both of these trade reversibility for something. Irreversible inhibition trades it for a block that cannot be overcome. Proteolytic activation trades it for safe storage and instant readiness.

Lever 6: pH and temperature

These two are modulators whether the body wants them to be or not. Every protein in you is being modulated by the pH and the temperature it sits in, continuously.

  1. Hydrogen ions change the charges on amino acid side chains, and temperature changes how much the chain moves.
  2. Both change the fold, and therefore the shape of the binding site.
  3. Small changes shift how well the protein works. Large changes destroy it.
  4. Every protein has a range where its shape is right, and that range is different for different proteins.

This is also the reason the whole body has to defend its pH so tightly. A pH problem is not one protein failing. It is every protein in the body being modulated at once.

Lever 7: up and down regulation

This is Route 2, the slow one. Instead of changing the protein, the cell changes how many copies of it exist.

  1. A signal reaches the cell nucleus and changes how much that gene is transcribed.
  2. More messenger RNA is made, or less.
  3. Translation produces more copies of the protein, or fewer.
  4. The cell can also change how fast existing copies are broken down, which moves the number the other way.
  5. The cell's response to that signal is now stronger or weaker, and it stays that way for hours to days.

Predict, then check

A person is exposed to a constantly high level of one hormone for weeks. Predict what happens to the number of receptors on their target cells, and what happens if the hormone level then suddenly drops to normal.

The cells down regulate, reducing receptor numbers, so the same hormone level produces a smaller response. That is the cell protecting itself from constant stimulation. If the hormone then drops to normal, the response is now far too small for that level, and the person feels the deficiency even though the hormone value looks fine. Rebuilding receptors takes time, which is why some medications are tapered rather than stopped. You will meet this again in Week 8.

NursingLong term steroids are tapered rather than stopped, because the body has adjusted to the outside supply and needs time to build its own production back up.

What this means: stopping a long term medicine suddenly can leave the body with nothing, because it stopped making its own.

Radiologic technologyA patient who has been on long term medication may respond differently to sedation or contrast than the dose chart predicts, because their receptor numbers have shifted.

What this means: someone on a medicine for years may not react the way the standard dose expects.

MedicineTolerance to a medication is often receptor down regulation, so the dose that worked in month one no longer works in month six.

What this means: the body adjusts, so the same dose stops having the same effect.

Respiratory therapyFrequent use of a rescue inhaler can blunt the response to it over time, which is part of why a worsening reliever requirement is treated as a warning sign.

What this means: using the rescue inhaler constantly makes it work less well, so needing it more is a red flag.

Predict, then check

In this week's lab you will run amylase at 20, 37 and 60 °C (68, 98.6 and 140 °F). Sketch the rate curve in your head, then say what happens when you cool the 60 °C tube back to 37 °C.

Low at 20 °C (68 °F) because molecules move slowly, highest near 37 °C (98.6 °F), very low at 60 °C (140 °F) because the enzyme has denatured. Cooling the hot tube back down does not restore activity, because the fold is gone and the binding site with it. That is the difference between slow and broken, and the cooling step is the only part of the experiment that can tell them apart.

Predict, then check

Muscle tissue runs out of ATP. Predict what happens to the muscle, and be careful, because the obvious answer is the wrong one.

It goes rigid rather than limp. Contraction needs ATP, but so does relaxation, because releasing the grip between the contractile proteins requires ATP to bind first. With no ATP the proteins stay locked together. This is the mechanism behind rigor mortis, and it is one of the things worth keeping in mind when we return to the runner with rigid muscles.

Buffers: the body's answer to an acid load

You already know what a buffer is chemically. Here is the physiology. Your cells produce acid every second of every day, and blood pH still holds between 7.35 and 7.45. That is not luck, it is a three part defense.

CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3−

CO2
carbon dioxide, made constantly by every working cell
HCO3−
bicarbonate, the base that takes up hydrogen ions
⇌
runs both ways, shifting toward whichever side relieves the change
  1. Seconds. Acid is added, bicarbonate binds the hydrogen ions, and the reaction shifts left. pH barely moves. This buys time and nothing else.
  2. Minutes. The lungs change breathing rate and depth to blow off more carbon dioxide, pulling the reaction further left.
  3. Hours to days. The kidneys adjust how much bicarbonate is kept and how much acid is excreted, restoring the sponge itself.

A buffer does not prevent a pH change. It slows one down while two organ systems do the real work. That is also why bicarbonate measures low in someone producing large amounts of acid: the sponge has been used up.

NursingA blood gas gives pH, carbon dioxide and bicarbonate together, and reading all three tells you whether the problem started in the lungs or somewhere else.

What this means: one blood test shows both how acidic the blood is and which system is causing it.

Radiologic technologyA patient breathing rapidly during a scan may be compensating for an acid problem, so that breathing pattern is a finding rather than only a motion artifact.

What this means: fast breathing during a scan can be the body fixing its acid level, not just nerves.

MedicineIn diabetic ketoacidosis the acid load overwhelms the buffer, bicarbonate measures low, and the patient breathes deeply and rapidly to blow off carbon dioxide.

What this means: the body makes too much acid, uses up its sponge, then breathes hard to get rid of the rest.

Respiratory therapyChanging rate or tidal volume on a ventilator changes carbon dioxide clearance, which moves blood pH directly and within minutes.

What this means: turning the ventilator up or down changes the acid level in the blood.

Predict before you check

Answer each one in your head first. No typing. Then open it and compare your reasoning, not only your conclusion.

1. Which lever is this?

A drug lowers an enzyme's activity. Giving far more substrate restores the rate completely. Name the lever and say how you know.

Competitive inhibition. Being beatable by more substrate is the signature, and only a competitive inhibitor has it, because only a competitive inhibitor is physically in the substrate's way. An allosteric inhibitor changed the shape of the site, so flooding with substrate does nothing.

2. How fast does it need to be?

Blood pressure drops when you stand up, and it is corrected within seconds. Which modulation route is the body using, and which one is it definitely not using?

Route 1, changing proteins that already exist, most likely through covalent modulation and allosteric effects downstream of a receptor. It is definitely not up regulation, because building new protein takes hours and you would have fainted long before it arrived. Timing alone rules out an entire category, which is a shortcut worth keeping.

3. The same property, two directions

A patient's medication has high affinity for its target receptor. Name one way that helps them and one way it causes a problem.

It helps because a low dose fills the sites and the effect lasts, since the drug releases slowly. It causes a problem because the body's own signal is outcompeted at that receptor, and because if the dose is too high or a reversal is needed, that same slow release is what makes the effect hard to undo quickly. High affinity is not automatically better. It is a trade.

4. Why the body defends pH so hard

Explain why a pH problem produces symptoms in nearly every organ system at once, rather than in one place.

Because pH is a modulator of every protein in the body at the same time. Hydrogen ions change charges on amino acid side chains everywhere, so enzymes, transporters and receptors in every tissue are all shifted at once. There is no single failing organ to point at. This is also why acid and base balance gets two full weeks later in the course.

Back to the claim

The runner, core temperature 41.6 °C (106.9 °F), confused, muscles rigid, muscle enzymes in the blood. The claim was that heat itself is denaturing his proteins and that is why his muscles and brain are failing.

What supports the claim

  • His temperature is above the range where human proteins begin losing function, around 40 to 41 °C (104 to 105.8 °F).
  • Muscle enzymes in the blood mean muscle cells have been damaged and their contents have leaked out.
  • Confusion fits with neurons whose transporters and enzymes are not working normally.

What this week taught you to question

  • Heat is one modulator of protein shape. Damaged muscle releases acid, and falling pH modulates every protein in the body by a different route. Get a blood gas.
  • Damaged muscle also releases potassium, which disturbs electrical signaling without changing any protein's shape at all. Get an electrolyte panel.
  • Dehydration reduces blood flow, so tissue may be short of oxygen, which stops ATP production. No ATP means muscle cannot relax, and that alone produces rigidity. The rigidity may not be a protein shape problem at all.
  • Confusion has other causes worth excluding, including low blood glucose.

Your decision

Write your answer as a claim, the evidence you are leaning on, and the one test result that would most change your mind. Then say which mechanism you would treat first and why.

The honest answer for this case is that heat is clearly part of it and is the thing you can act on immediately, but at least three mechanisms from this week are running at once, and one of them, the ATP one, is not a denaturation story at all.

That is the pattern for the whole course. A claim is put on the table, you learn the mechanism, and then you decide whether the evidence carries the claim.
