Water and solutions

Why water dissolves some things and not others

Mechanism

Water is a polar molecule. Oxygen pulls the shared electrons toward itself, so the oxygen end carries a slight negative charge and the two hydrogen ends carry a slight positive charge.

  • Those partial charges let water molecules cling to each other through hydrogen bonds, and cling to anything else that carries a charge.
  • Dissolving is exactly that clinging. Water surrounds a charged or polar particle with a shell of its own molecules and carries it away from its neighbors.
  • A molecule with no partial charges gives water nothing to hold, so water pushes it aside and it stays clumped together. That is why oil beads up.

Three words you will use all term

  • Hydrophilic. Dissolves easily in water. Polar or charged. Glucose, ions, most hormones.
  • Hydrophobic. Does not dissolve in water. Nonpolar, mostly carbon and hydrogen. Fats, steroid hormones.
  • Amphipathic. Both in one molecule, a charged head and oily tails. Phospholipids are amphipathic, and that is the entire reason a cell membrane exists.

Predict before you look

Two drugs are the same size. One is hydrophilic and one is hydrophobic. Which one crosses a cell membrane easily, and which one needs a carrier protein just to travel in the blood? Answer both in your head before the next slide.

05

Water and solutions

Solubility decides where a molecule can go

Response

The answer to the prediction, and why it matters more than it sounds.

  • The hydrophobic drug slips straight through a membrane, because the middle of a membrane is oily and so is the drug. But it cannot dissolve in plasma on its own, so it travels bound to a carrier protein.
  • The hydrophilic drug dissolves in plasma without help, and then cannot get through a membrane without a protein to carry it.
  • Neither one is better. They are different problems, and the body solves both with proteins.

This is the first appearance of the idea that runs through the whole lecture: when a molecule cannot do something on its own, a protein does it for them.

Nursing

Whether a drug dissolves is why some of them need a certain fluid, a filter, or a line of their own.

Example: Oily preparations separate out or stick to tubing, so the route matters as much as the dose.

Radiologic technology

MRI is not really imaging tissue. It is imaging hydrogen, and nearly all the hydrogen in a person sits in water and fat.

Example: Two soft tissues look different because they hold different amounts of water and fat, which is why MRI separates soft tissues that an X-ray cannot tell apart.

Medicine

Whether a molecule likes water or likes oil tells you where in the body it can reach.

Example: A fat soluble drug crosses into the brain readily and a water soluble one largely does not, which is why two similar drugs can differ completely in how sleepy they make someone.

Respiratory therapy

An inhaled drug has to survive as droplets the whole way down and still reach the small airways.

Example: Particle size and humidity change how much of a nebulized dose gets past the upper airway at all.

06

Water and solutions

Saying how much is in there

Mechanism

Molarity (M) = moles of solute ÷ liters of solution

Units: mol/L, written M. Body fluids are dilute, so values are usually mmol/L, written mM.

What it says: molarity counts particles, not weight. Equal weights of two different solutes are not equal numbers of molecules, and the body responds to the number of molecules.

Equivalents (Eq) = moles × the charge on the ion

Units: mEq/L for ions in blood. Na+ carries 1 charge, Ca2+ carries 2.

What it says: one mole of calcium carries twice the charge of one mole of sodium. Body fluids have to stay electrically neutral, so charge matters as much as particle count.

Reading a lab report

  • A deciliter, dL, is 100 mL. Glucose, calcium and cholesterol are usually reported in mg/dL in the United States.
  • Sodium is reported around 140 mM, which is 140 millimoles in every liter of plasma.
  • The same substance can be reported three different ways. When a number looks strange, check the unit before you check the physiology.

Where moles come from, and how to convert grams to moles, is on the chemistry review page. You will read these units on a chart; you will not be asked to convert between them.

The rule you are applying

Water can only hold onto something that carries charge, whether that is a full charge like an ion or a partial charge like the oxygen end of a hydroxyl group. If a molecule has none of that anywhere on it, water has nothing to grab, and the molecule stays clumped together with its own kind.

Six out of six means you have the rule. Oxygen is the most common miss, and it is the most useful one to get right, because it is the reason you have hemoglobin at all.

09

pH and buffers

A window the body defends harder than any other

Mechanism
  • An acid releases hydrogen ions into solution. A base takes them out, either by binding them directly or by releasing hydroxide that combines with them to make water.
  • pH is a way of writing the hydrogen ion concentration so the numbers stay manageable. Pure water is pH 7.
  • The scale is logarithmic, and that is the part people miss. Each step is ten times the hydrogen ion concentration of the step beside it. Going from pH 8 to pH 6 is not twice the acid, it is a hundred times the acid.
  • Different compartments sit at very different pH values on purpose. The stomach runs near 1 to 3 because its enzyme needs that; the inside of a cell sits near 7.0 to 7.2.
10

pH and buffers

What the equation is telling you

Mechanism

pH = −log [H+]

Abbreviations: square brackets mean concentration. log is base ten.

What it says: pH runs backwards. More acid gives a lower number. And because it is a log, a change that looks small on the scale is a large change in the acid itself.

[H+] = 10−pH

The same relationship turned around, for going from a pH on a report back to a real concentration.

What it says: normal arterial hydrogen ion concentration is about 40 nanomolar. That is roughly a millionth of the sodium concentration, and it is defended more tightly than anything else in the body.

Why the body cares this much

  • A free hydrogen ion is small and carries a charge, so it can join in hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds anywhere it lands.
  • Enough of them change which R groups on a protein carry a charge.
  • Change the charges and you change which weak bonds hold the protein in its shape.
  • Change the shape and you change the job. That is the whole mechanism, and it is the reason pH gets its own competency.

Hold onto that four-step chain. It is the bridge between this section and the protein section, and it is most of the answer to the opening problem.

12

pH and buffers

What a buffer actually does

Mechanism

A buffer is a pair of molecules that work as a sponge for hydrogen ions. One half of the pair soaks them up when there are too many, and the other half releases them when there are too few.

CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3−

Read it both ways. The double arrows mean this reaction runs in either direction depending on what the body needs.

What it says: add acid and the reaction runs left, because the bicarbonate on the right mops up the extra hydrogen ions and turns them into carbon dioxide and water. The pH still falls, but far less than it would have.

Why this one, out of all the buffers

  • It is everywhere. Bicarbonate is the main buffer in blood at 22 to 26 mmol/L.
  • Both ends are adjustable, and that is what makes it special. The lungs control the carbon dioxide end and can change it within minutes. The kidneys control the bicarbonate end over hours to days.
  • So the same chemical equation is wired to two different organs running on two different timescales. You will build the fast lever in Week 13 and the slow one in Week 15.

Look back at the patient on slide 2 of this deck. Their bicarbonate is 12, well under the normal 22 to 26. That number is not the problem. It is the evidence that the sponge has been used up.

13

Proteins

Twenty building blocks, and one variable part

Mechanism
  • Twenty amino acids build human proteins. Nine are essential, which means the body cannot make them and you have to eat them.
  • The R group is where the personality lives. Some R groups carry a charge, some are polar, some are oily, and one contains sulfur.
  • Which R groups sit where along the chain decides how that chain will fold, and the fold decides what the protein can do.
  • A peptide bond joins one amino acid to the next and releases a water molecule. It is covalent and strong, so a protein chain does not come apart on its own.

Two to nine amino acids is an oligopeptide, ten to a hundred a polypeptide, more than a hundred a protein. Insulin has 51, which is why you will see it called both.

14

Proteins

Four levels of structure

Mechanism

Notice which bonds hold which level. Only the primary structure is held by strong covalent bonds. Levels two, three and four are held mostly by weak bonds, and that is deliberate, because it lets a protein flex when something binds to it. It also means heat and pH can undo the fold without touching the chain.

15

Proteins

The shape is the job

Mechanism
  • Denaturation is losing the shape while keeping the chain. Same amino acids in the same order, wrong fold, no function.
  • Heat does it by shaking the weak bonds apart. pH does it by changing which R groups carry a charge, so the ionic bonds that held the fold either break or form where they should not.
  • A mild disturbance can be undone and the protein refolds. A severe one usually cannot. A cooked egg white never goes clear again.
  • The one covalent bond that helps hold a fold is the disulfide bond, sulfur to sulfur between two cysteines. A permanent wave works by breaking and re-forming them in hair.

Predict before you look

Go back to the four-step chain from slide 9: acid changes charges, charges change bonds, bonds change shape, shape changes the job. Our patient is at pH 7.18. Which step of that chain is already happening in them right now?

16

Proteins

Binding is how a protein does anything

Mechanism
  • A ligand is anything that binds to a protein: a substrate, a hormone, a drug, an ion.
  • Specificity means a protein binds one ligand or a small family of very similar ones, because the site and the ligand have to match in both shape and charge.
  • Affinity is how tightly it holds on once bound. Specificity is which one; affinity is how hard.
  • Binding is reversible. At any moment some pairs are forming and others are coming apart, and that is the only reason a signal can ever be switched off.

Every protein job underneath is the same three steps. Something binds, the protein changes shape, and the shape change does the work. Enzymes, transporters, receptors and motors are all that one mechanism wearing different clothes.

17

Enzymes

What an enzyme is, and what it is not

Mechanism
  • An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a reaction without being used up by it. One enzyme molecule can turn over thousands of substrate molecules a second.
  • It works by holding the substrates in the right position and straining the bonds that need to break, which lowers the activation energy, the hill every reaction has to climb before it can happen.
  • What an enzyme does not do: it does not make a reaction release more energy, it does not make an impossible reaction possible, and it does not decide the direction.
  • Without enzymes most reactions in your body would still happen, just far too slowly to keep you alive. Enzymes are about rate, and rate is what physiology is made of.

Enzymes

How fast, and what sets the ceiling

Mechanism
  • Vmax is the fastest this reaction can go with the enzyme that is present. At that point every binding site is occupied and the enzyme is saturated.
  • Km is a measure of grip. It is the substrate concentration that fills half the sites, so a small Km means the enzyme grabs substrate even when there is very little around.
  • Neither value is something you calculate in this course. What matters is which way the curve moves.

The relationships to carry forward

Work it through 3

Why sugar appears in the urine

Solve

The kidney reclaims glucose from the filtrate using a transporter protein. Transporters saturate exactly the way enzymes do, so figure 3 applies to them too.

  • Part A. At a normal blood glucose of 5 mmol/L the transporters keep up and no glucose reaches the urine. Where on the curve is the kidney operating?
  • Part B. Blood glucose rises to 20 mmol/L and glucose starts appearing in the urine. What changed?
  • Part C. Would giving the kidney more time fix it? Would more glucose? What would?

Solved

  1. Part A. On the steep, rising part, well below saturation. There is spare capacity, so every glucose molecule that arrives finds a free transporter and gets reclaimed.
  2. Part B. Blood glucose climbed past the point where the transporters saturate. Every site is now occupied at all times, so the transporters are moving glucose as fast as they ever can.
  3. The extra glucose arriving has nowhere to bind. It stays in the filtrate and leaves in the urine. Nothing is broken; the system simply hit its ceiling.
  4. Part C, time. No. The transporters are already running at Vmax every second. Slower flow would help a little by giving each molecule more chances, but the ceiling itself does not move.
  5. Part C, more glucose. No, and this is the point of the flat part of the curve. More substrate past saturation changes nothing.
  6. What would work is changing the protein side of the equation: fewer glucose molecules arriving, or more transporters in the membrane. Say it as a rule you can reuse all term: when a response stops rising even though the input keeps rising, look for a saturated protein.
20

Enzymes

Four things that change the rate

Mechanism
  • Substrate concentration. Up to saturation, more substrate means a faster rate. Past it, nothing.
  • Temperature. Warming speeds molecules up so they collide more often, until the weak bonds holding the fold start to go. Past the peak the enzyme denatures and the rate collapses.
  • pH. Each enzyme peaks at the pH of the place it works. Pepsin in the stomach peaks near 2, and would be destroyed at 7.4. Most enzymes in your cells peak near 7.
  • Inhibitors and activators. Two slides from now.

Climbing either slope is reversible: bring the condition back and the enzyme works again. Going over the top and denaturing usually is not.

Example: That is why correcting ventilation can change a patient's chemistry faster than any drug you could give.

22

Enzymes

Turning an enzyme on

Mechanism
  • The word allosteric means other site. Anything that binds away from the working site and changes the fold is allosteric, and it can switch a protein either on or off.

The reason

A digestive enzyme that was active the moment it was made would digest the cell that made it. So the pancreas builds them inactive, ships them out, and they are cut into their working shape only after they arrive in the small intestine. The same logic protects you from clotting inside a healthy vessel: the clotting factors are all sitting there in the blood right now, waiting to be cut.

23

Enzymes

Turning an enzyme off, two different ways

Mechanism
  • A competitive inhibitor is shaped enough like the substrate to sit in the binding site. The two compete for the same spot, so flooding the system with substrate wins the site back.
  • An allosteric inhibitor binds somewhere else and deforms the site. More substrate does not help, because the site itself is the problem.
  • Reversible inhibitors use weak bonds and let go. Irreversible ones bind covalently and the protein is finished until the cell builds a new one.

Work it through 4

Two poisons, two antidotes

Solve

Both of these are real, and the treatment for each follows directly from figure 5. Work out which kind of inhibitor you are dealing with, then say what the antidote has to do.

  • Case A. Carbon monoxide binds the oxygen site on hemoglobin and holds it roughly 200 times more tightly than oxygen does.
  • Case B. Cyanide binds an enzyme in the electron transport chain, away from where its substrate binds, and stops it working.

Decide before you open it

For each one: competitive or allosteric? And can you beat it by adding more of the normal ligand?

Solved

  1. Case A is competitive. Carbon monoxide occupies the exact site oxygen uses, so the two are in direct competition for one spot.
  2. That means mass action can win it back. Flood the patient with 100 percent oxygen, and in severe cases oxygen at high pressure in a chamber, so oxygen molecules vastly outnumber the carbon monoxide at the site.
  3. This is the left panel of figure 5 in real life: Vmax was never lost, you just have to push much further right along the substrate axis to reach it.
  4. Case B is allosteric. Cyanide binds away from the substrate site and deforms the enzyme, so no amount of extra substrate recovers the rate.
  5. The antidote cannot work by competition, so it works by removal instead. The classic treatment gives the body something cyanide binds even more tightly than the enzyme does, pulling it off.
  6. The transferable lesson: identify where the inhibitor binds and you have already narrowed down what the antidote has to do. Competitive, out-compete it. Allosteric, get rid of it.

Enzymes

The slow control: how many enzymes there are

Response
  • Up-regulation is the cell making more copies of a protein, or breaking down fewer. Down-regulation is the reverse.
  • Everything else on the last five slides happens in milliseconds to seconds. This one takes hours to days, because it means transcribing a gene and building protein.
  • So the body has a fast set of controls and a slow set, working on the same enzymes. When you meet hormone signaling in Week 8, this is the lever most of it is pulling.

A worked example you already know: someone who trains regularly builds more mitochondria and more of the enzymes inside them. Nothing about the individual enzyme changed. There is just more of it.

ATP and energy

What the cell actually spends energy on

Mechanism
  • A reaction that releases energy is exergonic. One that needs energy put in is endergonic. On their own, endergonic reactions do not happen.
  • Coupling is the trick the cell uses: run the two together, so the energy released by one pays for the other.
  • ATP is the intermediate that makes coupling possible. Breaking the bond to its last phosphate group releases energy the cell can immediately spend.
  • Every one of the three kinds of work below is something the cell does constantly. Stop making ATP and all three stop within minutes.
27

ATP and energy

ATP is a rechargeable battery, not a fuel tank

Mechanism

This is the single most useful thing to understand about ATP, and it is the part students most often have backwards.

  • You do not store much ATP. An adult holds only about 50 grams of it at any moment, yet turns over something on the order of your whole body weight in ATP across a day.
  • That only works because ATP is recycled continuously. Spend it to ADP, recharge it back to ATP, over and over, thousands of times a day per molecule.
  • So the pathways in the rest of this lecture are not making ATP from nothing. They are recharging ADP back to ATP using energy pulled out of food.

ATP + H2O → ADP + Pᵢ + energy

Abbreviations: ATP adenosine triphosphate, ADP adenosine diphosphate, Pᵢ inorganic phosphate, H2O water.

What it says: this is the spending half. The energy released here is what pays for chemical, transport and mechanical work.

ADP + Pᵢ + energy → ATP + H2O

The same equation run backwards. The energy now has to come from somewhere, and that somewhere is food.

What it says: this is the recharging half, and it is what glycolysis, the citric acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation exist to do.

Predict before you look

If you hold only 50 grams of ATP, how long could you survive if every pathway that recharges it stopped right now? Order of magnitude: days, hours, minutes, or seconds?

29

ATP production

Stage 1, glycolysis

Mechanism

Glycolysis splits one six-carbon glucose into two three-carbon pyruvate molecules. It happens in the cytosol and it does not need oxygen.

  • It costs 2 ATP to get started and makes 4, so the net gain is 2 ATP. The investment at the front is why a cell that is already out of ATP is in real trouble.
  • It also makes 2 NADH, which are electron carriers. Hold onto those, they are worth far more later.
  • It is the only stage that runs without oxygen, and that single fact is what makes it the emergency route.

Why every cell keeps this pathway

  • It is fast, and it works anywhere in the cell without needing a mitochondrion at all.
  • A mature red blood cell has no mitochondria, so glycolysis is the only way it makes ATP for its entire life.
  • Skeletal muscle sprinting flat out outruns its oxygen supply and leans on glycolysis for the same reason.

The catch

Glycolysis needs NAD+ to accept electrons at one of its steps. Every time it runs, it turns NAD+ into NADH, using up the supply. If the NADH is not emptied out again, glycolysis stops within seconds. With oxygen, the mitochondrion empties it. Without oxygen, the cell has to find another way, and that is exactly what lactate is for. Slide 37.

30

ATP production

Stages 2 and 3, into the mitochondrion

Mechanism

Stage 2. Pyruvate to acetyl CoA

  • Pyruvate crosses into the mitochondrial matrix and loses a carbon as carbon dioxide.
  • Makes 2 NADH per glucose. Makes no ATP at all.
  • This is a one-way link reaction, not a cycle. Once carbon leaves as CO2, you cannot rebuild glucose from what is left, which is why fat cannot be converted back into glucose in any useful amount.

Stage 3. The citric acid cycle

  • Also in the matrix. It turns twice per glucose, once for each pyruvate.
  • Per glucose it makes 2 ATP, 6 NADH, 2 FADH2 and 4 CO2.
  • Look at what it is really producing. Two ATP is almost nothing. The cycle's actual job is to strip electrons off the carbons and load them onto carriers.

Running total so far: 4 ATP made directly, plus 10 NADH and 2 FADH2 waiting to be cashed in. Neither stage 2 nor stage 3 uses oxygen directly, but both stop dead without it, because the carriers they fill have nowhere to unload.

31

ATP production

Stage 4, where the oxygen goes

Mechanism

Oxidative phosphorylation happens on the inner mitochondrial membrane, and it is where almost all of the ATP is actually made.

  • NADH and FADH2 hand their electrons to a chain of proteins in the membrane.
  • As the electrons pass down the chain, the proteins use the energy to pump hydrogen ions out of the matrix, building up a gradient.
  • Those hydrogen ions flow back in through an enzyme called ATP synthase, and that flow is what spins it and makes ATP.
  • At the end of the chain, oxygen accepts the spent electrons and combines with hydrogen to make water.

Predict before you look

Oxygen's entire job in this pathway is to sit at the end and accept electrons. Nothing else. So predict: if oxygen stops arriving, why do stages 2 and 3 stop as well, when neither of them uses oxygen directly?

The chain backs up

With no oxygen at the end, the electrons have nowhere to go, so the chain fills up and stops. The chain is the only thing that empties NADH and FADH2. Once those carriers are all full, stages 2 and 3 have nowhere to put the electrons they strip off, so they stop too. Only glycolysis, which can dump its electrons onto pyruvate instead, keeps running. This is exactly what is happening in the patient on slide 2.

Nursing

Oxygen is a drug with a purpose, and the purpose is this last step in the chain.

Example: A patient can have a normal oxygen saturation on the monitor and still be starved at the tissue, if blood pressure is too low to deliver it. That is why lactate is measured alongside saturation.

Radiologic technology

Nuclear medicine scans that measure metabolism are reading this pathway directly.

Example: A PET scan uses a glucose analogue, so tissue lighting up brightly is tissue consuming glucose fast, which is why tumours show up.

Medicine

Cyanide kills by blocking the very end of this chain while everything upstream is intact.

Example: The patient has plenty of oxygen in the blood and cannot use any of it, which is why their venous blood stays bright red.

Respiratory therapy

This is why a patient who cannot ventilate deteriorates in minutes rather than hours.

Example: There is no meaningful store of ATP to fall back on, so the deficit starts almost immediately.

ATP production

Adding it up, and why your book may disagree

Mechanism

For this course, use 30 to 32 ATP per glucose, and be able to say why the number is not exact. With oxygen and without

Response

Lactate is not a waste product and it is not a poison. Making it is what lets glycolysis keep running when the mitochondrion cannot help, and the liver later converts it back. But it is an acid, and producing a lot of it fast is exactly how our patient's pH ended up at 7.18.

Work it through 5

What the shortfall actually costs

Solve

Use 32 ATP per glucose with oxygen and 2 without.

  • Part A. A cell needs a steady supply of ATP. With oxygen it gets about 32 ATP from each glucose; without oxygen it gets 2. To hold its ATP supply steady, does it need more glucose or less when the oxygen goes?
  • Part B. Roughly how much more? Order of magnitude is all you need.
  • Part C. The patient's blood supply to that tissue is also reduced, so less glucose is arriving, not more. What happens to the cell, and what happens to the lactate?

Solved

  1. Part A. Far more glucose. Each glucose is worth about a sixteenth of what it was worth, so the cell has to burn through many more of them to get the same ATP.
  2. Part B. About 16 times as much, because 32 divided by 2 is 16. That single ratio is the whole reason oxygen matters so much, and it is the only number from this slide worth carrying.
  3. Part C. The demand went up sixteenfold at the exact moment supply went down. The cell cannot meet it, so ATP falls, and the jobs ATP pays for start failing in order of how much they cost.
  4. Meanwhile every one of those 160 glucose molecules per second is producing lactate. That is why the lactate climbed to 8.0, and why lactate is used clinically as a measure of whether tissue is getting enough oxygen.
  5. Say it as a principle: lactate is not measuring the lactate. It is measuring the gap between the ATP a tissue needs and the ATP oxygen is letting it make.

Perturb

The patient, start to finish

Perturb

The causal order

  1. Oxygen delivery fell first, because blood pressure was too low to push blood through the tissue.
  2. The lactate rose second, because the cell fell back on glycolysis and had to convert pyruvate to lactate to keep it running.
  3. The bicarbonate fell third, because it was spent buffering that acid. The low bicarbonate is the receipt, not the cause.
  4. And the pH fell because the buffer ran out before the acid did.

Notice the loop at the bottom of the figure.

Falling pH slows every enzyme in the cell, including the enzymes of glycolysis, which is the one pathway still making any ATP at all. So the cell gets worse at the only thing keeping it alive. That is positive feedback, and positive feedback does not correct itself.

This is why the treatment is not bicarbonate. Giving a buffer treats the number on the report. Restoring blood pressure and oxygen delivery treats the first box in the chain, and everything downstream follows.

Perturb

Predict before you look

Perturbation 1. A different patient has a normal blood pressure and normal lungs, but has taken cyanide. Their tissue oxygen delivery is fine. Which boxes of the chain in figure 10 still happen, and which one does not?

Perturbation 1

Box 1 does not happen: oxygen is being delivered perfectly well. Everything from box 2 onward happens exactly as drawn, because cyanide blocks the end of the electron transport chain, which is the same point of failure that missing oxygen would have caused. Same chain, different entry point. This is why cyanide poisoning produces a severe lactic acidosis in a patient whose oxygen saturation reads normal.

Predict before you look

Perturbation 2. Our original patient also develops a fever of 40 °C (104 °F). Using figure 4, does the fever make the enzyme problem better, worse, or is it unrelated?

Perturbation 2

Worse, and through a second, independent route. The pH is already pulling the folds apart by changing R group charges. The fever is now pushing on the same weak bonds from the other direction, and 40 °C (104 °F) sits just past the peak on the temperature curve, on the falling side. Two different perturbations, one shared mechanism: both are attacking the weak bonds that hold protein shape.
