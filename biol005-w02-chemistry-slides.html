The chemistry that does work in the body

Start

The problem for today

A patient's arterial pH falls from 7.40 to 7.10. Nothing in their DNA changed. No enzyme was destroyed. Yet enzymes all over the body stopped working properly. By the end of this lecture you will be able to explain every step of why.

03

Atoms

Three particles, and only one of them moves

Mechanism
  • Protons decide which element it is. The proton count is the atomic number.
  • Neutrons decide which isotope it is. Change the neutron count and you have the same element with a different mass.
  • Electrons do everything else. Every bond, every ion, every stored packet of energy, and every free radical is an electron story.

Electrons have four jobs in physiology

  • Shared between atoms, they make covalent bonds.
  • Gained or lost, they make ions.
  • Pushed to a higher orbital, they hold energy. That is how ATP and NADH carry it.
  • Left unpaired, they make free radicals, which are reactive and damaging.
04

Atoms

Isotopes and ions

Mechanism
  • An isotope has the same protons and a different number of neutrons. Chemically it behaves the same. Some isotopes are unstable and give off radiation, which is the basis of nuclear medicine.
  • An ion has gained or lost electrons and now carries a charge. Chemically it behaves very differently from the neutral atom, because charge decides what it can bind and where it can go.
  • A hydrogen atom that loses its one electron is just a bare proton. That is the H+ that pH measures.

Nursing

Radioactive tracers are given as a normal molecule carrying an unstable isotope, so the body handles it exactly as it would the ordinary version.

Example: A patient going for a bone scan gets a phosphate compound tagged with technetium. Bone takes up the phosphate as usual, and the isotope reports where it went.

Radiologic technology

Nuclear medicine imaging is built on isotopes. The camera sees the radiation, and the physiology decides where the isotope goes.

Example: Iodine 131 collects in the thyroid because the gland cannot tell it from ordinary iodine.

Medicine

A hydrogen ion is a proton with nothing else attached, which is why it is so small and so reactive.

Example: Acid base disorders are really disorders of free proton concentration, and every buffer in the body exists to soak protons up.

Respiratory therapy

Two isotopes of oxygen behave the same way in the lung, which is what lets researchers trace where oxygen ends up.

Example: Oxygen 18 labelled water is used to measure total energy expenditure in patients.

05

Bonds

Covalent bonds make molecules

Mechanism
  • A covalent bond is two atoms sharing a pair of electrons. It is strong, and it takes energy to make or break.
  • Single, double and triple bonds share one, two or three pairs. A double bond in a fatty acid is a kink in the chain, and that kink is the difference between butter and oil.
  • If the atoms share evenly, the molecule is nonpolar. Mostly carbon and hydrogen.
  • If one atom pulls harder, the molecule is polar. Water is the important one. Oxygen pulls the electrons toward itself, so the oxygen end is slightly negative and the hydrogen ends slightly positive.
06

Bonds

Noncovalent bonds are the reversible ones

Mechanism
  • Ionic bond: opposite charges attract. Sodium hands an electron to chlorine and the two ions stick together as a crystal. Put the crystal in water and they let go.
  • Hydrogen bond: a slightly positive hydrogen on one polar molecule is drawn to a slightly negative oxygen, nitrogen or fluorine on another. One is feeble. Millions of them give water its surface tension and hold the two strands of DNA together.
  • Van der Waals forces: fleeting attractions between any two atoms that get close. Nonspecific, and only matter when two surfaces fit closely.

Why the body runs on weak bonds

A hormone that bound its receptor with covalent bonds would never let go. The signal could never be turned off. Every reversible interaction in physiology, binding, recognition, folding, runs on these weak bonds because weak bonds can be undone.

07

Ions

The ions that run physiology

Mechanism
  • A cation has lost electrons and is positive. An anion has gained electrons and is negative.
  • An electrolyte is anything that separates into ions in water. The name comes from the fact that the solution conducts electricity.
  • Charge is what keeps an ion from crossing the oily middle of a membrane by itself. Every ion that moves in the body moves through a protein. Hold onto that. It generates most of weeks 3, 4 and 5.

Nursing

Potassium matters more than the other electrolytes because the heart's ability to fire depends on it.

Example: Potassium is replaced with monitoring and never pushed fast, because the margin between correcting it and causing an arrhythmia is small.

Radiologic technology

Ionizing radiation is named for exactly this. The beam knocks electrons off atoms in tissue and leaves charged particles behind.

Example: Most of that happens in water, because water is most of the tissue. The reactive fragments go on to damage DNA, and that chain is the reason dose limits and shielding exist.

Medicine

A charged particle cannot get through a membrane by itself. Every ion that moves needs a protein to go through.

Example: A drug that blocks one kind of channel can change the behavior of an entire organ.

Respiratory therapy

Electrolytes decide how hard a muscle can pull, and breathing is muscle work.

Example: Low potassium or low phosphate weakens the diaphragm, which shows up as a failed weaning trial rather than as breathlessness.

Solved problem 1

How heavy is one molecule of glucose?

Solve

Glucose is C6H12O6. Find its molecular mass, then do the same for table salt, NaCl.

Atomic masses you are given
ElementMass, Da
Carbon, C12
Hydrogen, H1
Oxygen, O16
Sodium, Na23
Chlorine, Cl35.5

A dalton (Da) and an atomic mass unit (amu) are the same thing.

Solved

  1. Count each element in the formula. Glucose has 6 carbons, 12 hydrogens and 6 oxygens.
  2. Multiply each count by its atomic mass. Carbon: 6 × 12 = 72. Hydrogen: 12 × 1 = 12. Oxygen: 6 × 16 = 96.
  3. Add them. 72 + 12 + 96 = 180 Da. One molecule of glucose weighs 180 daltons.
  4. Sodium chloride: 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 Da.
  5. The same numbers in grams give you one mole. 180 g of glucose is one mole of glucose. 58.5 g of NaCl is one mole of NaCl. Keep both numbers, they come back in problems 2 and 3.

Carbohydrates

Two sugars joined

Mechanism
  • Glucose and galactose are the same atoms in a slightly different arrangement. One hydroxyl group points the other way. That one difference is why they need different enzymes and different transporters.
  • This is the first place shape shows up. Same formula, different shape, different treatment by the body.

Shape, again

Lactose has to be split into galactose and glucose by the enzyme lactase before the gut can absorb it. Many adults stop making lactase. The unsplit lactose stays in the gut, pulls water in with it, and feeds the bacteria of the colon. The problem is not the sugar. It is a missing protein whose binding site fits that one shape.

12

Lipids

Fatty acids and the fats built from them

Mechanism
  • Lipids are mostly carbon and hydrogen, so they are nonpolar and do not dissolve in water. That is the whole reason they need carrier proteins in blood.
  • A double bond puts a kink in the chain. Kinked chains cannot pack tightly, so unsaturated fats are liquid at room temperature and saturated fats are solid.
13

Lipids

The lipid relatives that do signaling and structure

Mechanism
  • Eicosanoids (prostaglandins, leukotrienes, thromboxanes) are made on demand from a fatty acid in the membrane and act nearby. Aspirin and ibuprofen work by blocking the enzyme that makes them.
  • Steroids all start from cholesterol. Cortisol, aldosterone, estrogen and testosterone are cholesterol with small edits, and because they are lipids they pass straight through membranes.
  • Phospholipids are the reason cells exist. A phosphate head that loves water, two fatty acid tails that hate it. Put them in water and they arrange themselves into a double layer with the tails hidden inside. That double layer is next week.
14

Proteins

Amino acids and the peptide bond

Mechanism
  • Twenty amino acids build human proteins. Nine are essential, meaning we cannot make them and must eat them.
  • The R group is where the personality lives. Some R groups are charged, some are polar, some are oily, one contains sulfur. Which R groups sit where decides how the chain folds.
  • A peptide bond joins the amino group of one amino acid to the carboxyl group of the next, releasing a water molecule. It is covalent, so a protein chain does not fall apart on its own.
  • Two to nine amino acids is an oligopeptide, ten to a hundred a polypeptide, more than a hundred a protein. Insulin, at 51, is technically a polypeptide, which is why you will see it called both.

A few amino acids never appear in proteins and still matter. GABA is a brain signal. Creatine stores energy in muscle. Homocysteine in excess is linked to heart disease.

16

Proteins

Why the shape is the job

Mechanism
  • Only the primary structure is held by strong covalent bonds. Levels two, three and four are held mostly by the weak bonds from slide 6. That is deliberate. It lets a protein flex when something binds it.
  • It also means heat, pH and salt can undo the fold without touching the chain. That is denaturation: same amino acids, wrong shape, no function.
  • The one covalent bond that helps hold a fold is the disulfide bond, sulfur to sulfur between two cysteines. Insulin is two chains held by disulfide bonds. Hair is full of them, and a permanent wave works by breaking and re-forming them.
  • Change the pH and you change which R groups carry a charge. Change the charges and you change which ionic bonds hold. That is the mechanism behind today's opening problem. Hold onto it.
17

Nucleotides

Base, sugar, phosphate

Mechanism
  • Two jobs. First, catch energy in high energy electrons or phosphate bonds and hand it to something that needs it. Second, carry information, as DNA, RNA and cAMP.
  • In DNA the bases pair by hydrogen bonds: A with T (two bonds), G with C (three bonds). A purine always pairs with a pyrimidine, so the two strands stay a constant width apart.

Functional groups

The small parts that move as a unit

Mechanism

A functional group is a cluster of atoms that shows up over and over and moves between molecules as one piece.

Table 2.1, the four you will see most
GroupFormulaWhat it does
AminoNH2Accepts H+, so it acts as a base. On every amino acid.
CarboxylCOOHGives up H+, so it acts as an acid. On every amino acid and fatty acid.
HydroxylOHPolar, hydrogen bonds with water. Sugars are covered in them.
PhosphateH2PO4Charged, holds energy in ATP, switches proteins on and off.

Molecules combine across families

  • Conjugated proteins carry another kind of molecule. Lipoproteins carry lipid. LDL and HDL are lipoproteins.
  • Glycosylated molecules carry a sugar. Glycoproteins and glycolipids stud the outside of every cell and are how cells are recognized. Your blood type is a glycolipid.

Application

Adding a phosphate group to a protein adds two negative charges at that spot. New charges pull on nearby R groups, the fold shifts, and the binding site changes shape. Kinases add phosphate, phosphatases remove it. That is how a huge fraction of cell signaling turns proteins on and off, and you will see it every week from here on.

Solutions

Water is the solvent for everything

Mechanism
  • Solute is what dissolves. Solvent is what it dissolves in. In the body the solvent is always water, so every body fluid is an aqueous solution.
  • Hydrophilic molecules are polar or charged and dissolve readily. Hydrophobic molecules are nonpolar and do not.
  • Amphipathic molecules have both a polar and a nonpolar region. Phospholipids are the example that matters.

Predict before you look

Two drugs are the same size. One is hydrophilic, one is hydrophobic. Which one will cross a cell membrane easily, and which one will need a carrier protein to travel in blood? Say why in one sentence each.

Concentration

Saying how much is in there

Mechanism

Molarity (M) = moles of solute ÷ liters of solution

Units: mol/L, written M. Body fluids are dilute, so values are usually mmol/L, written mM.

What it says: molarity counts particles, not mass. Equal masses of two solutes are not equal numbers of molecules, and it is the number of molecules that does the work.

Percent solution = grams of solute per 100 mL of solution

Units: g/100 mL. A 5 percent solution is 5 g in every 100 mL. Pharmacies and IV bags use this because they weigh, they do not count moles.

What it says: the same number means different amounts of particles for different solutes, because their molecules weigh different amounts.

Equivalents (Eq) = moles × charge on the ion

Units: mEq/L for ions in blood. Na+ has 1 Eq per mole. Ca2+ has 2. HPO4 2- has 2.

What it says: one mole of calcium carries twice the charge of one mole of sodium. Body fluids must stay electrically neutral, so charge matters as much as particle count.

Two more units you will read on lab reports

  • mg/dL is milligrams per deciliter, a deciliter being 100 mL. Glucose, calcium and cholesterol are usually reported this way in the United States.
  • One mole is 6.02 × 1023 particles, and the molecular mass in grams is one mole of that substance. That is the bridge between weighing and counting.

Solved problem 2

Make a bag of D5W, then say how strong it is

Solve

D5W is 5 percent dextrose (glucose) in water. It is one of the most common IV fluids there is.

  • Part A. How many grams of glucose are in a 500 mL bag of D5W?
  • Part B. What is the molarity of D5W, in mM? You know from problem 1 that glucose is 180 g per mole.

Before the answer opens

Estimate part A in your head. 5 percent of 500 is a number you can get without a calculator.

Solved

  1. Part A. A 5 percent solution is 5 g in every 100 mL. A 500 mL bag is five of those. 5 × 5 g = 25 g of glucose, with water added until the total volume is 500 mL.
  2. Part B. Work with one liter. A 5 percent solution has 5 g per 100 mL, so 50 g per 1000 mL, which is 50 g/L.
  3. Convert grams to moles. 50 g ÷ 180 g/mol = 0.278 mol.
  4. That is 0.278 mol in one liter, so 0.278 M, which is 278 mM.
  5. Why it matters: body fluids sit near 280 to 300 milliosmoles per liter, and D5W was designed to land close to that so the bag does not shrink or swell the red cells it meets. The pharmacy label reads 252 mOsm/L rather than 278 because the dextrose in the bag is the monohydrate form, 198 g per mole. Same reasoning, slightly heavier molecule. Next week you will see what happens once the glucose is taken up and only water is left.

Solved problem 3

Normal saline, in every unit the chart uses

Solve

Normal saline is 0.9 percent NaCl. NaCl is 58.5 g per mole and splits completely into Na+ and Cl- in water.

  • Part A. What is the sodium concentration of normal saline in mM?
  • Part B. What is it in mEq/L?
  • Part C. Plasma calcium is 2.5 mmol/L. Calcium is Ca2+ and weighs 40 g per mole. Give that value in mEq/L and in mg/dL.

Solved

  1. Part A. 0.9 percent is 0.9 g per 100 mL, so 9 g per liter. 9 g ÷ 58.5 g/mol = 0.154 mol/L. Every NaCl gives one Na+, so sodium is 154 mM, and chloride is 154 mM as well.
  2. Part B. Sodium carries one charge, so equivalents equal moles. 154 mEq/L. Compare plasma, about 140 mEq/L. Saline is a little saltier than you are.
  3. Part C, equivalents. Calcium carries two charges. 2.5 mmol/L × 2 = 5 mEq/L.
  4. Part C, mass. 2.5 mmol/L × 40 mg/mmol = 100 mg/L. A deciliter is a tenth of a liter, so 10 mg/dL. That is exactly the number on a metabolic panel.
  5. The lesson: the same calcium reads 2.5, 5 or 10 depending on the unit. Whenever a value looks strange, check the unit before you check the physiology.
24

pH

A narrow window, defended hard

Mechanism
  • An acid gives up H+ in water. The carboxyl group does this. A base takes H+ out of solution, either by binding it directly, like ammonia, or by releasing OH- that combines with it, like sodium hydroxide.
  • Water itself is 1 × 10-7 M in H+, which is pH 7.
  • Arterial blood is 7.35 to 7.45. Inside most cells is a little lower, about 7.0 to 7.2. Stomach contents are near 1 to 3, and urine can swing from about 4.5 to 8.
  • The scale is logarithmic. From pH 8 to pH 6 is not twice the acid, it is a hundred times the acid.
25

pH

What the equation is actually saying

Mechanism

pH = −log [H+]

The log is the point. Square brackets mean concentration. A drop of one pH unit is a tenfold rise in hydrogen ion concentration. A drop of 0.3 is a doubling.

What it says: pH runs backwards. More acid means a lower number, and small changes in the number are large changes in the acid.

[H+] = 10−pH

The same equation turned around. Use it to get from a pH on a blood gas report back to an actual concentration.

What it says: normal arterial [H+] is about 40 nanomolar. That is a millionth of the sodium concentration, and it is defended more tightly than anything else in the body.

Why the body cares this much

  • Free H+ can join in hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds. Enough of it changes which R groups on a protein carry a charge.
  • Change the charges and the weak bonds holding the tertiary structure shift. The binding site changes shape. The protein works worse, or not at all.
  • A buffer is anything that soaks up H+ when there is too much and releases it when there is too little. Proteins buffer, phosphate buffers, and bicarbonate is the big one in blood. You build the bicarbonate system properly in week 13.

Solved problem 4

Back to the patient: how much did the acid change?

Solve

Three parts, building up. Use pH = −log [H+] and [H+] = 10−pH.

  • Part A. Warm up from the book. A solution has [H+] = 1 × 10−7 M. What is its pH?
  • Part B. Normal arterial [H+] is 40 nM, which is 4 × 10−8 M. Show that this gives pH 7.40.
  • Part C. Our patient dropped to pH 7.10. What is their [H+] now, and how many times higher is it than yesterday? Check your slide 2 guess.

Solved

  1. Part A. pH = −log(10−7). The log of 10 to a power is the power, so log(10−7) = −7, and the minus sign in front makes it pH 7.
  2. Part B. Split 4 × 10−8 into two logs: log(4) + log(10−8) = 0.60 + (−8) = −7.40. Flip the sign: pH 7.40. Worth memorizing: 40 nM is 7.40.
  3. Part C. [H+] = 10−7.10. Write it as 100.90 × 10−8. 100.90 is about 7.9, so [H+] is about 79 nM.
  4. 79 ÷ 40 is close to 2. The acid roughly doubled. A change of 0.30 on the pH scale is always a factor of 2, because log(2) is 0.30.
  5. That is the answer to the opening problem. The pH moved by a number that looks small, the hydrogen ion concentration doubled, and doubling the free protons is enough to change which R groups are charged on proteins throughout the body.

Binding

Specificity and induced fit

Mechanism
  • A ligand is anything that binds to a protein. A substrate, a hormone, a drug, an ion.
  • Specificity: a protein binds one ligand or a small family of very similar ones, because the binding site and the ligand have to be complementary in shape and charge.
  • Induced fit: neither side is rigid. Binding pulls the site into its working shape, which is how binding can switch a protein on.
  • Isoforms are closely related proteins that do the same job with different affinity, often in different tissues. Fetal hemoglobin is an isoform of adult hemoglobin that grips oxygen harder.
  • Competition: two ligands that fit the same site compete for it. Agonists switch the protein on. Antagonists sit in the site and block it.
29

Binding

Affinity, equilibrium and the law of mass action

Mechanism

P + L ⇌ PL    Keq = [PL] ÷ ([P][L])

P free protein, L free ligand, PL the bound pair. Keq is the equilibrium constant.

What it says: a larger Keq means a higher affinity. More of the mix is sitting bound at any moment.

Kd = [P][L] ÷ [PL] = 1 ÷ Keq

The dissociation constant. Numerically it is the ligand concentration at which half the protein is bound.

What it says: a smaller Kd means a tighter grip. Pharmacology reports drugs this way: a Kd of 1 nM is a drug that works at very low concentration.

  • Affinity is how strongly a protein holds its ligand. Binding is reversible, so at any moment some pairs are forming and some are coming apart.
  • Law of mass action: push the concentration of anything on one side and the reaction runs toward the other side until the ratio is Keq again.

Solved problem 5

Two receptors, one dose

Solve

A drug binds receptor A with a Kd of 1 nM and receptor B with a Kd of 100 nM. The drug concentration in plasma is 10 nM.

  • Part A. Which receptor has the higher affinity for the drug?
  • Part B. What fraction of each receptor is occupied at 10 nM? Use: fraction bound = [L] ÷ ([L] + Kd).
  • Part C. The dose is raised so plasma hits 100 nM. What happens to each?

Before the answer opens

Remember that Kd is the concentration at which half the sites are full. Where is 10 nM relative to each Kd?

Solved

  1. Part A. Smaller Kd is tighter binding. Receptor A, with a Kd of 1 nM, holds the drug 100 times more tightly than receptor B.
  2. Part B, receptor A. 10 ÷ (10 + 1) = 10 ÷ 11 = 0.91. About 91 percent of receptor A is occupied.
  3. Part B, receptor B. 10 ÷ (10 + 100) = 10 ÷ 110 = 0.09. About 9 percent of receptor B is occupied.
  4. Part C. At 100 nM, receptor A: 100 ÷ 101 = 0.99, essentially full, and barely changed. Receptor B: 100 ÷ 200 = 0.50, half full, up from 9 percent.
  5. This is the whole logic of a therapeutic window. At the low dose the drug is almost entirely on its target, receptor A. Raise the dose and the target gains almost nothing while the off target receptor starts to fill. Side effects arrive not because the drug changed, but because the mass action moved.

Binding

Disturb the equilibrium and watch it recover

Predict

Predict before you look

Hemoglobin in the lung sits in equilibrium with oxygen: Hb + O2 ⇌ HbO2. Blood now arrives at a muscle where the oxygen concentration is much lower than in the lung. Using nothing but the law of mass action, which way does the reaction run, and what does that do to the muscle?

The chain

Lower free oxygen means [L] on the left side has dropped. The ratio [HbO2] ÷ ([Hb][O2]) is now larger than Keq, so the reaction runs in reverse: HbO2 comes apart and releases oxygen until the ratio is restored. The muscle gets oxygen for no other reason than that it used some. No signal, no controller, just mass action. Week 13 adds the details of why the curve is S shaped, but the direction is already decided here.

32

Regulation

Switching a protein on

Mechanism
  • Three ways to turn a protein on. Cut it, give it a cofactor, or bind a modulator at a second site.
  • The word allosteric means other site. Anything that binds away from the working site and changes the fold is allosteric, and it can activate or inhibit.
  • Why make a protein inactive and switch it on later? Because a digestive enzyme that was active from the moment it was made would digest the cell that made it.
33

Regulation

Switching a protein off

Mechanism
  • Reversible modulators use weak bonds and let go. Irreversible ones bind covalently and the protein is done until the cell makes a new one. Aspirin inhibits its enzyme irreversibly, which is why one dose affects a platelet for the rest of that platelet's life.

Apply it

Carbon monoxide binds the same site on hemoglobin that oxygen does, and holds it about 200 times more tightly. It is a competitive inhibitor with an enormous affinity advantage. The treatment follows from mass action: flood the patient with 100 percent oxygen, sometimes at high pressure, so that oxygen finally outnumbers the CO at the site.

34

Regulation

Temperature and pH change the shape

Perturb
  • Warm a protein and its molecules collide more often, so its activity rises. Keep warming and the weak bonds holding the fold start to break. Around 50 °C (122 °F) this protein has unfolded completely. That is denaturation.
  • pH does the same thing by a different route. H+ changes which R groups are charged, the ionic bonds shift, and the fold changes.
  • A protein that has been mildly disturbed can refold when conditions return. A protein that has been badly denatured usually cannot. A cooked egg white does not become clear again.

The figure question

Is this protein more active at 30 °C (86 °F) or at 48 °C (118 °F)? Read it off the curve, then say what a fever of 41 °C (105.8 °F) would be doing to an enzyme with this curve.

35

Regulation

More protein, more ligand, until saturation

Mechanism
  • Up-regulation: the cell makes more copies of a protein, or breaks down fewer. Down-regulation: the reverse. This is how a cell tunes how sensitive it is to a signal, over hours to days.
  • Saturation: the rate has hit a ceiling set by the number of binding sites. Once you are on the flat part of the curve, the only way to go faster is more protein.

Solved problem 6

Read the two curves like a clinician

Solve

Use figure 2.13b and c from the previous slide.

  • Part A. On the protein graph, what is the rate when the protein concentration is A? At what protein concentration is the rate 2.5 mg/sec?
  • Part B. On the ligand graph, what is the rate at a ligand concentration of 200 mg/mL?
  • Part C. A patient's kidney reabsorbs glucose using a transporter. Blood glucose rises from 5 mM to 20 mM. Glucose starts appearing in the urine. Which graph explains it, and what is the bottleneck?

Solved

  1. Part A. At protein concentration A the line reads 1 mg/sec. Follow 2.5 mg/sec across to the line and drop down: it lands at C. Rate is proportional to the amount of protein, so two and a half times the protein gives two and a half times the rate.
  2. Part B. 200 mg/mL is past the bend. The curve flattened at about 100 mg/mL and stays at 4 mg/sec. Doubling the ligand from 100 to 200 changed nothing, because every site was already busy.
  3. Part C. This is the ligand graph. The transporters in the kidney are the protein, and glucose is the ligand. At 5 mM they keep up and every glucose is reclaimed. At 20 mM the transporters are saturated. The extra glucose has nowhere to bind, so it stays in the filtrate and leaves in the urine.
  4. The bottleneck is the number of transporters, not the amount of glucose. That is why giving the kidney more time does not help, and why the only long term fix is on the protein side, either fewer glucose molecules arriving or more transporters made.
  5. Say it as a rule: when a response stops increasing even though the input keeps rising, look for a saturated protein.

Now explain the patient, start to finish

Change one thing. The patient also has a fever of 40 °C (104 °F). Does the fever make the pH problem better, worse, or is it a separate problem? Use the temperature curve from slide 34 to defend your answer.

One version of the answer

  1. pH fell by 0.30, so free H+ roughly doubled, from about 40 nM to about 79 nM. Problem 4.
  2. Extra H+ binds to R groups that can accept it, amino groups especially. Charges that were negative or neutral become neutral or positive. Slides 16 and 25.
  3. The ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds that hold the tertiary fold depend on those charges. Some break, some form where they should not. Slide 6 and 16.
  4. The fold shifts, so the binding site shifts. Complementarity with the ligand drops, and with it affinity. Slides 28 and 29.
  5. Lower affinity means less protein sits in the bound state at the same ligand concentration, so the rate of every reaction that protein runs falls. Mass action, slide 29.
  6. The fever adds a second push on the same weak bonds. On the curve in figure 2.13a, 40 °C (104 °F) is just past the peak and sliding toward denaturation, so it makes the shape problem worse, not better. Two perturbations, one mechanism.
