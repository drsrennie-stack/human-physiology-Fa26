A. The vessels, and the rules of flowabout 12 min in

You should be able toVessel structure and function. Match arteries, arterioles, capillaries, venules, and veins to their role in pressure buffering, resistance, exchange, and capacitance. Pressure flow and resistance. Apply the relationship among flow, pressure gradient, and resistance and predict the effect of a change in vessel radius, blood viscosity, or vessel length on flow.

Say firstWe start with the pipes. In the drawing, the thickness of each wall tells you what that vessel is built to do.

Label the drawing: One circuit, with the pressure in each vessel

Context boxOne lap of the systemic circuit, left to right: artery, arteriole, capillaries, venule, vein. The graph underneath is the pressure in each vessel. Wall thickness is drawn to tell you what each vessel is built to do. First we name the parts. Then we follow what happens, step by step.

Pin 1This is the elastic artery. Aorta and its big branches: thick wall with layers of elastin. Stretches during ejection and recoils during diastole, so flow continues between beats. Why it matters: pressure reservoir: stiff arteries (age, atherosclerosis) raise systolic pressure and pulse pressure.

Pin 2This is the arteriole. Small artery with a thick ring of smooth muscle and little elastin. The main site of resistance; its radius sets how much flow a tissue gets and how much pressure is left for the capillaries. Why it matters: total peripheral resistance lives here; this is where blood pressure drugs act.

Pin 3This is the precapillary sphincter. A ring of smooth muscle where a capillary leaves a metarteriole. Opens or closes individual capillary beds. Why it matters: lets a tissue route blood to the cells that need it.

Pin 4This is the capillary. One layer of endothelium, 5 to 10 μm across, with a basement membrane. The only vessel that exchanges: gases, nutrients, waste and fluid cross here. Why it matters: huge total cross-section makes flow slowest here, which gives time for exchange.

Pin 5This is the venule. Small vein that collects capillaries. Some exchange continues; white cells leave the blood here in inflammation. Why it matters: first place pressure is low enough for cells to crawl out.

Pin 6This is the vein. Thin wall, little muscle, large lumen, very compliant. Capacitance vessel: holds about 60% of the blood at rest and gives it up when the sympathetic system tightens it. Why it matters: the venous reservoir is what venous return and preload are drawn from.

Pin 7This is the venous valve. Flaps of endothelium in limb veins. Let blood flow toward the heart only. Why it matters: make the skeletal muscle pump work; failed valves give varicose veins.

Pin 8This is the endothelium. The single cell layer lining every vessel. Makes nitric oxide and endothelin, controls what crosses, keeps blood from clotting on it. Why it matters: endothelial damage is the start of atherosclerosis.

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing. The lining is not just a lining; it signals to the muscle under it. Blood dragging across it, and chemicals such as acetylcholine, histamine and bradykinin, switch on the enzyme that makes nitric oxide. Nitric oxide diffuses into the smooth muscle, raises cGMP, and the muscle relaxes. The same cells can make endothelin when they are injured, when flow is low, or under angiotensin II, and endothelin works through IP₃ and calcium to constrict. So the vessel makes its own dilator and its own constrictor. Nitroglycerin and sildenafil both work on the nitric oxide side.

Pin 9This is the pulse pressure in the artery. The swing between systolic and diastolic in the arteries. Shows the stroke volume hitting an elastic wall. Why it matters: the pulse you feel at the wrist is this pressure wave, not the blood itself.

Pin 10This is the pressure drop across the arterioles. The steepest fall on the profile, from about 93 to about 35 mm Hg. Resistance converts pressure into a controlled trickle for the capillaries. Why it matters: dilate the arterioles and pressure downstream rises; constrict them and it falls.

Pin 11This is the capillary pressure. About 32 mm Hg at the arterial end falling to about 15 at the venous end. Pushes fluid out through the pores (filtration). Why it matters: this is the pushing force in the Starling balance.

Pin 12This is the venous pressure. Low and steady, a few mm Hg, nearly zero at the right atrium. The small gradient that still drives blood back; it needs help from the pumps. Why it matters: central venous pressure is a bedside measure of volume status.

How it happens: One circuit, with the pressure in each vessel

Context boxThis is the same drawing, and now we follow the sequence. Each click lights up where the step happens and draws a gold line to it, and the close-up in the box shows that moment. Have them write the step name in the numbered box on the sheet and the detail under it.

Step 1The ventricle ejects into an elastic artery. During systole the aorta's elastic wall stretches and stores some of the pulse's energy; pressure peaks near 120 mm Hg.

Step 2Diastole: the artery recoils. The stretched wall springs back and keeps pushing blood forward between beats, so flow never stops and pressure only falls to about 80; the pulses on the graph are this stretch and recoil.

Step 3The arteriole resists. A thick ring of smooth muscle makes the arteriole the narrowest adjustable point; most of the pressure is spent getting through it (about 93 down to 35) and the pulse disappears.

Step 4The capillary exchanges. Total cross-sectional area is enormous, so velocity is slowest here; with one cell of wall, gases, nutrients, waste and fluid cross.

Step 5Venules and veins collect and store. Pressure is now a few mm Hg; the thin, compliant veins hold about 60% of the blood, and valves keep it moving one way.

Step 6Back to the right atrium at about 0 mm Hg. The whole loop runs on one rule: flow = pressure gradient ÷ resistance, so cardiac output = MAP ÷ total peripheral resistance.

EffectThe effect, what changes because of this: Pressure falls all the way around, and most of it is spent at the arterioles. Resistance goes with 1 ÷ radius⁴, so halving an arteriole's radius raises its resistance 16 times: small changes in arteriolar tone are the body's main way to steer flow and set blood pressure. Give them a minute to write the effect box in their own words before you move on.

Fill in: The rules of flow

Line 1Flow = pressure gradient ÷ resistance (F = ΔP ÷ R); for the whole circuit, CO = MAP ÷ TPR.

Line 2Resistance depends on vessel length, blood viscosity, and radius; R is proportional to length × viscosity ÷ radius⁴.

Line 3Halve the radius and resistance rises 16 times, so flow falls to one sixteenth at the same pressure.

Line 4Double the viscosity (polycythemia) and resistance doubles, flow halves.

Line 5Length matters when new vessels grow (obesity, a tumor); otherwise it is fixed.

Line 6Velocity of flow is flow ÷ total cross-sectional area: fastest in the aorta, slowest in the capillaries, faster again in the veins.

Say firstYou will take blood pressures in lab, so it is worth knowing exactly what the cuff is doing to the artery.

Label the drawing: The arterial pressure wave and the cuff

Context boxLeft: the pressure in a large artery, beat by beat. Right: the same wave with a cuff pressure (gold) being let down across it. The sounds you hear through the stethoscope are drawn below the wave. First we name the parts. Then we follow what happens, step by step.

Pin 1This is the systolic pressure. The peak, about 120 mm Hg, during ventricular ejection. Set by stroke volume and how stiff the arteries are. Why it matters: this is the top number of a blood pressure reading.

Pin 2This is the diastolic pressure. The trough, about 80 mm Hg, just before the next ejection. Set mainly by total peripheral resistance and how long diastole lasts. Why it matters: this is the bottom number of a blood pressure reading, and it tells you how tight the arterioles are.

Pin 3This is the dicrotic notch. The small blip on the falling side of each pulse. Marks the aortic valve closing (S2) and the brief backflow against it. Why it matters: separates ejection from diastole on a pressure trace.

Pin 4This is the pulse pressure. Systolic minus diastolic, about 40 mm Hg. Rises with a bigger stroke volume or stiffer arteries. Why it matters: a wide pulse pressure in an older adult means stiff arteries.

Pin 5This is the mean arterial pressure. The average pressure over the cycle, about 93 mm Hg; diastolic + one third of pulse pressure. The pressure that actually drives flow to the organs. Why it matters: mAP = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance; the number the baroreflex defends.

Pin 6This is the cuff pressure. The pressure in the inflated cuff, let down slowly. Above systolic it flattens the artery; below diastolic it does nothing. Why it matters: the sounds you hear only exist while cuff pressure is between the two.

Pin 7This is the first Korotkoff sound. The first tapping heard as the cuff is let down. Cuff pressure has just dropped below systolic: blood spurts through the squeezed artery in turbulent bursts. Why it matters: the cuff reading at that moment is the systolic pressure.

Pin 8This is the last Korotkoff sound. The sounds muffle and then stop. Cuff pressure has dropped below diastolic: the artery stays open and flow is smooth and silent. Why it matters: the cuff reading at that moment is the diastolic pressure.

How it happens: The arterial pressure wave and the cuff

Context boxThis is the same drawing, and now we follow the sequence. Each click lights up where the step happens and draws a gold line to it, and the close-up in the box shows that moment. Have them write the step name in the numbered box on the sheet and the detail under it.

Step 1Cuff above systolic: the artery is shut. At 140 mm Hg the cuff squashes the brachial artery flat; no blood passes, no sound.

Step 2Cuff drops below systolic: the first tap. The first time the pulse peak exceeds cuff pressure, blood squirts through the narrowed artery in a turbulent burst; that tap is the first Korotkoff sound and the cuff reading is systolic.

Step 3Sounds continue while the cuff is between the two. Each beat the artery opens briefly and snaps shut: tapping with every pulse.

Step 4Cuff drops below diastolic: silence. The artery stays open all the time, flow is smooth (laminar) and makes no noise; the cuff reading when the sounds vanish is diastolic.

Step 5Pulse pressure and mean pressure. Pulse pressure = systolic − diastolic (120 − 80 = 40). Mean arterial pressure = diastolic + ⅓ pulse pressure (80 + 13 = 93), because diastole lasts longer than systole.

Step 6What sets each number. Systolic rises with stroke volume and stiff arteries; diastolic rises with resistance (arteriolar tone) and heart rate; MAP = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance.

EffectThe effect, what changes because of this: Two numbers from a cuff tell you about the pump and the pipes: a high diastolic means the arterioles are tight, a wide pulse pressure means stiff arteries or a big stroke volume. MAP is the number the body defends, and it has only two levers: cardiac output and resistance. Give them a minute to write the effect box in their own words before you move on.

Fill in: Work the numbers

Line 1Pulse pressure = systolic − diastolic 120 − 80 = 40 mm Hg.

Line 2MAP = diastolic + ⅓ pulse pressure 80 + 40 ÷ 3 = 93 mm Hg (closer to diastolic because diastole lasts longer).

Line 3MAP = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance raise either and MAP rises; the baroreflex adjusts both.

Line 4A patient at 150/70 pulse pressure 80, MAP 97: stiff arteries (wide pulse pressure), MAP about normal.

C. How an arteriole decides its own toneabout 70 min in

You should be able toLocal control of blood flow. Explain myogenic, metabolic, and endothelial control of arteriolar tone and predict the local flow response to increased tissue metabolism.

Say firstWho decides how open an arteriole is? There are five answers, and the tissue the arteriole serves has most of the say.

Mini mind map: How an arteriole decides its own tone

CenterCenter of the map: Arteriolar tone. One branch per click; each branch has its own small drawing of how that one works. Have them draw the map on the sheet, with the branch name first and the explanation under the picture.

Branch 1Myogenic. Stretch opens cation channels in the smooth muscle, it depolarizes and contracts; drop the pressure and it relaxes. Keeps flow steady when arterial pressure changes (autoregulation).

Branch 2Metabolic (active hyperemia). A working tissue makes more CO₂, H⁺, K⁺ and adenosine and uses up O₂; all of them relax the arteriole. Flow follows metabolism: exercising muscle, a digesting gut, an active brain region.

Branch 3Endothelial. The lining makes nitric oxide (cGMP, relax) in response to shear stress, ACh, histamine and bradykinin, and endothelin (contract) in response to injury. Nitroglycerin and sildenafil act on the NO side.

Branch 4Reactive hyperemia. Block the flow, metabolites build up and the arterioles dilate; release the block and flow overshoots before settling. The same metabolic control, seen after an interruption.

Branch 5Sympathetic tone. Tonic norepinephrine on α₁ receptors keeps most arterioles partly constricted; more firing constricts, less firing dilates. Local signals can override it: exercising muscle dilates despite high sympathetic activity.

D. Capillary exchange, and edemaabout 116 min in

You should be able toCapillary exchange and Starling forces. Identify the four Starling forces at a capillary and determine the net direction of fluid movement at the arterial and venous ends. Edema. Explain how increased capillary hydrostatic pressure, reduced plasma protein, increased permeability, or lymphatic blockage each produce edema and match a clinical scenario to its mechanism. Say firstNow the exchange itself, which is the reason the whole circuit exists. We need two forces, one capillary, and the lymphatic vessel that cleans up afterward.

Label the drawing: Starling forces along one capillary

Context boxOne capillary opened out, blood flowing left to right. Red arrows: pressure pushing fluid out through the pores. Blue arrows: the pull of plasma proteins drawing fluid back in. Green: the lymphatic that collects what is left over. First we name the parts. Then we follow what happens, step by step.

Pin 1This is the capillary hydrostatic pressure, arterial end. Blood pressure inside the capillary at its start, about 32 mm Hg. Pushes fluid out through the pores. Why it matters: the force that wins at the arterial end.

Pin 2This is the capillary hydrostatic pressure, venous end. Blood pressure at the capillary's end, about 15 mm Hg. Still pushes out, but weakly. Why it matters: at this end the osmotic pull is stronger than the push, so fluid comes back in.

Pin 3This is the plasma colloid osmotic pressure. About 25 mm Hg of pull from plasma proteins, mostly albumin, that cannot leave the capillary. Draws water back in along the whole length. Why it matters: low albumin (liver failure, kidney loss, starvation) means fluid stays out: edema.

Pin 4This is the interstitial fluid. The fluid between the cells, about a quarter of the extracellular fluid. Its own hydrostatic pressure is near zero and its protein is low, so its forces are small. Why it matters: edema is simply too much interstitial fluid.

Pin 5This is the net filtration (arterial end). Out minus in: about 32 − 25 = 7 mm Hg outward. Fluid leaves the capillary carrying dissolved nutrients. Why it matters: filtration is how plasma, with its dissolved nutrients, reaches the cells.

Pin 6This is the net absorption (venous end). In minus out: about 25 − 15 = 10 mm Hg inward. Most of the filtered fluid returns here. Why it matters: across the body about 3 L a day is left over, not returned.

Pin 7This is the lymphatic capillary. Blind-ended vessel with overlapping flaps that open inward. Picks up the leftover 3 L a day and any protein that leaked, and returns it to the veins. Why it matters: block it and the limb swells (lymphedema).

Pin 8This is the capillary wall (endothelium with pores). One cell thick, with pores between the cells (and fenestrations in kidney and gut). Water and small solutes pass; proteins mostly do not. Why it matters: inflammation widens the pores, proteins leak, and the osmotic pull collapses.

How it happens: Starling forces along one capillary

Context boxThis is the same drawing, and now we follow the sequence. Each click lights up where the step happens and draws a gold line to it, and the close-up in the box shows that moment. Have them write the step name in the numbered box on the sheet and the detail under it.

Step 1At the arterial end, hydrostatic pressure wins. Capillary hydrostatic pressure is about 32 mm Hg pushing out; plasma colloid osmotic pressure is about 25 pulling in; net 7 mm Hg outward: filtration.

Step 2Fluid leaves, proteins stay. Water and small solutes pass through the pores between endothelial cells; albumin is too big, so the osmotic pull inside stays at 25.

Step 3Pressure falls along the capillary. Friction drops hydrostatic pressure from 32 to about 15 by the venous end; the osmotic pull does not change.

Step 4At the venous end, the osmotic pull wins. 25 in against 15 out: net 10 mm Hg inward: absorption. Most of what left comes back.

Step 5The lymphatic takes the remainder. About 3 L a day across the body is not reabsorbed; lymphatic capillaries pick it up, with any protein that leaked, and return it to the veins near the heart.

Step 6The interstitial forces are small. Interstitial hydrostatic pressure is near zero and interstitial protein is low, so in health the balance is set by the two capillary forces.

EffectThe effect, what changes because of this: Filtration at the start, absorption at the end, lymph for the difference. Raise capillary pressure, lower plasma protein, open the pores, or block the lymphatics and fluid piles up in the tissue: that is edema, and each cause maps to a disease. Mini mind map: Four ways to get edema

CenterCenter of the map: Edema. One branch per click; each branch has its own small drawing of how that one works. Have them draw the map on the sheet, with the branch name first and the explanation under the picture.

Branch 1Raised capillary pressure. More hydrostatic push along the whole capillary: more filtration, less absorption. Right heart failure (ankles), left heart failure (lungs), a blocked vein, standing all day.

Branch 2Low plasma protein. Less albumin means less osmotic pull back in; filtration continues, absorption fails. Liver failure, nephrotic syndrome, protein malnutrition (kwashiorkor).

Branch 3Leaky capillaries. Inflammation and histamine widen the pores; albumin escapes and takes its osmotic pull outside with it. A swollen ankle, hives, burns, anaphylaxis.

Branch 4Blocked lymphatics. The 3 L a day that is not reabsorbed has nowhere to go. Filariasis (elephantiasis), lymph nodes removed in breast cancer surgery.

Steps in order: Lymph, back to the blood: about 3 L a day

Step 1Interstitial space. Of the roughly 20 L filtered out of capillaries each day, about 17 L is reabsorbed at the venous end; the 3 L left behind, plus any protein that leaked, is lymph.

Step 2Lymphatic capillary. Blind-ended tube whose endothelial cells overlap like one-way flaps: fluid pushes them open to get in and shuts them to get out.

Step 3Lymphatic vessels. Valves and the skeletal muscle pump move the lymph along, the same way they move venous blood.

Step 4Lymph nodes. Filtered through nodes on the way: immune cells inspect it.

Step 5Thoracic duct, or right lymphatic duct. The thoracic duct collects from the whole body below the diaphragm and the left upper quarter; the right lymphatic duct takes the right upper quarter.

Step 6Subclavian veins. Both ducts empty into the junction of the internal jugular and subclavian veins, so the 3 L is back in the blood, and the plasma protein with it.

E. Venous returnabout 170 min in

You should be able toVenous return. Explain the skeletal muscle pump, the respiratory pump, and venoconstriction and predict the effect of prolonged standing on venous return and stroke volume.

Say firstBlood has to get back up from the feet to the heart with almost no pressure left to push it. The body uses three pumps.

Label the drawing: Three pumps that bring blood back

Context boxLeft: the trunk with the diaphragm between thorax and abdomen and the vena cava running up through both. Middle: a leg vein with its valves and the calf muscle around it. Right: a vein before and after sympathetic constriction. First we name the parts. Then we follow what happens, step by step.

Pin 1This is the venous valve. Flaps of endothelium in limb veins. Let blood flow toward the heart only. Why it matters: make the skeletal muscle pump work; failed valves give varicose veins.

Pin 2This is the skeletal muscle pump. Leg muscles squeezing the deep veins each time they contract. Pushes blood toward the heart; the valves stop it falling back. Why it matters: standing still for a long time loses this pump and blood pools.

Pin 3This is the respiratory pump: thoracic pressure falls on inspiration. The chest cavity's pressure drops as the ribs lift and the diaphragm descends. Draws blood up the vena cava toward the right atrium. Why it matters: deep breathing raises venous return, which is one reason it helps when you feel faint.

Pin 4This is the respiratory pump: abdominal pressure rises. The descending diaphragm squeezes the abdomen. Pushes blood out of the abdominal veins toward the chest. Why it matters: the two halves of the respiratory pump work together.

Pin 5This is the sympathetic venoconstriction. Norepinephrine on α₁ receptors in venous smooth muscle. Makes the veins less compliant, so the same volume sits at higher pressure and moves toward the heart. Why it matters: this is how the body pulls blood out of the venous reservoir in hemorrhage and in exercise.

Pin 6This is the venous pooling in the legs. Blood settling in the compliant leg veins under gravity. Lowers venous return, end-diastolic volume and stroke volume. Why it matters: why soldiers standing at attention faint, and why you feel faint standing up fast.

Pin 7This is the vena cava. The two great veins returning blood to the right atrium. Carry the venous return, at a pressure of only a few mm Hg. Why it matters: easily compressed; late pregnancy lying flat can squeeze it.

How it happens: Three pumps that bring blood back

Context boxThis is the same drawing, and now we follow the sequence. Each click lights up where the step happens and draws a gold line to it, and the close-up in the box shows that moment. Have them write the step name in the numbered box on the sheet and the detail under it.

Step 2The skeletal muscle pump. Each contraction squeezes the deep veins; the valve above opens and the valve below closes, so blood can only go up.

Step 6More venous return, more stroke volume. Venous return fills the ventricle; by Frank-Starling the next beat ejects more.

Label the drawing: The baroreceptor reflex

Context boxLeft: the two places pressure is sensed, the carotid sinus and the aortic arch. Middle: the medulla. Right: the three targets. Dark lines carry the signal in; blue is the parasympathetic output, rust the sympathetic output. Read it for a fall in pressure, such as standing up. First we name the parts. Then we follow what happens, step by step.

Pin 1This is the carotid sinus baroreceptor. Stretch receptors in the wall of the carotid sinus, where the common carotid splits. Fire faster when stretched by higher pressure, slower when pressure falls. Why it matters: guards pressure to the brain; pressing on the neck here can slow the heart.

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing. A baroreceptor is a stretch sensor, nothing more. At normal pressure the wall is stretched a little and the nerve endings fire a few action potentials a second. When pressure rises the wall stretches more and they fire faster. The message to the medulla is the firing rate, carried by the glossopharyngeal nerve from the carotid sinus and the vagus from the aortic arch. When pressure falls the rate drops, and the medulla reads that too. That is the whole sensor: more stretch, more spikes.

Pin 2This is the aortic arch baroreceptor. Stretch receptors in the wall of the aortic arch. Same signal as the carotid sinus, for the whole body. Why it matters: both sets report continuously; the reflex is tonic, not just emergency.

Pin 3This is the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX), afferent. The nerve carrying carotid sinus signals to the medulla. It carries sensory signals in; it is the afferent arm of the reflex for the carotid sinus. Why it matters: cut it and the medulla no longer hears from the carotid sinus.

Pin 4This is the vagus nerve (CN X), afferent. Carries aortic arch signals to the medulla. Sensory input (the vagus also carries the parasympathetic output back out). Why it matters: the same nerve carries the sensory signal in and the parasympathetic signal back out.

Pin 5This is the medullary cardiovascular control center. The nucleus of the solitary tract and the neighboring centers in the medulla. Compares baroreceptor input with the set point and adjusts the two autonomic outputs. Why it matters: this is the integrating center of the reflex, where the comparison with the set point happens.

Pin 6This is the parasympathetic output to the SA node. Vagal fibers releasing acetylcholine on M₂ receptors. Slows the pacemaker. Why it matters: this is the fast brake on heart rate, and resting vagal tone is why the resting rate is near 70 rather than 100.

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing; it is the one from last week. Acetylcholine on M₂ receptors works through Gi: it lowers cAMP, so the funny channels open less, and it opens potassium channels, so the cell sits lower. The slope flattens and the floor drops, and threshold comes later. This is the fast brake, and because the vagus is firing all the time at rest, your resting rate sits near seventy instead of the node's own hundred.

Pin 7This is the sympathetic output to the heart. Sympathetic fibers releasing norepinephrine on β₁ receptors in the SA node and ventricles. Raises heart rate and contractility. Why it matters: raises cardiac output within seconds.

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing; it is from last week too. Norepinephrine on β₁ receptors works through Gs and raises cAMP. In the SA node the funny channels open more and the slope gets steeper, so heart rate rises. In the ventricles the same cAMP opens more L-type calcium channels and speeds the SR pumps, so each beat is stronger and shorter. Rate and contractility both go up, which is why cardiac output can rise within seconds.

Pin 8This is the sympathetic output to arterioles. Norepinephrine on α₁ receptors in arteriolar smooth muscle. Constricts arterioles, raising total peripheral resistance. Why it matters: resistance is one of the two terms in MAP = CO × TPR, so this output moves blood pressure quickly.

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing; it is the chemical door from last week. Norepinephrine lands on an α₁ receptor, the receptor turns on Gq, Gq turns on phospholipase C, IP₃ is made, IP₃ opens the SR calcium channel, and the receptor-operated channel lets more calcium in. The arteriole constricts, resistance rises, and since mean pressure is cardiac output times resistance, blood pressure rises right away. This is the output the baroreflex leans on hardest.

Pin 9This is the sympathetic output to veins. Norepinephrine on α₁ receptors in venous smooth muscle. Venoconstriction raises venous return and stroke volume. Why it matters: pulls blood out of the reservoir when pressure falls.

How it happens: The baroreceptor reflex

Context boxThis is the same drawing, and now we follow the sequence. Each click lights up where the step happens and draws a gold line to it, and the close-up in the box shows that moment. Have them write the step name in the numbered box on the sheet and the detail under it.

Step 1Pressure falls, the baroreceptors stretch less. Carotid sinus and aortic arch receptors fire slower.

Step 2Fewer signals reach the medulla. Along the glossopharyngeal (carotid sinus) and vagus (aortic arch) nerves to the cardiovascular control center.

Label the drawing: The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, with ADH and ANP

Context boxThe long-term lever on blood pressure: a chain of hormones that ends in the kidney keeping salt and water. Follow the arrows from the kidney at the left, through the blood, to the targets on the right. ANP, in blue, pulls the other way. First we name the parts. Then we follow what happens, step by step.

Pin 1This is the juxtaglomerular cells (renin). Modified smooth muscle cells in the wall of the afferent arteriole of the nephron. Release renin when renal pressure falls, when the macula densa sees less NaCl, or when sympathetic β₁ receptors are stimulated. Why it matters: the kidney's own pressure sensor starts the cascade.

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing. Three things make these cells release renin, and all three mean the same thing to the kidney: pressure or volume is low. First, the cells are stretch sensors in the afferent arteriole wall; less stretch means less pressure, and they release renin. Second, sympathetic nerves release norepinephrine onto their β₁ receptors. Third, the macula densa next door tastes the tubular fluid; less sodium chloride going past means less filtrate, so it signals the JG cells. Renin goes into the blood and the cascade starts.

Pin 2This is the angiotensinogen (from the liver). A plasma protein always present in blood. Renin cuts it to angiotensin I. Why it matters: nothing happens without the enzyme; the substrate is just waiting.

Pin 3This is the angiotensin I. A ten amino acid peptide, inactive. Nothing yet; it circulates until an enzyme converts it. Why it matters: it is a step with no action of its own, which is why the next enzyme matters so much.

Pin 4This is the angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE). An enzyme on the endothelium, especially in the lungs. Converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II. Why it matters: aCE inhibitors (the -pril drugs) block this step to lower blood pressure.

Pin 5This is the angiotensin II. The active hormone of the whole system. Constricts arterioles, triggers aldosterone and ADH, drives thirst, and raises sympathetic activity. Why it matters: it raises pressure right away by raising resistance, and over the following days by raising blood volume.

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing. One hormone, four targets, one direction. On arterioles it works through AT₁, IP₃ and calcium to constrict, which raises resistance now. On the adrenal cortex it releases aldosterone, which will raise volume over days. On the posterior pituitary it releases ADH, and in the hypothalamus it drives thirst, both adding water. And it turns sympathetic activity up. Pressure up now by resistance, up later by volume.

Pin 6This is the aldosterone (adrenal cortex). A steroid from the zona glomerulosa. Makes the distal nephron reabsorb Na⁺ (and water follows) and secrete K⁺. Why it matters: the slow lever: more volume, more pressure, over hours to days.

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing. Aldosterone is a steroid, so it walks into the distal nephron cell and acts on genes. The cell makes more sodium channels and more sodium-potassium pumps, so more sodium is reabsorbed, water follows it, and potassium is secreted in exchange. Because it works through new proteins it is slow: hours to days. This is the slow lever on blood volume.

Pin 7This is the antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin). A peptide from the hypothalamus released by the posterior pituitary. Makes the collecting duct reabsorb water; at high levels constricts arterioles. Why it matters: released by angiotensin II, by high osmolarity, and by a large fall in pressure.

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing. ADH binds V₂ receptors on the collecting duct cell, raises cAMP, and the cell inserts aquaporin-2 water channels into its membrane. Water leaves the tubule and the urine becomes concentrated. At high levels it also binds V₁ receptors on arterioles and constricts them, which is why it has a second name, vasopressin. It is released by angiotensin II, by a rise in osmolarity, and by a large fall in pressure.

Pin 8This is the thirst center. Hypothalamic neurons that angiotensin II and high osmolarity stimulate. They make you feel thirsty and drink. Why it matters: volume cannot be replaced by the kidney alone; intake has to rise.

Pin 9This is the atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP). A peptide released by the atria when they are stretched by too much volume. Makes the kidney excrete Na⁺ and water, dilates arterioles, and inhibits renin, aldosterone and ADH. Why it matters: it is the brake on the whole system, released when there is already too much volume.

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing. This is the brake. When the atria are stretched by too much volume they release ANP. ANP makes the kidney excrete sodium and water, dilates arterioles, and inhibits renin, aldosterone and ADH, so it undoes the whole cascade. Everything else we have drawn pushes pressure up; this one pushes it down, and it is released only when there is already too much.

How it happens: The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, with ADH and ANP

Context boxThis is the same drawing, and now we follow the sequence. Each click lights up where the step happens and draws a gold line to it, and the close-up in the box shows that moment. Have them write the step name in the numbered box on the sheet and the detail under it.

Step 1The kidney releases renin. Juxtaglomerular cells release renin when renal perfusion pressure falls, when the macula densa senses less NaCl, or when sympathetic nerves fire on β₁ receptors.

Step 2Renin cuts angiotensinogen. A liver protein always present in plasma becomes angiotensin I, which is inactive.

Step 3ACE makes angiotensin II. Angiotensin-converting enzyme on the lung endothelium converts I to II; this is the step the -pril drugs block.

Step 4Angiotensin II constricts arterioles. Resistance rises at once: a fast effect.

Step 5Angiotensin II triggers aldosterone. The zona glomerulosa releases aldosterone; the distal nephron reabsorbs Na⁺ and water follows: more volume over hours to days.

Step 6Angiotensin II triggers ADH and thirst. The posterior pituitary releases ADH (water reabsorbed in the collecting duct) and the hypothalamus makes you drink.

Step 7ANP opposes it. When the atria are overstretched they release atrial natriuretic peptide: the kidney excretes Na⁺ and water, arterioles dilate, and renin, aldosterone and ADH are all inhibited.

EffectThe effect, what changes because of this: The baroreflex fixes pressure in seconds by changing resistance and output; this system fixes it over hours and days by changing blood volume. Most blood pressure drugs act here: ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, aldosterone antagonists, and diuretics that undo the volume. Steps in order: Losing blood: compensation, then failure

Step 1Blood volume falls. Less venous return, less EDV, less stroke volume, cardiac output and MAP fall.

Step 2The baroreflex fires within seconds. Heart rate and contractility up (β₁), arterioles constrict in skin, gut and kidney (α₁), veins constrict: pale, cool skin and a fast weak pulse; flow is kept for brain and heart.

Step 3Capillaries pull fluid back in. Lower capillary pressure means absorption beats filtration: interstitial fluid refills the plasma over minutes to hours (autotransfusion).

Step 4The hormones take over. Renin, angiotensin II, aldosterone and ADH keep Na⁺ and water; thirst rises; the kidney makes little urine.

Step 5The slow repairs. Erythropoietin restores red cells over days; the liver replaces plasma proteins.

Step 6Where compensation fails. Past roughly 30 to 40% loss, MAP can no longer be held: the heart and the vasomotor center themselves are underperfused, acidosis relaxes the arterioles, capillaries leak, and the fall in pressure feeds itself (progressive, then irreversible shock).
