The engineering team

This week you are not a student. You are on a device team, and the team is everyone in this class. Your job is to design one piece of equipment that measures something about the heart, or steps in when it fails, and to defend the physiology behind it to your colleagues.

Post by Friday 10:00 pmTwo replies by Sunday 10:00 pmCompetencies 158 to 173

The brief

Pick one target. Design around the physiology, not the gadget.

Choose one of the five targets below. Nobody is assigned; pick the one you understand least well, because writing the post is how you will come to understand it. You may invent a device or redesign one that exists, but every design choice has to come from how the heart works. A post that describes a product without the physiology earns nothing.

Targets

  1. Rhythm. A device that reads the heart's electrical activity and decides whether the rhythm is safe. What does it look for, and what does it do when it finds a block or a run of tachycardia?
  2. Pump output. A device that estimates stroke volume, ejection fraction or cardiac output without opening the chest. What signal would you measure, and how would you turn it into a volume?
  3. Valves. A device that listens to the heart and flags a stenosis or a regurgitation, and says which valve. How does it tell S1 from S2, systole from diastole, a flow murmur from a leak?
  4. Injury. A bedside test that tells an emergency department within minutes whether chest pain is a heart attack. Which molecule, why that one, and what does a negative result mean at hour one versus hour six?
  5. Backup. A device that takes over when the conduction system fails: a pacemaker or a defibrillator. When does it fire, where does it deliver its signal, and how does it avoid firing when it should not?

Your post has three parts, labeled

  1. The signal. What exactly will the device measure or change, and what is the physiology that produces that signal. Name the structures, the ions or the proteins, and the sequence. This is the longest part.
  2. Normal and abnormal. What a healthy reading looks like, with a number or a pattern, and what one specific abnormal reading looks like and why the physiology produces it.
  3. How the body fools it. One way the body's own compensation or variation would give your device a misleading reading, and what you would build in to handle it. Examples: a trained athlete's resting rate of 45, a fast heart rate that hides P waves, a normal ejection fraction in a patient who has lost half their blood, a troponin drawn too early.

About 250 to 400 words. Write it like a memo to colleagues, not an essay. Drawings are welcome; photograph them and attach.

The two replies

Reply 1: find the flaw

Pick a teammate's post on a different target from yours. Find the place where the physiology does not hold, or where the body would fool the device in a way they did not consider, and say so plainly, with the mechanism. Then propose the fix. "Great post" is not a reply. A reply that names no mechanism earns nothing.

Reply 2: what you got wrong

Reread your own post after you have read three others. Write a reply under it that says what you now think you got wrong or left out, what changed your mind, and what you would redesign. This is the part most people skip, and it is the part that is graded hardest, because noticing your own error is the whole skill this course is trying to build.

Ten points. Late posts lose the replies they would have earned, because your colleagues cannot reply to a post that is not there.

Using AI here

You can use it the way an engineer would: to check a fact, to find a reference range, to ask what a device on the market actually measures. You cannot use it to write the post, and it will show, because an AI does not know what you got wrong in your first draft and cannot write Reply 2 for you. If you used it, say what you asked it and what you checked.
