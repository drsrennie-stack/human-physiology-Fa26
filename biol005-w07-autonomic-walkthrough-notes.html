Step by step: where the signal travels

In your textbook: Silverthorn Chapter 12, the parts on the autonomic division and the somatic motor division.

A somatic motor command, step by step

  1. The cell body of the somatic motor neuron sits in the ventral horn of the spinal cord (or in the brainstem).
  2. Its single, myelinated axon leaves through the ventral root and runs all the way to a skeletal muscle. There is no synapse between the cord and the muscle.
  3. At the neuromuscular junction it releases acetylcholine (ACh), which binds nicotinic receptors on the muscle fiber.
  4. The muscle fiber depolarizes and contracts. A somatic motor neuron can only excite its muscle.

A parasympathetic signal to the heart, step by step

  1. The preganglionic neuron's cell body sits in the brainstem.
  2. Its long axon travels in the vagus nerve (cranial nerve X) to a ganglion on the heart.
  3. In the ganglion it releases ACh onto nicotinic receptors on the postganglionic neuron, which depolarizes and fires.
  4. The short postganglionic axon reaches the SA node and releases ACh from its varicosities.
  5. ACh binds M2 muscarinic receptors on the pacemaker cells. K+ channels open and less Ca2+ enters, so the cells take longer to reach threshold.
  6. The heart rate slows.
  7. Acetylcholinesterase breaks down the ACh, ending the signal.

A sympathetic signal to an arteriole, step by step

  1. The preganglionic neuron's cell body sits in the lateral horn of the thoracic or lumbar spinal cord.
  2. Its short axon leaves through the ventral root and enters a ganglion of the sympathetic chain beside the cord.
  3. It releases ACh onto nicotinic receptors on postganglionic neurons. It can branch up and down the chain and reach many of them.
  4. The long postganglionic axon runs to the arteriole and swells into varicosities along the vessel wall.
  5. The varicosities release norepinephrine, which binds alpha-1 receptors on the smooth muscle.
  6. IP3 and Ca2+ rise inside the smooth muscle cells, the muscle contracts, and the arteriole narrows.
  7. Norepinephrine diffuses away or is taken back into the varicosity and broken down by monoamine oxidase (MAO).

A sympathetic signal to a sweat gland, step by step

  1. The preganglionic neuron leaves the thoracic or lumbar cord and synapses in a chain ganglion, releasing ACh onto nicotinic receptors.
  2. The postganglionic neuron, a sympathetic cholinergic neuron, runs to the sweat gland.
  3. It releases ACh, not norepinephrine, onto muscarinic receptors on the gland, and the gland secretes sweat.

A sympathetic signal through the adrenal medulla, step by step

  1. The preganglionic neuron leaves the thoracic cord and runs straight to the adrenal medulla, with no ganglion on the way.
  2. It releases ACh onto nicotinic receptors on chromaffin cells, which are modified postganglionic neurons without axons.
  3. The chromaffin cells secrete epinephrine, with some norepinephrine, into the blood.
  4. Epinephrine travels as a neurohormone to adrenergic receptors throughout the body, for example beta-2 receptors on bronchiole smooth muscle, which relaxes and widens the airway.

Heart rate with each breath, step by step

  1. You breathe in.
  2. The brainstem briefly turns down vagal (parasympathetic) firing to the SA node.
  3. Less ACh reaches the M2 receptors, and the heart speeds up within about a beat.
  4. You breathe out, vagal firing returns, and the heart slows.
  5. This rise and fall is respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and its size reflects vagal tone.

Two efferent pathways

In your textbook: Silverthorn Chapter 12, the parts on autonomic pathways with two efferent neurons and on the somatic motor division.

The efferent (output) side of the peripheral nervous system has two divisions. The somatic motor division controls skeletal muscle. The autonomic division, also called the visceral nervous system, controls cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, many glands, and some adipose (fat) tissue.

Preganglionic neuron
The first autonomic neuron. Its cell body is in the brain or spinal cord, and its axon ends in an autonomic ganglion.
Autonomic ganglion
A cluster of nerve cell bodies outside the CNS, where the preganglionic neuron synapses on postganglionic neurons. One preganglionic neuron can reach many postganglionic neurons there (divergence), and some ganglia work as small integrating centers.
Postganglionic neuron
The second autonomic neuron. Its cell body is in the ganglion, and its axon runs to the target tissue.
  • The heart's pacemaker cells start each beat on their own, so autonomic nerves speed up or slow down a heart that is already beating. A transplanted heart, with its nerves cut, still beats.
  • Skeletal muscle has no rhythm of its own, so every contraction starts with a somatic motor neuron.

  • The vagus nerve, cranial nerve X, carries parasympathetic fibers to the organs of the chest and most of the abdomen.
  • Fight-or-flight and rest-and-digest are simplified labels. Both divisions are active most of the time, and the body usually shifts the balance between them rather than switching one off.
  • In a crisis the sympathetic division gives priority to the heart and skeletal muscle, and digestion waits.

Varicosities
Swellings along the end of a postganglionic axon, filled with vesicles. Transmitter diffuses from them to any nearby cells with receptors, so one axon affects many cells.
Cholinergic
Releasing or responding to ACh.
Adrenergic
Releasing or responding to norepinephrine or epinephrine.
Chromaffin cells
The cells of the adrenal medulla: modified sympathetic postganglionic neurons with no axons, which secrete epinephrine (and some norepinephrine) into the blood as a neurohormone.
  • The nicotinic receptors in autonomic ganglia are a different subtype from those at the neuromuscular junction, so a drug can block one without the other.
  • ACh is broken down by acetylcholinesterase. Norepinephrine diffuses away or is taken back into the varicosity and broken down by monoamine oxidase (MAO).
  • Bronchiole smooth muscle has few sympathetic endings, so its beta-2 receptors respond mainly to epinephrine from the adrenal medulla.
  • The response is set by the receptor, not by the molecule: the same adrenergic signal narrows an arteriole (alpha-1) and widens a bronchiole (beta-2).
  • The adrenal medulla is described as a modified sympathetic ganglion.

Dual innervation and tone

In your textbook: Silverthorn Chapter 12, the parts on antagonistic control and tonic control by the autonomic division.

Dual innervation
An organ receives fibers from both divisions.
Antagonistic control
The two divisions have opposite effects on the same organ, as on the heart rate.
Autonomic tone
A steady, low rate of firing at rest. Turning it up or down moves the organ in either direction.
  • With no nerves acting on it, the SA node fires at about 100 beats per minute (roughly 90 to 110). The resting heart beats at about 70.
  • Cutting or blocking the vagus nerve raises the heart rate to the SA node's own rate, about 100. Cutting the sympathetic supply at rest slows the heart only a little. So at rest the parasympathetic division dominates the heart.
  • Removing one division from a dually innervated organ leaves the other unopposed, so the organ moves the way the remaining division pushes it.
  • During exercise, vagal tone falls and sympathetic firing rises, and the heart rate climbs above 100.
  • Most blood vessels and the sweat glands receive only sympathetic neurons. Steady sympathetic tone keeps arteriole smooth muscle partly contracted. More firing narrows the vessel (vasoconstriction); less firing than at rest widens it (vasodilation).
  • Losing sympathetic tone to the vessels, as after some injuries high in the spinal cord, widens them and can drop blood pressure.

Heart rate with each breath (lab: autonomic testing)

  • Heart rate rises during inspiration and falls during expiration. This is respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and it is normal, usually largest in young, healthy people.
  • It comes from the vagus nerve: vagal activity is turned down with each breath in and returns with each breath out.
  • Only the vagus acts fast enough to change the rate within one breath. ACh at the SA node takes effect within about a second and wears off as quickly, while sympathetic effects through cAMP build and fade over several seconds.
  • A large beat-to-beat variation with breathing means strong vagal tone. You measure it in lab as heart rate variability.

Autonomic drugs

In your textbook: Silverthorn Chapter 12, the part on autonomic agonists and antagonists.

A drug that blocks a receptor acts on every organ that uses that receptor. Knowing which receptor dominates in each organ lets you predict both the effect a drug is given for and its side effects.

DrugBlocksWanted effectSide effects to predict
Propranolol (nonselective beta blocker)Beta-1 and beta-2Slower, weaker heartbeat; lower blood pressureAirway narrowing and wheezing, dangerous in asthma (beta-2 on bronchiole smooth muscle)
Metoprolol (beta-1 selective, cardioselective)Mainly beta-1Slower, weaker heartbeat; lower blood pressureAirways mostly spared at usual doses; selectivity is not complete at high doses
Atropine (muscarinic antagonist)MuscarinicFaster heart rate in a dangerously slow heartDilated pupils and blurred near vision, dry mouth, slower gut and constipation, less sweating with hot, dry skin
  • Beta blockers treat high blood pressure and some heart conditions by blocking beta-1 receptors on the heart, so it pumps less blood each minute.
  • Atropine blocks the parasympathetic division at every target, and the sympathetic cholinergic neurons to the sweat glands too. It speeds the heart by blocking the vagal tone at the SA node.
  • Near vision blurs with atropine because the muscle that focuses the lens for close work is under parasympathetic control.
  • Drugs are often designed to reach one organ. An inhaled muscarinic blocker widens the airways in some lung diseases, because breathing it in keeps most of the drug in the lungs.
