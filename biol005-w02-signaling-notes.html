The problem for today

Where this sits

You have the cell as a structure, and you have the ways things cross its membrane. Both of those are about a single cell looking after itself.

This section is about cells talking to each other. Your body has roughly thirty trillion cells and they have to act as one thing. A signal molecule released in one place has to reach the right cells somewhere else, be recognized, be converted into something the inside of the cell can act on, be amplified enough to matter, and then be switched off.

Every signal pathway you will meet for the rest of this course, and there are a great many, is the same five steps in a different costume: signal, receptor, transduction, response, termination. Learn the shape once here and the endocrine, nervous and cardiovascular weeks stop being new material and start being examples.

Signal types, and how far they travel

Competency 17

Signals are classified by how they get to their target, and the classification tells you how fast the response is and how widely it spreads.

Gap junctional. Ions and small molecules pass directly from one cell into the next through connexon pores, with nothing released into the extracellular fluid at all. This is the fastest kind, and you met the junction itself in the first half of this week. It is how cardiac muscle contracts as one unit.

Contact dependent. A membrane molecule on one cell binds a receptor on the cell touching it. Immune cells check each other this way.

Autocrine. The cell releases a signal that acts on itself. Common in immune signaling and in growth control.

Paracrine. Released into the interstitial fluid and acting on neighboring cells, over a short distance, then broken down before it travels far. Histamine in an allergic response works this way.

Neurotransmitter. A neuron releases it into a tiny synaptic cleft onto one target cell. Very short distance, very fast, very precise.

Neurohormone and hormone. Released into the blood and carried everywhere. Slow to arrive, long lasting, and it reaches every cell in the body, so specificity has to come from somewhere else.

That last point is the one worth holding. A hormone in the blood touches every cell you have. What decides which cells respond is not where the signal goes; it is which cells carry a receptor for it. No receptor, no response. That single idea explains why one hormone can do different things in different tissues and nothing at all in most of them.

Where the receptor sits, and what decides that

Competency 18

There are only two places a receptor can be, and one property of the signal molecule decides which.

Lipophobic signals, meaning water soluble ones: peptides, proteins, catecholamines like epinephrine. They cannot cross the lipid bilayer, so their receptor has to be on the cell surface, with a binding site facing out.

Lipophilic signals, meaning lipid soluble ones: steroid hormones, thyroid hormone. They pass straight through the membrane, so their receptor is inside the cell, in the cytosol or the nucleus.

Everything else follows from that one fact, and this is the part students find surprising.

A surface receptor cannot reach into the cell itself, so the message has to be relayed by proteins already sitting inside. Those relays are fast, because the machinery is already built and waiting. The response starts in seconds, and it stops soon after the signal leaves.

An intracellular receptor binds its signal and the complex acts on DNA, turning genes on or off. Making new protein takes time. The response starts in hours and lasts for hours or days after the signal has gone.

So if a question tells you a hormone is a steroid, you already know the receptor is inside, the mechanism is gene transcription, the onset is slow and the effect is long. You have not memorized that; you worked it out from solubility.

One thing to be careful about. Lipophilic hormones do not dissolve well in blood, which is mostly water, so they travel bound to carrier proteins. Only the free fraction can enter cells, and that is why a total hormone level and a free hormone level are different measurements.

Worked problem 1

Two patients are given a drug. Patient A gets a peptide hormone and reports an effect within a couple of minutes that has worn off an hour later. Patient B gets a steroid hormone and feels nothing for several hours, but the effect is still measurable two days later. Explain both time courses from the chemistry of the molecules, without looking up either drug.

Peptides are lipophobic. Steroids are lipophilic.

The work
  1. Patient A. A peptide cannot cross the membrane, so its receptor is on the cell surface.
  2. Surface receptors work through proteins that are already in the cell: G proteins, enzymes, second messengers. Nothing has to be built, so the response begins in seconds to minutes.
  3. Those same second messengers are broken down continuously, so when the hormone leaves the receptor the signal collapses quickly. Short onset, short duration.
  4. Patient B. A steroid crosses the membrane and binds a receptor inside the cell.
  5. The receptor complex acts on DNA and changes which proteins the cell makes. Transcription and translation take hours, which is the delay.
  6. Once made, those proteins persist until they are degraded, so the effect outlives the hormone by a long way. Slow onset, long duration.
  7. The picture to draw: two cells side by side, one with the signal stopping at the surface and a cascade inside, one with the signal walking straight through to the nucleus. Put a clock on each.
  8. The point to carry: onset and duration are not facts to memorize per hormone. They fall out of where the receptor is, which falls out of whether the molecule dissolves in lipid.

The pathway, feature by feature

Competency 19, part one

Every surface signal pathway has the same parts, and naming them makes any new pathway readable.

The ligand is the signal molecule. The receptor binds it, and binding changes the receptor's shape. That shape change is the whole trick: it is how information gets from outside to inside without the molecule itself ever entering.

Transduction is converting the message into a form the inside of the cell can use. Amplification happens along the way. The response is what the cell actually does: open a channel, run an enzyme, secrete something, contract, divide.

Four families of surface receptor, and you can sort almost anything into one of them.

Receptor channels. The receptor is itself an ion channel. Ligand binds, channel opens, ions flow. This is the fastest possible surface mechanism, milliseconds, and it is how the neuromuscular junction works.

G protein coupled receptors. The receptor activates a G protein, which activates an amplifier enzyme, which makes a second messenger. The biggest family by far, and the target of a large share of all prescription drugs.

Receptor enzymes. The receptor has an enzyme site on its inner face, usually a kinase. The insulin receptor is one.

Integrin receptors. Linked to the cytoskeleton, they transduce mechanical information about what the cell is attached to.

G protein coupled receptors, step by step

Competency 19, part two

This is the pathway to be able to draw from blank paper, because so much else is a variation on it.

The parts: a receptor that crosses the membrane seven times, a G protein sitting on the inner face with three subunits called alpha, beta and gamma, an amplifier enzyme, and a second messenger.

At rest the alpha subunit holds GDP and the whole thing is quiet.

One. The ligand binds the receptor on the outside and the receptor changes shape.

Two. The receptor's inner face now grips the G protein, and the alpha subunit swaps its GDP for GTP. That swap is the switch turning on.

Three. The activated alpha subunit separates and moves along the inner face of the membrane to the amplifier enzyme.

Four. The amplifier enzyme runs and produces second messenger, a lot of it.

Five. The second messenger activates a protein kinase, which adds phosphate groups to target proteins and changes what they do. Phosphorylation is the cell's general purpose on switch.

Six. The alpha subunit hydrolyses its own GTP back to GDP and reassembles with beta and gamma. The switch turns itself off, which is why the pathway does not run forever.

Worth noticing: the G protein is a timer as well as a switch. How long the alpha subunit takes to hydrolyze GTP sets how long the signal lasts. Cholera toxin works by locking the alpha subunit so it cannot hydrolyze its GTP; the switch jams on, cAMP stays high, chloride and water pour into the gut, and that is the diarrhea.

Second messengers, and the ones to know

Competency 20

A second messenger is a small molecule made inside the cell in response to a signal outside it. The hormone is the first messenger and never gets in; the second messenger is what actually carries the news around the cytosol.

Cyclic AMP. The amplifier enzyme is adenylyl cyclase, which converts ATP to cAMP. cAMP activates protein kinase A. Epinephrine acting on a beta receptor runs this pathway.

IP3 and DAG. The amplifier enzyme is phospholipase C, which cuts a membrane phospholipid into two messengers at once. IP3 diffuses to the endoplasmic reticulum and opens calcium channels there; DAG stays in the membrane and activates protein kinase C.

Calcium. The most widely used messenger in the body, and it works because the cell holds cytosolic calcium extremely low, about ten thousand times lower than outside. Opening a calcium channel for a moment therefore produces a large proportional change, and calcium binds calmodulin or troponin and changes what they do. That very low resting level, which the calcium pumps from the transport half of this week maintain, is what makes calcium usable as a signal at all.

Cyclic GMP and nitric oxide. Nitric oxide is a gas that diffuses straight into the neighboring cell, raises cGMP, and relaxes smooth muscle. This is how nitroglycerin relieves angina and how sildenafil works.

Amplification: why one molecule matters

Competency 22

A hormone is present in blood at very low concentration, often a billionth of a mole per liter. The response it produces can be enormous. The gap between those two is amplification, and it is a property of the cascade, not of the hormone.

Each step multiplies. One ligand activates one receptor, but that receptor activates many G proteins before the ligand lets go. Each G protein switches on an amplifier enzyme, and each enzyme makes many molecules of second messenger. Each second messenger activates a kinase, and each kinase phosphorylates many target proteins.

Multiply four steps of roughly ten to a hundred each and one hormone molecule ends up changing millions of product molecules. The classic worked figure for epinephrine driving glycogen breakdown in a liver cell is a single hormone molecule producing on the order of a hundred million molecules of glucose.

Two consequences. Sensitivity: a cell can respond to a vanishingly small signal, which is what lets hormones work at the concentrations they do. And fragility: a cascade with that much gain has to be shut off precisely, or a trace of signal would run away. Which is the next section.

Worked problem 2

Epinephrine is released during a fright and reaches the liver at a concentration of about one nanomole per liter, while glucose leaves the liver in millimolar amounts. Work through the cascade and say where the gain comes from, then say what would happen if the G protein could not hydrolyze its GTP.

Epinephrine is lipophobic and acts at a beta receptor. Adenylyl cyclase, cAMP and protein kinase A are involved.

The work
  1. Epinephrine cannot enter the cell, so it binds a surface receptor. Shape change.
  2. The receptor activates G proteins. One occupied receptor turns on many G proteins while the ligand is bound, so the first multiplication happens here.
  3. Each alpha subunit activates adenylyl cyclase, and each enzyme molecule converts many ATP into cAMP. Second multiplication, and the biggest one.
  4. cAMP activates protein kinase A, and each kinase phosphorylates many targets. Third multiplication.
  5. The phosphorylated enzymes break glycogen down to glucose, and each of those enzymes works on many glycogen molecules. Fourth.
  6. Four multiplying steps take one nanomolar signal to a millimolar output. No single step is remarkable; the product of four is.
  7. If the alpha subunit could not hydrolyze its GTP, the switch would never turn itself off. cAMP would stay high, kinase A would stay active, and the liver would keep dumping glucose with no signal present. That is not hypothetical; it is the mechanism of cholera toxin in the gut and of certain tumours in endocrine tissue.
  8. The picture to draw: a funnel widening at each step, with the step names on it, and a separate small drawing of the alpha subunit with its GTP crossed out.

Receptor enzymes and intracellular receptors

Competency 21

Receptor enzymes skip the G protein. The receptor itself carries an enzyme site on its inner face, almost always a tyrosine kinase, and ligand binding switches that enzyme on directly.

The insulin receptor is the one to know. Insulin binds, the receptor autophosphorylates, and a chain of intracellular proteins is phosphorylated in turn. One of the results is that vesicles carrying GLUT4 transporters fuse with the plasma membrane, which raises glucose uptake in muscle and fat. Notice that the final step is exocytosis inserting a facilitated diffusion carrier: three separate ideas from this week in one pathway.

Intracellular receptors are the lipophilic route. The hormone crosses the membrane, binds its receptor in the cytosol or nucleus, and the complex binds DNA at a response element and changes transcription of specific genes.

Cortisol and the sex steroids work this way, and so does thyroid hormone, which is not a steroid but is lipophilic and behaves like one for this purpose. Slow, long lasting, and it changes what the cell is rather than just what it is doing at this moment.

Turning the volume up and down

Competency 23

A cell is not a fixed listener. It changes how loudly it hears a signal by changing its receptors, and this is as important clinically as the signal itself.

Down regulation. Sustained high signal, and the cell removes receptors from its surface or makes fewer of them. The same amount of hormone now produces less effect. This is the mechanism behind drug tolerance, and it is part of why a constant infusion of a hormone works less well than a pulsed one.

Up regulation. Sustained low signal, and the cell adds receptors, becoming more sensitive. A denervated muscle does this and becomes hypersensitive to acetylcholine.

Then the two words for molecules that bind receptors.

An agonist binds and activates. It does what the natural ligand does. A full agonist can produce the maximum response; a partial agonist activates the receptor but cannot reach the maximum however much you give.

An antagonist binds and does not activate. It occupies the site and blocks the real ligand. A beta blocker is an antagonist at the receptor epinephrine uses.

Competitive antagonists bind the same site and can be outcompeted by enough agonist, so the dose response curve shifts right but still reaches the same maximum. A noncompetitive antagonist cannot be outcompeted, so the maximum itself comes down. Which of those two a drug is decides whether more agonist can rescue the patient, which is why the distinction is on the exam and in the clinic.

Signal termination, and what happens when it fails

Competency 24

Nothing above matters unless the signal can be stopped. A pathway with that much amplification and no off switch is a pathology, not a control system.

The ways a signal ends.

The ligand leaves or is destroyed. Acetylcholinesterase breaks down acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft in about a millisecond. Neurotransmitters are also taken back up into the cell that released them. Hormones are cleared by the liver and the kidney, and a hormone's half life tells you how long its effect persists.

The receptor stops responding. Receptors are internalised or chemically modified so they no longer couple to their G protein.

The switch turns itself off. The G protein hydrolyses its GTP, as above.

The second messenger is destroyed. Phosphodiesterase breaks cAMP down continuously. Calcium is pumped back out of the cytosol and into the endoplasmic reticulum.

The phosphate comes off. Phosphatases reverse what the kinases did. Every on switch in the cell has a matching off switch, and the state of a protein at any moment is the balance between the two.

Failure of termination is a recognisable category of disease. Cholera toxin jams the G protein on. Some tumours carry a mutation that leaves a receptor permanently active with no ligand at all. Conversely, sildenafil works by inhibiting termination: it blocks the phosphodiesterase that would break down cGMP, so the relaxing signal lasts longer. Knowing how a pathway is switched off tells you where a drug can act.

Dose and response, and what a curve tells you

Competency 25

Plot the response against the dose and the curve is not a straight line. It rises slowly at low doses, steeply in the middle, then flattens.

The flattening is saturation, and it is the same idea as the transport maximum from the other half of this week: a finite number of receptors, all occupied. Past that point more drug adds nothing and side effects are all that is left.

Plot dose on a logarithmic axis and the same data becomes a tidy S shape, which is why nearly every published dose response curve is drawn that way.

Two numbers come off the curve.

EC50 is the dose giving half the maximum response, and it measures potency: how much drug you need. A lower EC50 means a more potent drug.

Maximal response measures efficacy: how much the drug can do at its best.

They are independent, and confusing them is the commonest error here. A very potent drug with a low ceiling is worse for a severe problem than a less potent drug with a high one. A partial agonist has lower efficacy no matter how potent it is.

On the curve: a competitive antagonist shifts it right, so the EC50 rises but the maximum is unchanged. A noncompetitive antagonist lowers the maximum. Being able to look at a shifted curve and say which kind of blocker was added is the competency.

Worked problem 3

Two dose response curves are drawn for the same agonist. In experiment one a blocker is added and the curve shifts to the right with the same maximum. In experiment two a different blocker is added and the maximum falls, with no rightward shift. Identify each blocker, and say which patient you could rescue by giving more agonist.

Competitive antagonists bind the same site as the agonist. Noncompetitive ones do not.

The work
  1. Experiment one. The maximum is unchanged, so every receptor can still produce a full response if the agonist reaches it. More agonist restores the original effect.
  2. That is a competitive antagonist. It binds the same site and the two molecules compete, so raising agonist concentration wins the competition.
  3. The shift right means a higher EC50: you need more drug for the same effect. Potency has fallen, efficacy has not.
  4. Experiment two. The maximum is lower, so some receptors cannot produce a response at any agonist concentration.
  5. That is a noncompetitive antagonist. Raising the agonist does not help, because the problem is not competition for the binding site.
  6. Efficacy has fallen. EC50 may be unchanged.
  7. Clinically: you can out dose the competitive blocker and you cannot out dose the noncompetitive one. That is the difference between an overdose you can treat with more agonist and one you have to support until the drug clears.
  8. The picture to draw: one set of axes with three curves on it, the control, the right shifted one, and the flattened one, with EC50 marked on each.
