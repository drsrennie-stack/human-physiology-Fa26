The problem for today

Slides 1 to 2 of the deck

Every cell in your body has a voltage across its membrane. It is small, about 70 thousandths of a volt, and the inside of the cell is the negative side. In this lecture we build that voltage starting from zero. We start with a cell that has no voltage at all, add one membrane protein at a time, and read a voltmeter after each step, so that you can see what each protein contributes. Then we put numbers on it with two equations, and last we change the blood chemistry and predict what happens.

Here is the problem we solve by the end. A construction worker is pinned under a collapsed wall for five hours with both legs crushed. He is awake and talking the whole time. The rescue team lifts the wall, and within minutes his heart rhythm turns chaotic and his heart stops. Nothing new injured him. The only thing that changed is that blood began flowing through his legs again.

Blood drawn in the ambulance shows a plasma potassium of 7.0 mM, where the normal range is 3.5 to 5.0 mM. His sodium (139 mM, range 135 to 145), chloride (102 mM, range 98 to 106), glucose (94 mg/dL, range 70 to 100) and temperature, 36.8 °C (98.2 °F), are all in range. So potassium rose by about 3 mM and everything else held. That does not sound like much, and sodium can move by 3 mM without anyone noticing. Write down your best guess for why 3 mM of potassium can stop a heart. By the end of these notes you will be able to calculate the answer.

Two terms, and what a voltmeter reads

Slides 3 to 4 of the deck

Two terms need to be kept apart from the start. The resting membrane potential is a steady voltage across the membrane of a cell that is not being stimulated. Almost every cell in the body has one: liver cells, fat cells and skin cells as well as neurons and muscle cells. An action potential is a brief, rapid change in that voltage that travels along the membrane. Only excitable cells can produce one, which means neurons and the three kinds of muscle. A liver cell has a resting potential because it has the sodium potassium pump and potassium leak channels, which are covered below. It cannot fire an action potential because it does not have voltage gated sodium channels. This lecture covers the resting potential. The action potential is the next lecture, and you need this one to understand it.

A membrane potential is always a comparison. One electrode goes inside the cell, one stays outside, and the meter reports the inside compared with the outside. By agreement the outside is called zero, so −70 mV means the inside is 70 millivolts more negative than the outside. A millivolt is one thousandth of a volt. A AA battery is 1,500 mV, so a cell holds about one twentieth of that across a membrane only 8 nanometers thick. Typical resting values are about −70 mV in a neuron and about −90 mV in skeletal and heart muscle.

One point is easy to get wrong. The cell is not filled with negative charge. The fluid inside and the fluid outside are each electrically neutral, with equal numbers of positive and negative charges. The only place the charges are unequal is a very thin layer on the two faces of the membrane, slightly more negative charge on the inner face and slightly more positive charge on the outer face.

Resting membrane potential
The steady voltage across the membrane of an unstimulated cell, inside compared with outside. About −70 mV in a neuron.
Action potential
A brief, rapid change in membrane potential that travels along the membrane. Only neurons and muscle cells produce one.
Millivolt (mV)
One thousandth of a volt.
Excitable cell
A cell that can fire action potentials: neurons and skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle cells.

Where the membrane proteins come from

Slide 5 of the deck

Every pump and channel in this lecture is a protein, and every protein is built from a gene. All of your cells contain the same genes. What differs from one cell type to another is which genes are switched on. All cells switch on the genes for the sodium potassium pump and for potassium leak channels, which is why almost every cell has a resting potential. Neurons and muscle cells also switch on the genes for voltage gated sodium and potassium channels, which is why only they can fire action potentials.

A membrane protein follows a fixed route from its gene to the membrane. In the nucleus the gene is copied into mRNA. Ribosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum read the mRNA and build the protein directly into the ER membrane. The Golgi finishes and sorts the protein. A vesicle carries it to the surface, and when the vesicle fuses with the cell membrane, the protein becomes part of that membrane. Transcription and translation are on the review page, so those steps are not repeated here.

A cell can change how many copies of a channel are in its membrane by adding more or taking some out. More open channels for an ion means a higher permeability to that ion, and permeability decides what the membrane potential will be. This is also why a mutation in one channel gene causes disease mainly in the tissues that use that gene. One inherited form of long QT syndrome comes from a faulty potassium channel in heart muscle: heart cells take longer to repolarize, and the person is at risk of dangerous rhythms. Diseases caused by a faulty ion channel gene are called channelopathies. Cystic fibrosis is another one, caused by a faulty chloride channel.

Gene expression
Using a gene to build its protein. Cells differ in which genes they express, not in which genes they have.
Channelopathy
A disease caused by a faulty ion channel gene, such as some forms of long QT syndrome or cystic fibrosis.

Building the voltage: the pump

Slides 6 to 8 of the deck

Start with an imaginary cell that has a lipid membrane and nothing else. Potassium, sodium and everything else are mixed the same way inside and outside, and ions cannot cross the oily middle of a bilayer, so nothing moves. Inside there are large negative molecules that can never leave, proteins and the phosphate groups on ATP and nucleic acids, but each one is paired with a positive ion, so the inside is neutral. The voltmeter reads zero. For a voltage to appear, positive and negative charges have to end up unequally shared across the membrane. That takes two things: something to make the two sides different, and a way for some ions, and not others, to cross.

The first protein we add is the sodium potassium pump, the Na+/K+ ATPase. It is in the membrane of every cell you have. Each cycle moves 3 sodium ions out and 2 potassium ions in, and splits one ATP to pay for it, because both ions are being moved toward the side where they are already more concentrated. The pump uses a lot of energy: roughly a fifth to a third of the ATP a resting body uses, and more than that in neurons.

Because three positive charges leave for every two that enter, the pump does make the inside slightly negative. A pump that moves net charge like this is called electrogenic. But look at the voltmeter. Even with the pump running at full speed, the cell is only a few millivolts negative. A real neuron is at −70 mV, so the pump alone does not explain the resting potential.

The main thing the pump does is create two steep concentration gradients. Potassium ends up at about 140 mM inside and 4 mM outside, 35 times more concentrated inside. Sodium ends up at about 145 mM outside and 15 mM inside, roughly 10 times more concentrated outside. Chloride is about 110 mM outside and 10 mM inside, and free calcium is about 1 mM outside and only 0.0001 mM inside. The inside of a cell is high in potassium and the fluid around it is high in sodium. A concentration difference is a form of stored energy, like water held behind a dam, and the pump uses ATP to build it. No ions cross yet, because there is still no channel for them to cross through.

Sodium potassium pump (Na+/K+ ATPase)
A membrane protein that moves 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in for each ATP it splits. It builds and maintains the ion gradients.
Electrogenic
Describes a pump that moves net charge across the membrane and so contributes directly to the membrane potential.
Concentration gradient
A difference in the concentration of a substance between two places. Substances diffuse from the higher concentration toward the lower.
Typical concentrations
K+ 4 mM outside, 140 inside. Na+ 145 outside, 15 inside. Cl− 110 outside, 10 inside. Free Ca2+ about 1 outside, 0.0001 inside.

Potassium leak channels and the equilibrium potential

Slides 9 to 11 of the deck

The second protein we add is a potassium leak channel. A leak channel is a protein pore with no gate, so it is open all the time. This one is lined so that potassium fits through and sodium does not. The channel does not choose a direction. It only makes movement possible. Potassium is 35 times more concentrated inside, so it diffuses out.

Each potassium ion that leaves carries one positive charge out and leaves behind the negative partner it was paired with. Those large anions are too big for any channel. So every potassium ion that leaves makes the inner face of the membrane a little more negative. A voltage produced this way, by an ion diffusing down its concentration gradient, is called a diffusion potential.

Now two forces act on every potassium ion. The chemical force comes from the concentration gradient and pushes potassium out. The electrical force comes from the voltage: a negative interior attracts a positive ion and pulls potassium back in. Together they are the electrochemical gradient. At the start the electrical pull is very small, so potassium leaves quickly. Each ion that leaves makes the inside more negative, and that makes it harder for the next one to leave. At one particular voltage the two forces are exactly equal and opposite. Potassium ions still cross in both directions, but as many enter as leave, so there is no net movement, even though the channels are still open. That voltage is the equilibrium potential for potassium, written EK, and it is also called the Nernst potential. With 140 mM inside and 4 mM outside it is about −95 mV.

Many students picture potassium leaving until the cell is nearly empty. That is not what happens. Setting up −95 mV across the membrane of a typical cell takes far fewer than one potassium ion out of every thousand in the cell, and the concentrations do not measurably change. The reason so few ions can do so much is where they sit. The potassium ions that left stay pressed against the outer face of the membrane, held by the negative charges they left on the inner face only 8 nanometers away. Opposite charges held that close together across a thin insulator produce a large voltage from a small number of charges, which is how a capacitor works. This is why a membrane potential can change in a thousandth of a second, and why a cell can do it again and again without running down.

Leak channel
An ion channel with no gate. It is always open. Potassium leak channels are the most numerous and set the resting potential.
Diffusion potential
A voltage produced across a membrane when an ion diffuses down its concentration gradient and leaves its oppositely charged partner behind.
Electrochemical gradient
The combined effect of the chemical force (concentration) and the electrical force (voltage) on an ion.
Equilibrium potential (Nernst potential)
The membrane potential at which the electrical force on an ion exactly balances its concentration gradient, so there is no net movement of that ion. EK is about −95 mV.

A sodium cell, and one voltage scale

Slides 12 to 13 of the deck

Take a second imaginary cell. Give it the same pump, so it has the same gradients, but close the potassium channels and open channels that only sodium fits through. Sodium is concentrated outside, so the chemical force pushes it in. At the start the inside is slightly negative, so the electrical force pulls it in as well. Both forces point the same way, so sodium enters quickly. Each sodium ion brings a positive charge with it, so the inside goes from slightly negative, through zero, to positive. Once the inside is positive, the electrical force reverses and begins pushing sodium back out. The two forces balance at about +61 mV, the equilibrium potential for sodium, ENa. The same rules gave the opposite sign, because the gradient points the opposite way.

Put the two cells on one voltage scale. Whenever an ion can cross the membrane, its movement shifts the membrane potential toward that ion's equilibrium potential: −95 mV for potassium and +61 mV for sodium. The more open channels an ion has, the larger its effect, so the ion with the highest permeability has the most influence on the membrane potential. A resting neuron sits at −70 mV, which is 25 mV from EK and 131 mV from ENa. That tells you the resting membrane is far more permeable to potassium than to sodium, roughly 25 to 40 times more. It is not exactly at EK, which means a small amount of another ion is also crossing. That ion is sodium.

The Nernst equation

Slides 14 to 16 of the deck

The Nernst equation calculates the equilibrium potential. It converts a concentration ratio into the voltage that would exactly cancel it. At body temperature, 37 °C (98.6 °F), it can be written Eion = (61.5 mV ÷ z) × log10([ion] outside ÷ [ion] inside), where z is the charge on the ion: +1 for potassium and sodium, −1 for chloride, +2 for calcium. The 61.5 mV bundles the gas constant, the Faraday constant, the conversion to log base 10 and body temperature, and many books round it to 61 or 60. The full form is E = (RT ÷ zF) × ln(out ÷ in), which is the same equation before the constants are multiplied out.

Three things are worth reading out of it. Only the ratio of outside to inside matters, and a tenfold ratio gives 61.5 mV for an ion with a single charge. The sign tells you which side ends up negative: for a positive ion that is more concentrated inside, the ratio is less than 1, its log is negative, and E comes out negative. And an ion with a double charge feels the voltage twice as strongly, so it needs only half the voltage to balance the same ratio, which is why z is in the denominator.

Eion = (61.5 mV ÷ z) × log10 ( [ion] outside ÷ [ion] inside )

Worked problem 1

Potassium is 4 mM outside the cell and 140 mM inside. Find EK, then say which way potassium moves in a neuron resting at −70 mV.

Body temperature, 37 °C (98.6 °F). z = +1.

The work

  1. Write the ratio, outside over inside. 4 ÷ 140 = 0.0286.
  2. Take the log. log10(0.0286) = −1.544. It is negative because the ratio is less than 1.
  3. Potassium carries one positive charge, so z = +1. EK = 61.5 × (−1.544) = −95 mV.
  4. Read the answer as a sentence: if potassium were the only ion that could cross, the inside would settle 95 mV negative, and at that voltage potassium would stop moving.
  5. Now the direction. The neuron is at −70 mV, which is not negative enough to hold potassium in. The outward push from concentration is still larger than the inward pull from voltage, so at rest potassium leaks out, slowly and continuously.
  6. Books that use slightly different concentrations get −90 mV. Either number is fine as long as you can show where it came from.

Worked problem 2

Sodium is 145 mM outside and 15 mM inside. Chloride is 110 mM outside and 10 mM inside. Find both equilibrium potentials, then say which way each ion is driven in a neuron resting at −70 mV.

Watch the sign of z for chloride.

The work

  1. Sodium ratio: 145 ÷ 15 = 9.67. log10(9.67) = 0.985. With z = +1, ENa = 61.5 × 0.985 = +61 mV.
  2. Direction for sodium. The cell is at −70 mV and sodium wants it at +61 mV. Concentration and voltage both point inward, so sodium is driven in, and it is a long way from balanced: 131 mV. Very little gets in at rest only because very few sodium channels are open.
  3. Chloride ratio: 110 ÷ 10 = 11. log10(11) = 1.041.
  4. Chloride is negative, so z = −1. ECl = (61.5 ÷ −1) × 1.041 = −64 mV.
  5. Direction for chloride. Concentration pushes chloride in, and the negative interior pushes it back out. At −64 mV those two cancel. A neuron at −70 mV is already very close to that, so chloride is nearly balanced and hardly moves at rest.
  6. What the three answers say together: at rest, potassium is slightly off balance, chloride is close to balanced, and sodium is far off balance and held back only by closed channels.

The real resting cell, and the Goldman equation

Slides 17 to 19 of the deck

A real membrane has many potassium leak channels and a few sodium leak channels. The small inward sodium leak brings positive charge in and raises the potential from −95 mV to about −70 mV. At −70 mV neither ion is at its own equilibrium, so potassium keeps leaking out and sodium keeps leaking in, all the time. The voltage holds steady because the two leaks carry equal and opposite charge, and the pump returns the ions that leaked. This is a steady state and not an equilibrium. An equilibrium needs no energy to stay where it is. A steady state stays constant only while energy keeps being supplied, and here that energy is ATP.

The Goldman equation, also called the Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz or GHK equation, handles several ions at once. It is the Nernst equation with each ion's concentration weighted by the membrane's permeability to that ion, P, which in practice means how many of that ion's channels are open. For potassium and sodium it reads Vm = 61.5 × log10((PK[K+]out + PNa[Na+]out) ÷ (PK[K+]in + PNa[Na+]in)). Chloride can be added as a third term. It is left out here because chloride is already close to balanced at rest.

The pump therefore contributes in two ways. Its direct contribution, from moving 3 charges out for every 2 in, is only a few millivolts. Its indirect contribution is everything else, because without the gradients the leak channels have nothing to work with. The resting potential is produced mainly by potassium leaking out through leak channels, and the pump makes that possible by keeping the cell full of potassium. If a poison blocked every pump, the voltmeter would lose only a few millivolts in the first second, because the gradients are still there. Over the following hours sodium would leak in and potassium out with nothing to return them, both equilibrium potentials would drift toward zero, the membrane potential would follow, and the cell would swell as water followed the sodium in.

Worked problem 3

At rest the membrane is about 25 times more permeable to potassium than to sodium. Set PK = 1 and PNa = 0.04, use the typical concentrations, and find Vm.

K+ 4 out and 140 in. Na+ 145 out and 15 in.

The work

  1. Top of the fraction, the outside concentrations: (1 × 4) + (0.04 × 145) = 4 + 5.8 = 9.8.
  2. Bottom of the fraction, the inside concentrations: (1 × 140) + (0.04 × 15) = 140 + 0.6 = 140.6.
  3. Ratio: 9.8 ÷ 140.6 = 0.0697. log10(0.0697) = −1.157.
  4. Vm = 61.5 × (−1.157) = −71 mV. That matches what an electrode measures in a real neuron.
  5. Check the extremes. Set PNa to zero and the equation becomes the Nernst equation for potassium, −95 mV. That was cell 1. Make PNa very large and the answer approaches +61 mV. That was cell 2.
  6. Look at step 1 again. Sodium is barely permeable, yet it contributes 5.8 against potassium's 4, because there is so much of it outside. A small permeability multiplied by a large concentration is enough to lift the potential 24 mV above EK.
Steady state
A condition that stays constant only because energy is continually spent to hold it there. The resting potential is a steady state.
Equilibrium
A condition that stays constant with no energy input. A single ion at its Nernst potential is at equilibrium.
Permeability (P)
How easily an ion crosses the membrane, set mainly by how many of its channels are open.
Goldman (GHK) equation
Predicts the membrane potential from the concentrations of several ions, each weighted by its permeability.

Channels, and the words for a potential that moves

Slides 21 to 22 of the deck

Every ion channel is a protein pore, and channels are sorted by what makes the pore open. Leak channels have no gate and are always open. They are in every cell, and potassium leak channels set the resting potential. Voltage gated channels open when the membrane potential changes. Sodium and potassium versions run along axons, and calcium versions sit at axon terminals and in heart muscle. Ligand gated channels, also called chemically gated, open when a specific molecule binds to them. The acetylcholine receptor on skeletal muscle at the neuromuscular junction is one, and so are the receptors on a neuron's dendrites. Mechanically gated channels open when the membrane is stretched, pressed or bent, as in touch receptors in the skin and hair cells in the inner ear.

Only leak channels are open in a resting cell, which is why this lecture needed only them. A cell uses the other three to change its membrane potential. The Goldman equation tells you what opening any of them does: it raises the permeability for whichever ion fits through, which gives that ion a bigger weight, so the membrane potential moves toward that ion's equilibrium potential. The concentrations do not need to change at all.

Four words describe a membrane potential that moves. A resting membrane is polarized, meaning it has a negative side and a positive side. Depolarization is any change that makes the inside less negative than rest. Overshoot is the part of a depolarization that goes past 0 mV, where the inside is briefly positive, so define it by the zero line and not by the height of the peak. Repolarization is the return toward the resting potential after a depolarization. Hyperpolarization is any change that makes the inside more negative than rest. A hyperpolarized cell is farther from threshold and harder to excite. A depolarized cell is closer to threshold and, at least at first, easier to excite.

Voltage gated channel
Opens in response to a change in membrane potential. Found along axons, at axon terminals and in muscle.
Ligand gated channel
Opens when a specific molecule, such as a neurotransmitter, binds to it.
Mechanically gated channel
Opens when the membrane is stretched, pressed or bent.
Threshold
The membrane potential, about −55 mV in a neuron, at which voltage gated sodium channels open and the cell fires. Covered in the next lecture.

Changing the blood

Slides 23 to 24 of the deck

Plasma potassium is the outside potassium for every cell in the body, so a change in the blood is a change in the Nernst ratio for every cell at once. More potassium outside means a weaker gradient pushing potassium out, less negative charge left behind, and every cell depolarizes. Less potassium outside means a steeper gradient, and every cell hyperpolarizes.

With low potassium, rest moves away from threshold. Nerve and skeletal muscle cells become harder to excite, and the patient has muscle weakness and cramps. Heart muscle responds to low potassium in a more complicated way, which we take up in the cardiac weeks. With high potassium, rest moves toward threshold, and for a short time cells fire more easily. Then a second effect becomes more important. Voltage gated sodium channels that stay at a depolarized potential for too long switch to an inactivated state and cannot reopen until the membrane repolarizes. Cells become unable to fire. In skeletal muscle that is weakness. In the heart it is slowed, disorganized conduction.

Now compare sodium. Dropping plasma sodium by 20 mM, from 145 to 125, moves ENa from +61 to +57 mV and the resting potential from −71 to −73 mV, a shift of 2 mV. Raising potassium by only 3 mM moved the resting potential 7 mV. There are two reasons, and both are in the equations. The resting membrane is about 25 times more permeable to potassium, so the resting potential follows EK and mostly ignores ENa. And the Nernst equation works on ratios: 4 to 7 mM nearly doubles the outside potassium, while 145 to 125 mM changes sodium by only 14 percent. Low sodium is still dangerous, but for a different reason. It lowers the osmolarity of the extracellular fluid, water moves into brain cells, and they swell.

The patient, explained

Slide 25 of the deck

Most of the potassium in the body is inside skeletal muscle cells, at 140 mM. Five hours of crushing killed a large mass of muscle, and dead cells release their contents. While the worker was pinned, the wall acted like a tourniquet. No blood flowed through his legs, so the released potassium stayed there. When the wall was lifted, blood flow returned and carried that potassium into his circulation all at once, and plasma potassium rose from 4 to 7 mM.

From there it is worked problem 4. EK moved from −95 to −80 mV in every cell of his body, and resting potentials followed it upward by about 7 mV. Heart cells held at a depolarized potential have many of their sodium channels held in the inactivated state. The electrical signal crosses the heart slowly and unevenly, the rhythm breaks down, and the heart stops pumping. That is why a 3 mM change in potassium affects the heart when a 3 mM change in sodium has almost no effect.

Look at the treatment as an experiment. The variable that failed is plasma potassium, and through it the gap between rest and threshold. Calcium given into a vein does not change potassium at all. Raising the calcium outside the cell moves threshold to a less negative value, which restores the gap, and it works within minutes. Insulin with glucose, and albuterol, both speed up the sodium potassium pump, so potassium moves out of the plasma and into cells within the hour, although it is still in the body. Dialysis, or working kidneys, is the only step that removes potassium from the body. The same physiology shows up whenever many cells break open at once, as in large burns, and whenever the kidneys cannot excrete potassium, as in kidney failure.
