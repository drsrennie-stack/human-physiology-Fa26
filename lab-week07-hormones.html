Part 1. Your autonomic nervous system, at home

Heart rate with breathing, and with standing up

Your heart rate is set from moment to moment by the two branches of the autonomic nervous system. These two tests let you watch them work. Find your pulse at the thumb side of your wrist, or at the side of your neck, with two fingers, not your thumb.

Test A. Breathing

  1. Sit quietly for 5 minutes.
  2. Count your pulse for 30 seconds while breathing normally, and double it. Do this twice and write both.
  3. Now breathe slowly, about 5 seconds in and 5 seconds out, for one minute, with your fingers on your pulse. Notice when the beats come closer together and when they spread apart. If a phone or watch shows your heart rate, read the highest number while you breathe in and the lowest while you breathe out, for three breaths.

Test B. Standing up

  1. Lie flat for 5 minutes. Count your pulse for 30 seconds and double it.
  2. Stand up and stay still. Count your pulse for 30 seconds starting at 1 minute, and again starting at 3 minutes.

If you feel dizzy or light-headed, sit or lie back down right away. Skip Test B if you have ever fainted on standing up or have a heart condition, and say so on your worksheet. This is practice of a test, not a medical test.

Read an unknown off a standard curve

A hormone assay works like the ELISA you just ran, but it measures how much hormone is in a sample. You first run standards, samples with known amounts of hormone, and record the color each one makes as a number called absorbance. The line through those points is the standard curve. Then you read your patient's sample off it. Below are practice standards for an assay of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), where more hormone gives more color.

Part 5. Clinical correlation

TSH 8.4 with a normal free T4

A patient has a TSH of 8.4 mIU per L, above the usual 0.4 to 4.0, and a free T4 of 1.1 ng per dL, which is inside the reference range. TSH is released by the anterior pituitary, and thyroid hormone feeds back on the pituitary and the hypothalamus. A small change in T4 makes a much larger change in TSH.
