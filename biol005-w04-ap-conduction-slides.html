How action potentials carry information

An action potential is the same size every time a neuron fires one. Today we ask how a signal that never changes size can tell the brain that one touch is light and another is hard, how it travels a meter along an axon without fading, and what decides how fast it gets there.

  • First we look at the few milliseconds after an action potential, the refractory periods. They limit how often a neuron can fire, and they keep the signal moving in one direction.
  • Then we follow the action potential along the axon and work out why a large myelinated axon conducts about a hundred times faster than a small unmyelinated one. Four problems are worked on the slides. Try each one before you open the answer.
  • Last, we change the conditions. We remove myelin, change the potassium and calcium in the blood, and block the sodium channel. That explains what happens in the dentist's chair.

Silverthorn chapter 9. Week 4 competencies 15 to 18.

The problem for today

A dentist injects lidocaine beside the nerve that serves the lower teeth. Within a few minutes the lip and the teeth on that side are numb, and the drill causes no pain. The patient can still feel the dentist pushing on the tooth. A few hours later all feeling is back. The drug never reached the pain receptors in the tooth, and they still respond to the drill. So where did the message stop, and why did pain disappear while pressure did not?

What still happens after the injection?

Here is what you need to decide. Lidocaine blocks voltage gated Na+ channels. It was injected beside the nerve, partway between the tooth and the brainstem, and it stays close to where it was injected. The endings of the pain receptors in the tooth respond to the drill with channels that are not voltage gated Na+ channels.

Pain is not felt in the tooth. It is felt when action potentials reach the brain, and an action potential only gets there if every stretch of axon along the way can fire. So one blocked stretch is enough to stop the message.

Three states of the voltage gated Na+ channel

The channel always goes through the states in this order: closed, open, inactivated, and back to closed.

  • You already know that a neuron fires when its trigger zone reaches threshold, about −55 mV, that the action potential peaks near +30 mV, and that it is all or none: if it fires, it is full size.
  • Everything today follows from one fact about this channel. After it opens it inactivates, and an inactivated channel cannot open again, however strongly the membrane is depolarized, until the membrane has repolarized and the channel has reset to closed.

Inactivated

At the peak most of the channels have already inactivated, which is one reason the potential stops rising. Depolarizing the membrane further does nothing to an inactivated channel. The membrane first has to repolarize, which the voltage gated K+ channels bring about, and then the inactivation gate reopens and the activation gate closes. Only then is the channel closed and ready again. That resetting takes a few milliseconds, and those few milliseconds are the subject of the next three slides.

04

Refractory periods

For a few milliseconds after it fires, the membrane cannot fire normally

  • A refractory period is a stretch of time after an action potential during which that patch of membrane is harder, or impossible, to fire again.
  • The absolute refractory period starts when the membrane reaches threshold and lasts about 1 to 2 ms, which is most of the action potential. The Na+ channels are open at first and then inactivated. No stimulus of any size can start a second action potential, because there are no closed, ready channels for it to open.
  • The relative refractory period follows and lasts a few more milliseconds. Some Na+ channels have reset, but not all of them. The voltage gated K+ channels are still open, so K+ is still leaving and the membrane is hyperpolarized, farther from threshold than usual.
  • A second action potential is possible now, but it takes a larger than normal stimulus, and it may be smaller than normal because fewer Na+ channels take part.

Try it

A neuron fires an action potential. A second stimulus, twice the normal strength, arrives (a) 1 ms after the first action potential began, and in another trial (b) 4 ms after it began. Decide what happens each time before you open the answer.

Nothing, then a smaller action potential

(a) At 1 ms the membrane is in the absolute refractory period. Its Na+ channels are open or inactivated. Doubling the stimulus does not help, because a stimulus works by opening closed channels, and there are none. Nothing happens.

(b) At 4 ms the membrane is in the relative refractory period. Two things work against the stimulus. Open K+ channels are letting positive charge leave as fast as the stimulus brings it in, and the membrane starts from about −80 mV, so it has farther to go. A stimulus of twice the normal strength can still reach threshold. The action potential that results is smaller than normal, because only the Na+ channels that have reset can open.

Everyday comparison: a toilet that has just been flushed. While the tank is empty, pressing the handle does nothing, however hard you press. While it is refilling, you can flush again, but you get a weaker flush.

Worked problem 1

What refractory periods do, and the fastest a neuron can fire

  • They keep action potentials separate. A second one cannot begin until the first is nearly over, so action potentials never overlap or add together the way graded potentials do.
  • They set a maximum firing frequency. If each action potential has to be followed by an absolute refractory period, only so many fit into one second.
  • They make conduction one way. The membrane just behind a traveling action potential is refractory, so the action potential cannot turn around. Slide 9 shows this.

maximum frequency = 1 ÷ absolute refractory period

Put the refractory period in seconds, and the answer comes out in action potentials per second.

A frequency in events per second is also written in hertz, Hz.

What it says: one action potential has to finish its absolute refractory period before the next can start, so the refractory period is the shortest possible spacing.

An axon has an absolute refractory period of 2 ms. What is the largest number of action potentials it can send in one second? What if the refractory period were 1 ms?

Solved

  1. Put the refractory period into seconds. 2 ms = 0.002 s.
  2. One action potential needs 0.002 s before the next one can start, so the most that fit into one second is 1 ÷ 0.002 = 500 action potentials per second.
  3. Repeat it for an absolute refractory period of 1 ms. 1 ÷ 0.001 = 1,000 per second. A shorter refractory period gives a higher ceiling.
  4. Read the answer as a sentence: however strong the stimulus is, this axon cannot send more than 500 action potentials in one second, because for 2 ms after each one its sodium channels cannot open.
  5. This is a ceiling and not a typical rate. Most neurons fire far below it, commonly well under 100 per second, because the relative refractory period comes next and a stimulus has to be unusually strong to fire the axon during it.
07

Coding

Every action potential is the same size, so strength is coded by frequency

  • A stronger stimulus opens more channels in the receptor ending, so it produces a larger graded potential, and one that lasts longer.
  • At the trigger zone, a graded potential that is still above threshold when the absolute refractory period ends fires another action potential. The larger it is, the sooner in the relative refractory period it can do that. So a larger, longer graded potential produces more action potentials per second.
  • The height of each action potential does not change. What changes is how many there are in a given time. This is frequency coding. The ceiling is the one you just calculated.
  • At the axon terminal, each action potential releases about the same amount of neurotransmitter. More action potentials per second release more neurotransmitter, and that is how the next cell receives the information about strength. The length of the burst codes how long the stimulus lasted.
08

Coding

A stronger stimulus also recruits more neurons

  • A single axon has only one way to report a stronger stimulus: fire more often. The nervous system as a whole has a second way. A stronger stimulus reaches more receptors, including ones with a higher threshold, so more neurons fire. This is called recruitment, or population coding.
  • The brain reads both: how fast each axon is firing, and how many axons are firing.

It is near its ceiling

Its ceiling is 500 per second, so it cannot double its rate. At most it can go from 400 to 500, and in practice it gets there only with a very strong stimulus, because it would have to fire at the very start of each relative refractory period.

The brain still finds out, because the stronger stimulus recruits receptors that were silent before. Frequency coding in one axon runs out at high intensities, and recruitment extends the range.

09

Conduction

Local current fires the next patch of membrane

  • In the active zone, Na+ has entered and the inside of the membrane is positive. The neighboring stretches are still negative inside. Positive charge flows along the inside of the axon toward the negative regions on both sides. This flow of charge is called local current.
  • Ahead of the action potential, the local current depolarizes resting membrane. When that patch reaches threshold, its own Na+ channels open and it fires a full action potential. That patch then does the same for the patch after it.
  • Behind the action potential, the local current arrives at membrane that fired a moment ago. Its Na+ channels are inactivated and its K+ channels are open. It is refractory, so it cannot fire. By the time it has recovered, the active zone has moved too far away to reach it.
  • That is why an action potential that starts at the trigger zone travels only toward the axon terminal.

  • So the action potential that reaches the axon terminal is not the one that left the trigger zone. It is a copy, made fresh by each patch of membrane along the way. That is what regenerated means, and it is why the signal does not fade.
  • A row of falling dominoes is a fair comparison on two points. Each domino falls with its own stored energy, so the last one falls as hard as the first. And a domino that has fallen cannot fall again until someone sets it back up, which is its refractory period.

Both ways

Both ways. The membrane on both sides of the electrode is at rest, with channels closed and ready, so local current fires both neighbors, and two action potentials travel away from the electrode in opposite directions. Each one still cannot turn around, because the membrane behind it is refractory.

The axon itself has no preferred direction. In the body, conduction is one way because action potentials normally start at one end, at the trigger zone, and from there the only resting membrane is ahead.

11

Conduction velocity

Two things decide how fast an axon conducts

1. The diameter of the axon

  • Local current flows through the cytoplasm inside the axon. A wider axon has more room for charge to flow, so its internal resistance is lower, in the same way that a wide pipe carries water more easily than a narrow one.
  • With lower resistance, the local current reaches farther ahead before it fades, so membrane farther down the axon is brought to threshold sooner. A larger diameter gives a higher velocity.

2. How much current leaks out through the membrane

  • Current that leaks out across the membrane is lost to the job of depolarizing the next patch. Myelin is many layers of glial cell membrane wrapped around the axon. It raises the resistance of the membrane, so very little current leaks out, and it also means less charge is needed to change the voltage across that stretch. Both effects let the local current spread farther and faster.

Predict

An animal needs a fast axon for an escape reflex. It could make the axon very wide, or it could wrap an ordinary axon in myelin. Which gives more speed for the space it takes up?

Myelin, by a wide margin

The squid has no myelin, so it uses diameter. Its giant axon is about 0.5 mm across, which is 500 μm, and it conducts at about 25 m/s. Your largest myelinated axons are about 20 μm across, myelin included, and conduct at up to about 120 m/s.

Cross sectional area goes with the square of the diameter. (500 ÷ 20)2 = 252 = 625. About 625 of your myelinated axons fit into the space of one squid giant axon, and each of them is nearly five times faster.

That matters for a nerve running down a limb. The sciatic nerve carries many thousands of fast axons in a bundle about as thick as a finger. Without myelin, a nerve with the same number of equally fast axons could not fit in your leg.

12

Conduction velocity

Continuous conduction and saltatory conduction

  • In an unmyelinated axon, voltage gated channels are spread along the whole membrane, and every patch has to depolarize and fire in turn. This is continuous conduction. Opening channels takes time at every patch, so it is slow.
  • In a myelinated axon, the voltage gated Na+ channels are concentrated at the nodes of Ranvier, the short bare gaps between segments of myelin. Under the myelin there are very few channels, and no action potential happens there.
  • Local current from one node flows inside the axon, under the myelin, and reaches the next node still strong enough to bring it to threshold. The action potential is regenerated only at the nodes. This is saltatory conduction, from the Latin word for jumping.
  • Nothing actually jumps. What travels between the nodes is local current, which is a very fast spread of charge along the inside of the axon. The time is spent at the nodes, where channels have to open, and there are far fewer of those stops.
  • It also costs less. Na+ enters and K+ leaves only at the nodes, so far fewer ions cross, and the Na+/K+ pump uses less ATP to move them back.
13

Conduction velocity

Real axons conduct at anywhere from 0.5 to 120 meters per second

  • Nerve fibers are sorted into classes by diameter and myelin. You do not need to memorize the letters. Learn the two ends: large myelinated fibers conduct at up to about 120 m/s, and small unmyelinated fibers at about 0.5 to 2 m/s.
  • What each class carries makes sense. Commands to skeletal muscle and information about body position use the fastest fibers. Slow, aching pain and warmth use the slowest.

Myelin first, then diameter

Large myelinated, about 15 μm: roughly 90 m/s. Small myelinated, about 3 μm: roughly 18 m/s. Large unmyelinated, about 1.2 μm: about 1 to 2 m/s. Small unmyelinated, about 0.4 μm: about 0.5 m/s.

Notice that the small myelinated fiber is about ten times faster than the largest unmyelinated fiber in a human nerve. Within each group, the larger diameter is faster.

Two rough rules give these numbers. For a myelinated axon, velocity in m/s is about 6 times the diameter in μm. For an unmyelinated axon, it is about the square root of the diameter in μm. Both are approximations.

Worked problem 2

From your toe to your spinal cord

You stub your toe. The sensory axons from the toe run about 1 m to the spinal cord. How long does the first action potential take to get there in a large myelinated fiber conducting at 100 m/s? In a small unmyelinated fiber conducting at 1 m/s?

time = distance ÷ velocity

Distance in meters and velocity in meters per second give time in seconds. Multiply by 1,000 for milliseconds.

What it says: a signal that travels faster spends less time on the way. For a fixed distance, ten times the velocity means one tenth of the time.

Then use the chart on the last slide to explain why a stubbed toe hurts twice.

Solved

  1. The relationship is time = distance ÷ velocity.
  2. Large myelinated fiber: 1 m ÷ 100 m/s = 0.010 s = 10 ms.
  3. Small unmyelinated fiber: 1 m ÷ 1 m/s = 1 s. That is 100 times longer, for the same stretch of leg.
  4. Now the two kinds of pain fiber. A delta fibers are thin and myelinated. At about 20 m/s, 1 m takes 1 ÷ 20 = 0.05 s = 50 ms. C fibers are unmyelinated. At about 1 m/s, 1 m takes 1 s.
  5. So when you stub your toe, the action potentials in the A delta fibers reach the spinal cord about a second before the ones in the C fibers. You feel a sharp, well located pain first, and a dull, aching, spreading pain about a second later. These are called first pain and second pain. Both sets of action potentials started at the same moment.
  6. The withdrawal reflex uses the faster fibers, which is why your foot has already moved before the ache begins.

Change the conditions

Remove the myelin from part of an axon

  • Where myelin is lost, local current leaks out through the bare membrane, so less of it reaches the next node. That node takes longer to reach threshold, and conduction slows. If too little current arrives, the node never reaches threshold and conduction fails.
  • The bare stretch cannot make up for it, because it was under myelin and has very few voltage gated Na+ channels of its own.

Predict

A person with multiple sclerosis takes a hot bath, and body temperature rises from 37.0 °C (98.6 °F) to 37.5 °C (99.5 °F). Vision blurs and the legs feel weak. An hour after cooling down, both are back to where they were. What changed at the nodes?

Channels close faster when they are warm

Warming speeds up the gating of ion channels. The Na+ channels at a node inactivate sooner, so each action potential is briefer and sends less current on to the next node.

A healthy node receives several times more current than it needs, so this makes no difference. A node beyond a demyelinated stretch is receiving barely enough. A little less current and it does not reach threshold, so conduction stops there. Cooling brings it back. No new damage has occurred.

Nursing

When myelin is damaged, getting too hot can bring a person's symptoms back for a while.

Example: A patient with multiple sclerosis may notice blurred vision or weak legs after a hot shower, exercise or a fever. This is called Uhthoff's phenomenon. It fades when the body cools and it does not mean new damage, so nurses teach patients ways to stay cool and to report symptoms that do not fade.

Radiologic technology

An MRI scan can show the patches where myelin has been lost.

Example: In multiple sclerosis, MRI of the brain and spinal cord shows the demyelinated patches, called plaques or lesions, as bright spots in the white matter on T2 and FLAIR images. MRI is the main imaging test used to diagnose the disease and to follow it over time.

Medicine

Doctors can measure how fast a nerve conducts.

Example: In a nerve conduction study a nerve is stimulated at two points along a limb and the response is timed. Healthy arm nerves conduct at roughly 50 to 60 m/s. A large drop in velocity points to damaged myelin, as in Guillain-Barré syndrome. A smaller response at normal speed points to lost axons. You will make this measurement in lab.

Respiratory therapy

If the nerves to the breathing muscles lose their myelin, breathing becomes weak.

Example: In Guillain-Barré syndrome the immune system strips myelin from peripheral nerves, and these can include the phrenic nerves to the diaphragm. Respiratory therapists measure vital capacity and inspiratory force at the bedside, because falling numbers show that the breathing muscles are weakening.

Worked problem 3

Change the potassium in the blood

Plasma K+ is the outside K+ for every neuron and muscle cell. For 2.5 mM, 4 mM and 7 mM, work out how far the resting potential is from threshold, and predict what happens to excitability.

Hyperkalemia has two stages. Explain both, and say what happens to the sodium channels between them.

Solved

  1. Normal, 4 mM: the resting potential is about −70 mV and threshold is about −55 mV, so the gap is 15 mV. A graded potential has to depolarize the trigger zone by 15 mV to fire the neuron.
  2. Hypokalemia, 2.5 mM: from the resting potential lecture, the resting potential is about −76 mV. The gap to threshold is −55 − (−76) = 21 mV. The same graded potential that used to reach threshold now falls short, so neurons and skeletal muscle are less excitable. The patient is weak.
  3. Hyperkalemia, 7 mM: the resting potential is about −64 mV. The gap is −55 − (−64) = 9 mV. At first a smaller graded potential is enough, so cells are more excitable.
  4. Now add what you know about the sodium channel. A membrane that stays depolarized holds some of its voltage gated Na+ channels in the inactivated state, and an inactivated channel cannot open. The longer and the further the membrane is depolarized, the fewer channels are closed and ready.
  5. With too few channels ready, the action potential rises slowly and is small, and if enough are inactivated the cell cannot fire at all. So hyperkalemia makes cells more excitable first and then less excitable, and what changes in between is the number of Na+ channels that are available.
  6. The Goldman equation gives −71 mV for the normal resting potential with the values from that lecture. It is rounded to −70 mV here. Books also vary by a few millivolts on threshold.
18

Change the conditions

Change the calcium in the blood

  • Ca2+ ions in the extracellular fluid bind to the outside of the voltage gated Na+ channel, and while they are bound the channel needs a larger depolarization to open.
  • With low extracellular Ca2+, fewer are bound and the channels open more easily. Threshold moves closer to the resting potential. Nerves begin to fire without being stimulated, and the muscles they supply contract. The result is tingling, muscle cramps and sustained spasm, called tetany.
  • With high extracellular Ca2+, the reverse: channels are harder to open, and nerve and muscle are less excitable.

Two different pools of calcium

The Ca2+ that makes skeletal muscle contract is stored inside the muscle cell, in the sarcoplasmic reticulum. A low plasma Ca2+ hardly changes that store. What it changes first is the Na+ channels of nerves, which fire on their own and drive the muscle. You will see the muscle side of this in the muscle weeks.

Two bedside signs show the extra excitability. In Chvostek's sign, tapping the facial nerve in front of the ear makes the face twitch. In Trousseau's sign, a blood pressure cuff kept inflated on the arm for about three minutes brings on a spasm of the hand and wrist.

This also explains a step from the crush injury in the resting potential lecture. Calcium given into a vein during hyperkalemia does not change potassium. It moves threshold away from the depolarized resting potential and restores the gap.

Nursing

Low calcium in the blood makes nerves fire when they should not.

Example: After thyroid or parathyroid surgery the parathyroid glands may stop working for a time, and plasma calcium can fall. Nurses watch for tingling around the mouth and in the fingers, muscle cramps, and a positive Chvostek or Trousseau sign, and report them early.

Radiologic technology

Breathing too fast can make the lips and fingers tingle.

Example: An anxious patient who hyperventilates in the scanner breathes off carbon dioxide, and the blood becomes more alkaline. More calcium then binds to plasma proteins, so free calcium falls. The patient feels tingling lips and fingers and sometimes cramping hands. Coaching slow breathing reverses it.

Medicine

Stored blood contains a chemical that ties up calcium.

Example: Blood for transfusion is kept from clotting with citrate, which binds calcium. During a massive transfusion the citrate can lower the patient's free calcium enough to affect nerve, muscle and heart, so ionized calcium is measured and replaced as needed.

Respiratory therapy

Very low calcium can make the muscles of the voice box go into spasm.

Example: Severe hypocalcemia can cause laryngospasm, which is heard as stridor, a high pitched noise on breathing in. A ventilator set to move too much air causes a respiratory alkalosis, and that lowers free calcium in the same way as hyperventilation.

Worked problem 4

Block the sodium channel, or lower the sodium outside

An axon is bathed in fluid with half the normal Na+, 72.5 mM where it was 145 mM. Inside stays at 15 mM. Calculate the new ENa and predict what happens to the peak of the action potential. Then compare that with an axon whose Na+ channels are blocked.

  • Local anesthetics such as lidocaine cross the membrane, enter the voltage gated Na+ channel from the inside of the cell, and block the pore. Tetrodotoxin, from the last lecture, blocks the same channel from the outside. Either way, the channel cannot pass Na+.

Solved

  1. Normal sodium: ENa = 61.5 × log10(145 ÷ 15) = 61.5 × 0.985 = +61 mV.
  2. Half the outside sodium: ENa = 61.5 × log10(72.5 ÷ 15) = 61.5 × 0.684 = +42 mV. Halving the outside concentration lowers ENa by 61.5 × log10(2) = 18.5 mV.
  3. At the peak of an action potential the membrane is far more permeable to Na+ than to anything else, so the peak follows ENa. When ENa drops by about 19 mV, the peak drops by about the same amount, from about +30 mV to about +10 mV. Hodgkin and Katz did this experiment on the squid giant axon in 1949, and the peak fell by about as much as the Nernst equation predicts.
  4. The resting potential changes only a little, because the resting membrane is barely permeable to Na+. You saw the same thing in the resting potential lecture.
  5. The action potential is smaller, but it is still there. The channels still open, Na+ still enters, and each patch of membrane still fires the next. With a local anesthetic or tetrodotoxin the channels cannot open at all, so there is no action potential to conduct.
20

The problem, explained

Where the message stopped

  1. The receptors and the first stretch of axon work normally

    The drill still opens channels in the pain receptor endings, graded potentials still form, and action potentials still start and travel toward the brainstem. The drug is not there.

  2. In the injected stretch, the Na+ channels cannot open

    Lidocaine crosses the axon membrane and blocks voltage gated Na+ channels from the inside. It binds most readily to channels that are open or inactivated, so axons that are firing are blocked sooner.

  3. The action potential cannot be regenerated there

    Local current from the last working patch still flows into the blocked stretch, but no channels open, so it is only a graded depolarization. It fades within a few millimeters. Nothing reaches the brainstem, so there is no pain.

  4. Small fibers are blocked before large ones

    To stop a myelinated axon, the drug has to block about three nodes in a row. In thin A delta fibers the nodes are close together, so a short stretch of drug covers enough of them. In thick A beta fibers, which carry pressure, the nodes are up to a millimeter or two apart, and the same stretch of drug may not cover three. C fibers are thin and have no myelin to keep the drug out.

Treat it as an experiment

Problem: action potentials from the tooth reach the brain. Target variable: the number of Na+ channels that can open in one stretch of axon. Intervention: a drug that blocks them. Mechanism: no regeneration in that stretch. Predicted effect: pain fibers stop first, pressure and motor fibers later, and everything returns when the drug diffuses away, because nothing was damaged.

Every excitable cell has these channels

Voltage gated Na+ channels are in every neuron and in heart muscle. If the drug reaches them in the blood, the first effects are in the brain: numbness of the tongue and around the mouth, ringing in the ears, and then seizures. In the heart, conduction slows and the rhythm can fail. This is why a local anesthetic is kept local, and one reason a drug that narrows blood vessels, epinephrine, is often mixed with it. The narrowed vessels carry the anesthetic away more slowly, so it also lasts longer.

The order small fibers first, large fibers last is the usual teaching rule and it is a simplification. Where a fiber lies within the nerve and how often it is firing also matter.

Nursing

A numbed part of the body cannot warn its owner that it is being hurt.

Example: After a nerve block or an epidural, a patient cannot feel heat, pressure or an awkward position in the numb area, and the muscles may be weak. Nurses protect the limb, and check that feeling and strength have returned before the patient walks. After dental work, patients are told not to chew until the numbness is gone, so they do not bite the lip or cheek.

Radiologic technology

Numbing the skin takes away sharp pain, but the patient still feels pushing.

Example: Before an image guided biopsy, a joint injection for an arthrogram, or a line placement, lidocaine is injected into the skin. Technologists tell the patient to expect pressure and movement but not sharp pain, which is exactly what the fiber sizes predict.

Medicine

The same drug that numbs a nerve can also quiet an irritable heart.

Example: Lidocaine blocks voltage gated sodium channels in heart muscle as well as in nerve, and it is used as an antiarrhythmic for some ventricular rhythms. For the same reason, too much local anesthetic in the blood is dangerous to the brain and the heart.

Respiratory therapy

Numbing the throat turns off the gag and the cough for a while.

Example: Before a bronchoscopy, lidocaine is sprayed or nebulized onto the airway so the scope can pass without coughing. Until the gag reflex returns, the patient takes nothing by mouth, because food or drink could enter the airway without triggering a cough.

Putting it together

The whole lecture on one map

  • The action potential itself carries no information about strength, because it is always the same size. The information is in how many arrive per second and in how many axons are carrying them.
  • It arrives full size because every patch of membrane makes it again, and it arrives quickly if the axon is wide and myelinated.
  • Every perturbation today acted on the same thing: how many voltage gated Na+ channels are closed and ready to open. Hyperkalemia inactivates them. Calcium changes how easily they open. A local anesthetic blocks them. Demyelination reduces the current that reaches them.
