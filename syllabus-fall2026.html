03. Course description and learning outcomes

Catalog description

Human Physiology introduces the physiological principles of function, integration, regulation and homeostasis of the human body. It includes topics at the molecular, cellular, tissue, and organ levels found within all eleven organ systems. Laboratory includes activities such as EEGs, EMGs, EKGs, blood tests, spirometric measurements, urinalysis, digestion experiments, and many others, simulated in this online section. The clinical aspects of evaluating and maintaining health are also discussed in lecture and laboratory.

This is a physiology course. We are after mechanism: what the body must accomplish, how it accomplishes it, and what happens when conditions change. Where anatomy appears, it appears because it explains a mechanism. What runs through all of it is clinical context, because a mechanism you have seen matter to a real patient is a mechanism you remember.

Student learning outcomes

By the end of this course you should be able to:

  • Critical thinking. Collect, analyze and evaluate scientific data and show you understand it.
  • Scientific awareness. Understand the purpose of scientific inquiry and how basic scientific principles and the scientific method apply.
  • Communication. Use language and nonverbal communication to describe and present scientific data and results to an audience.

These three outcomes are the official ones. The Competency Packet, 268 specific things you will be able to do, each one a single skill, sits underneath them, and every exam question in this course traces back to that list. Nothing is graded that is not on it. The third outcome, communication, is exactly what the draw-and-teach midterms practice.

04. How this course works, and why it is built this way

This is a fully online course, which means nobody schedules your hours for you. So the course gives you a fixed weekly shape to pour them into, and inside that shape it gives you real choices about how you learn. Both halves of that sentence matter, and the rest of this section is about why.

One path, several ways to walk it, one standard at the end

Every week has the same eight steps in the same order. You will find those eight steps as a module in Canvas and as a week page on the course website, with identical material in both, so you can work wherever you prefer. You never have to work out where to go next or what this week is about. What you do choose, every week, is how you get there.

You do not chooseYou do choose
Where to go nextWhich representation helps you learn: video, notes, textbook, or a combination
What the week is aboutHow you retrieve: drawing, brain dump, explaining out loud, rebuilding the process
What the competencies areWhich application case you solve, and which room it is set in
What is due, or whenWhich support you use, and when in the week you work
What you have to be able to do at the endWhat you change when something is not working

That balance is deliberate. A course with no choice in it ignores that you are all arriving from different places. A course that is nothing but choice hands you a menu and leaves you to work out what mattered. This one fixes the destination and opens up the route.

What mastery means here

If I am going to tell you that you choose how you learn, I owe you a reliable way to find out whether your choice actually produced learning. Otherwise choice quietly becomes "I watched the video, therefore I am done", and that is not the same thing at all.

So here is the definition this course runs on. You have not mastered something because you watched it, read it, highlighted it, or recognized it when you saw it. You have mastered a competency when you can retrieve what you know without your resources and use it to solve a problem you have not seen before.

LevelWhat it sounds like
1. I recognize itI have seen this before. It looks familiar.
2. I can recall itI can explain or reconstruct it without looking at anything.
3. I can use itI can apply it to a problem I have not seen before.
4. I can reason with itI can predict what happens when the system changes, and explain why.

In this course, mastery means levels 3 and 4, with recall as the foundation that makes either possible. Recognition is not on the list. It is the level that feels like understanding while you are reading and disappears the moment the notes are closed.

The Mastery Check, and why it has no points

Every week ends with a Mastery Check. It is not a quiz, and it is not called one, because the word quiz makes everyone think about points instead of about information. It is a real practice exam that you build yourself, on the week you are in.

  • No points and no penalty. You turn the report in so I can see you are practicing, and no score from it reaches your grade. Its only job is to tell you whether the way you chose to learn that week worked.
  • You build it yourself. Open the practice exam, tick the week you are in, choose 30 questions, and go. That takes about five seconds and it means the exam is yours rather than one handed to you.
  • Do not use your notes the first time. If you look things up it will tell you what you can find. You already know you can find things. What you need to know is what you can retrieve.
  • Unlimited attempts, and every one is a different exam. The bank holds about 5,600 questions, so sitting the same week twice measures whether you learned the physiology rather than whether you remembered the questions. Try, find the gap, relearn it, try again.
  • Five kinds of question, in the same mix every time. Multiple choice, multiple answer, true or false where you have to make the false statement true, brain dumps you check against a rubric, and application questions. Roughly nine in ten sit above simple recall, because recognizing a right answer and being able to do physiology are not the same skill.
  • You rate your confidence before you check. That is what lets the report separate a gap, where you were unsure and wrong, from a misconception, where you were sure and wrong. Those need completely different fixes, and the confident wrong ones are the expensive kind because they do not go away on their own.
  • You do get a score, and every attempt is logged. That is on purpose. The score is not what you are graded on, but it is how improvement gets measured: the log keeps every exam you take, so you and I can both see whether the numbers are moving over a run of attempts rather than guessing from one.
  • The score is not the most useful part of the report, though. Underneath it you get your score by week, the competencies to review, the ones you got wrong while you felt confident, and how this attempt compares with your last one on the same weeks. That is what tells you what to do next.
  • One exam does not cover a week. Thirty questions sample it. A competency you have never been asked about is not one you have proved, which is the real reason to come back and build another.

The report lists the competencies to review and never the questions or the answers, so you can upload it. That is the weekly practice log, and it carries no points either: I read it to see who is practicing and who might want a hand before an exam, not to grade anybody.

Then the discussion does the last piece. It does not ask what study strategy you used. It asks what the check revealed, what you did about it, and what happened when you tried again. That is evidence of your thinking, and it is gradeable without anyone having to grade your learning process itself.

Where the effort actually goes

Your first meeting with every concept is the weekly note sheet, built from that week's competencies. It is not graded, and doing it before anything else is the single habit that makes the rest of the week work. Come to the videos and the reading with questions already in hand and they answer something. Come cold and they wash over you.

The spaced recall cards, also ungraded, keep September's material present in October when Midterm 1 asks for it.

And the application cases are where the physiology stops being a subject and becomes something you do. You pick the room. The reasoning is the same in all of them.

What the Mastery Check sends you to do

The check is the honest measurement of the week, and it sends you one of two ways. You can take it as soon as you have studied, and most students take a first one before they post to the discussion, because the discussion asks what it told you.

  • All competencies solid. You are done for the week. Do not keep reviewing material you can already use, it is the least valuable hour available to you.
  • Not yet. You will be told exactly which competency, and given a different resource to try. Retrieve and practice that one thing, then build another check and sit it again.

Going back through a competency twice is not falling behind. It is the course working the way it was built to work, and it costs far less than arriving at a midterm having found out too late.

07. Grading and assessment

Four categories, and each one is named for what it asks you to do rather than for the format it arrives in. The work that carries the most weight is the work that proves the reasoning is yours.

Show Me What You Know, 35 percent

  • These are not multiple choice tests. Each midterm asks you to draw a physiological pathway and teach it to me on video, out loud, with no notes. Draw the mechanism, label it, explain each step and say why it happens.
  • The format is chosen for a reason. Recognizing the right answer on a list and being able to build the answer from nothing are different skills, and only one of them is any use to you in a clinical program.
  • Midterm 1 covers Weeks 1 to 7. Midterm 2 covers Weeks 9 to 14. Each is worth 17.5 percent.
  • Each is a window, not an hour. Midterm 1 opens Monday, October 26 at 8:00 am and closes Wednesday, October 28 at 10:00 pm, at the front of Week 8. Midterm 2 opens Monday, December 14 at 8:00 am and closes Wednesday, December 16 at 10:00 pm, at the front of Week 15. Each window sits at the front of its week so the block before it is finished and the week it sits in is still open to you afterwards.
  • You will not be surprised by the format. Every week's study step is this exact task in miniature, ungraded: draw the mechanism from memory and teach it out loud.

Investigate It, 25 percent

  • One lab a week, aligned to that week's material. You generate and interpret real physiological output: a trace, a curve, a set of values, a calculation.
  • Every lab uses the same reasoning structure, and it is the structure a clinical write up uses too: question, prediction, evidence, interpretation, conclusion. You write the prediction before you see the data. That order is not optional, and it is where most of the learning is.
  • Where the lab uses PhysioEx, it takes both halves. PhysioEx must show complete in Pearson, and you must turn in the worksheet and data record. The points live on the worksheet. PhysioEx carries no points and is only the gate. Clicking through PhysioEx alone earns nothing, and a worksheet with no PhysioEx behind it does not get graded.

Use It, 25 percent

  • Every week gives you three or four application cases set in different rooms: nursing, medicine, radiology, and exercise or allied health. You choose one. They differ in context. They do not differ in rigor, and every one of them assesses the same underlying competency.
  • Whichever case you choose, it asks the same five things: name the physiological variable and the system regulating it, predict what is happening, explain the mechanism step by step, interpret the evidence you were given, and justify your conclusion including what would change it.
  • The weekly cases are 20 percent of the course, turned in each Sunday.
  • The other 5 percent is your patient chart, the capstone. You keep one paper chart on one patient for the whole term: every week you add that week's numbers to the flowsheets, add to the problem list, draw the control pathway, and write down what you think is going on. Nothing is collected week to week. The finished chart is turned in once, on Wednesday, December 16, and by then you are holding a full term of your own reasoning across every system in the course.
  • This is the category where the choice is real and the standard is not. You pick the room. The physiology you have to be able to do in it is fixed.

Think About It, 15 percent

  • One post a week, not two. It carries the physiology and your thinking about it together, in one post.
  • What it asks for is a decision, the evidence, and the adjustment. What did your Mastery Check reveal? What did you do about it? What happened when you tried again? And one question about the physiology itself, which changes each week.
  • Your Mastery Check and practice exam numbers are yours. Share them in the discussion if you want to, or keep them private and talk about your experience instead. What is worth reading is what you changed and whether it worked. Your practice log comes to me separately, as an assignment, so I can reach out if I see you struggling.
  • Your initial post is due Friday at 10:00 pm so there is something to reply to. Peer replies are due Sunday at 10:00 pm.

What is not graded, and why that is deliberate

Several things you will do every week carry no points at all.

  • The note sheet and the retrieval work. This is where you find out what you do not know. Grading it would push you to make it look finished instead of making it honest, and an honest sheet with gaps in it is worth more to you than a tidy one.
  • The practice items and the book problems. Predict, commit, check, correct, explain. Getting these wrong is the point of doing them. The book problems sit in the reading and study steps with two routes through them: work them forwards if you have a way in, or work them backwards, reading a solution and writing beside each line why that step is there before reproducing it from blank paper. The second route is for the problems you open and cannot start. It is not the easy option, it is usually the slower one, and it is a real way to learn a hard topic rather than a way around it.
  • The Mastery Check. No points, no penalty, and as many attempts as you want. It does score you, and it reports competency by competency so you know what to go back to. The score is for you; none of it reaches the gradebook. See Section 04 for why it works that way.

None of this is optional in any way that matters. It is the entire route to the four graded categories above, and the students who skip it are the ones who arrive at a midterm able to recognize a pathway and unable to draw one.

The workload, honestly

California community colleges follow the Carnegie standard, and by the official course outline this 4-unit course is 216 learning hours: 144 hours of instruction plus 72 of study. Over our 15 weeks, that is an official floor of about 14 hours a week. A floor is what it takes to pass, roughly, it is not a promise of what it takes to do well.

Realistically, plan on 16 to 20 hours a week to genuinely learn this material: to walk into a midterm able to draw a pathway and teach it with no notes, not just recognize it.

If you need an A or a B, budget 20 hours, and sometimes closer to 30. Most of you are headed for nursing or other health programs that require an A or B in this course; a C will not get you in. What it actually takes depends on the grade you are going for, your experience as a student, and your background. If you have not had anatomy yet, or you are taking it at the same time as this course, you will be teaching yourself the parts of the body on your own time while learning how they work in mine, and that is real added hours. The Competency Packet lists exactly what the course assumes you arrive with, so you can see your own gaps in Week 1 instead of discovering them in Week 6.

I would rather you knew all of this in September than discovered it in October. Load the early weeks heavily. Physiology compounds, and so does falling behind in it. This is a mastery-based course: attending and turning things in does not by itself earn the grade. The study hours are what do.

10. Attendance, participation and enrollment

In an online course, participation is submitted work. There is no roll call. What counts as attending is the graded work coming in: your discussion post, your lab, and your weekly application case. The note sheet, the practice and the Mastery Check matter enormously to your learning, but they carry no points and I cannot certify attendance from them, so they are not what the college looks at. Staying enrolled and staying current are the same thing here.

  • Week 1 is mandatory. Turn in Week 1's graded work: the introduction discussion, the vision board discussion and the reference range lab. A student who submits nothing in the first weeks can be dropped as a no-show, and the seat goes to the waitlist.
  • Census is September 27. That is also the day the Weeks 2 and 3 block is due. I certify who is actively participating. If nothing has come in by then, I am required to report you as not attending, and you can be dropped. Do not let a registration date end your semester in the first month.
  • Get into Canvas at least three times a week and turn on announcement notifications, because that is where a schedule change or an exam-window note appears first.

Dropping the course is yours to do

If you decide to leave the course, you have to formally drop it yourself through your student portal. Do not assume I have done it for you, and do not assume it happens on its own. A course you walked away from but never dropped becomes an F you did not need to earn. The Fall 2026 deadlines are in Section 14.

11. Academic integrity and the use of AI

Academic honesty

Cheating and plagiarism are not tolerated in this course. A violation earns at least a zero on the work in question, may result in an F for the course, and is reported for disciplinary action under college policy. Complete your own work, and ask me if you are not sure where the line falls. Asking is always the cheaper option.

Handwritten means handwritten

Unless an assignment says otherwise, everything you turn in is handwritten and hand-drawn. Typed work earns a zero.

This is not nostalgia. Drawing a pathway by hand is how your brain builds it, and it is how the midterms work, so it is how the practice works. Read the directions on every assignment before you start; work that ignores the directions cannot be redone.

Using AI

For studying, AI is encouraged. Tools like ChatGPT and Claude are useful for the same things a good study partner is useful for: explaining a concept a second way, quizzing you, helping you plan your week. Teaching a concept to something that asks follow-up questions is one of the better ways to find out whether you actually have it.

For graded work, the rules are strict. What you turn in must be your own work, in your own hand, from your own head. Turning in AI-written work as yours is cheating, and no outside help of any kind is allowed during exams.

Here is the honest reason, beyond the rules: the midterms are the single largest piece of your grade at 35 percent, and they are you, on camera, teaching with no notes. A tool that does your weekly work for you is quietly taking the practice you will desperately want back in exam week. Nobody can outsource their own understanding.

The full picture, including how I use AI to build this course and the complete lists of where you can and cannot use it, is on the How AI is used in this course page.

No extra credit

There is no individual extra credit, and asking for it will not create any. If I ever offer extra credit, I offer it to the whole class at once.

12. Due dates, late work and make-ups

Everything is due Sunday at 10:00 pm. Discussions run ahead of that: the initial post is due Friday at 10:00 pm and peer replies Sunday at 10:00 pm. Up to 24 hours late: automatic 50% reduction. After 24 hours: zero, no make-up. There are no exceptions, not for family, travel, work, other obligations, or emergencies.

I know that sounds strict, so here is the honest reasoning. This course never requires you to be anywhere at any particular time. You can do every bit of this work at whatever hour of whatever day fits your life, and the next week is always open before you need it. That means the cushion for life's surprises is built in before the deadline, not after it. If you know a trip, a busy stretch at work, or a family event is coming, work ahead the week before. If something unexpected lands on a Saturday, the week's work should not have been waiting on that Saturday.

This is the trade the course asks of you: total freedom over your hours, in exchange for full responsibility for them. Nobody takes attendance, so nobody rescues a deadline either. Students who pick their work times at the start of the week and protect them do very well here. Students who plan to "find time Sunday" usually find something else instead.

You can work one week ahead. Two weeks are open at any time: the week you are in and the week after it. The Weeks 2 and 3 block counts as one, so while you are in it you can also see Week 4. If you know a hard stretch is coming, get the next week done early. You cannot open more than one week ahead, and that limit is deliberate: covering four weeks of physiology in a weekend feels productive and holds almost nothing. Weeks you have finished stay open all term.

Submit early. The internet fails, computers crash, and files refuse to upload, always at 11:58 pm. A submission that exists on Thursday cannot be lost on Sunday.

If something serious happens in your life, come and talk to me about your options in the course as a whole, including the withdrawal deadline in Section 14. What I cannot do is reopen a deadline for one student that everyone else met under the same conditions.
