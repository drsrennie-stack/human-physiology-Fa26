How your grade can move up

Scholar Points

Up to a 2.5 percent bump on your final course grade, earned by putting real hours into practising this material with other people. It is not extra credit, and the difference between the two matters.

2.5% maximum 30 hours for the full bump 3 hours a week counted

Why this is not extra credit

I do not offer extra credit. Extra credit is a back-end fix: something bolted on at the end of the term so a grade that went badly can be pulled up, usually by work that has little to do with the course. It rewards whoever is most behind and it teaches nobody anything.

Scholar Points work from the front end. They are not a rescue and they are not a second chance. They are what happens when you put the hours into the practice that makes this course easier while you are still taking it: saying the physiology out loud, teaching it to somebody, being quizzed by somebody who will not let you off. Students who do that tend to have a better grade before any bump is applied, and the bump is on top of that.

Which is the honest sales pitch for it. The 2.5 percent is real and I will apply it, but it is the smaller half of what you get. The larger half is that the exams get easier because you spent the term producing this material out loud instead of rereading it.

How it works

  • Thirty hours over the semester earns the full 2.5 percent. Fewer hours earn a proportion of it. Fifteen hours is 1.25 percent, six hours is half a percent, and so on. Nothing is all or nothing.
  • Three hours a week is the most that counts. You can do more and you are welcome to, but a single marathon weekend cannot substitute for showing up across the term. Spacing is the whole reason this works.
  • Hosting and attending count the same. Running a session is more work and it is also the better study hour, so it already pays you twice. It does not need a multiplier.
  • It is applied at the end of the semester, to your final course percentage, after everything else is in.
  • It can only help you. There is no version of this that costs you anything, and choosing not to do it is a perfectly reasonable choice that leaves your grade exactly where your work put it.

What counts as an hour

Time spent working the physiology with at least one other person: a Study With Me session, a study group you set up yourself, quizzing each other, teaching a competency at a whiteboard, doing brain dumps side by side, running a Kahoot, working the book problems together. It does not include time you spent alone, and it does not include doing graded work together, which is not allowed anyway.

Questions people ask

Is 2.5 percent enough to change my letter grade?

Sometimes, at a boundary. An 87.5 becomes a 90. It is not enough to rescue a grade that is well down, and it was never designed to, which is the difference between this and extra credit.

What if I can only manage ten hours?

Then you earn a third of it, which is roughly 0.83 percent, and you also spent ten hours practising physiology out loud. Both of those are worth having. There is no threshold you have to clear before anything counts.

Can I log time I studied alone?

No. Studying alone is most of what this course asks of you and it is already reflected in your grade through the work itself. This is specifically for the hours you spend working it with other people, because that is the part nobody does unless something nudges them.

Do you check?

I read the logs. I am not auditing timestamps, and an honour system that somebody games mostly cheats them out of the hours, which were the actual point. Put down what you did.

What if I host and two people come for twenty minutes?

Log the time you were there working. Twenty minutes is 0.33 hours, and small sessions are real sessions.
