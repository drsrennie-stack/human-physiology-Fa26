BIOL 005 · Week 4

Integration at the synapse

A single neuron in your spinal cord receives thousands of synapses. Some make it more likely to fire and some make it less likely, and at every moment the cell has one decision to make: send an action potential or stay silent. Today we work out how that decision is made.

  • We start with the two kinds of small potential a synapse can produce, the EPSP and the IPSP, and the ion channel behind each one.
  • Then we add them up at the trigger zone, with numbers, and decide in several cases whether the cell fires. A moving model lets you test your answers.
  • Last, we look at three ways the rules can be changed: inhibition of a single terminal, synapses that strengthen with use, and electrical synapses that use no transmitter at all.

Silverthorn chapter 9. Week 4 competencies 24 to 28.

The problem for today

A gardener steps on a rusty nail. A week later his jaw is clamped shut, and the slightest noise or touch throws his whole body into painful spasms. He is awake through all of it. His muscles, and the motor neurons that drive them, are structurally normal. A bacterial toxin from the wound has reached his spinal cord. What did it do there, so that an ordinary sound now makes every muscle contract at once?

03

The plan

From many synapses to one decision

  • Last lecture ended with one synapse: transmitter binds a receptor, a channel opens, and the postsynaptic membrane potential changes a little. That small change is a graded potential.
  • From the graded potential lecture you know two things about it. It gets smaller as it spreads, and graded potentials can add.
  • A neuron fires only if the membrane at its trigger zone reaches threshold, about −55 mV. The trigger zone is where the axon leaves the cell body.
  • Today fills in the middle of this map. Synaptic integration is the name for the whole process: the neuron adds all of its excitatory and inhibitory inputs and gives one answer.
04

Two kinds of input

A synapse can depolarize the cell or hold it back

  • An excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP) is a small depolarization. At most fast excitatory synapses in the brain, glutamate opens the AMPA receptor, a channel for small positive ions. Na+ enters, a little K+ leaves, and net positive charge comes in.
  • An inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP) makes the cell less likely to fire. One kind opens K+ channels, so K+ leaves and the membrane hyperpolarizes toward EK, −95 mV. The other kind opens Cl− channels. GABA-A and glycine receptors work this way.
  • The transmitter does not decide which one you get. The receptor and its channel decide. Acetylcholine excites skeletal muscle through one receptor and slows the heart through another.
05

Why it inhibits

A chloride channel inhibits even when the voltage barely moves

  • Use the rule from the resting potential lecture: opening channels for an ion moves the membrane toward that ion's equilibrium potential. With our concentrations ECl is −64 mV, a little above rest. Opening Cl− channels moves the membrane only slightly, and it moves it up.
  • It is still inhibition, for two reasons. The open channels hold the membrane near −64 mV, which is 9 mV below threshold. And when an EPSP lifts the membrane above −64 mV, Cl− enters through the open channels and cancels part of the positive charge. The second effect is called shunting inhibition.
  • Cells and books vary here. Many adult neurons keep Cl− inside nearer 5 to 7 mM, which puts ECl at −83 to −74 mV, and then the IPSP is a hyperpolarization. Silverthorn describes it that way. What matters in every case is that ECl is below threshold.

Nursing

Many calming and sleep medicines work by making the brain's own inhibitory synapses stronger, and breathing is controlled by neurons too.

Example: Benzodiazepines such as diazepam and midazolam bind the GABA-A receptor and make its Cl− channel open more often when GABA is present. A sedated patient is watched for a slow breathing rate and for how easily they wake.

Radiologic technology

Some patients are given a sedative so they can hold still for a scan, and a sedated patient has to be watched during the scan.

Example: A patient with claustrophobia may receive midazolam before an MRI, and young children are often sedated for one. Breathing and oxygen saturation are monitored while the patient is in the scanner.

Medicine

A seizure is too much excitation spreading through the brain, and the first drugs used to stop one strengthen inhibition.

Example: Benzodiazepines are the first drugs given for a seizure that does not stop on its own. They act at the GABA-A receptor, so inhibitory synapses hold more neurons below threshold.

Respiratory therapy

The medicines that keep a patient comfortable on a ventilator also quiet the neurons that drive breathing.

Example: Propofol and midazolam both act at the GABA-A receptor. Sedation is usually lightened before a spontaneous breathing trial, so the patient's own drive to breathe can be assessed.

Worked problem 1

Three recordings from one neuron: which channel opened?

An electrode in a neuron records three traces on different trials. Each starts at −70 mV. The first rises 3 mV, the second falls 4 mV, and the third stays flat. For each one, work backwards to the channel that opened and the ion that moved. Give the flat trace two different explanations.

What you need from the first lecture this week
Ion or channelEquilibrium potential
K+−95 mV
Cl−−64 mV with our numbers, lower in many neurons
AMPA receptor channel (Na+ and K+)about 0 mV
Na++61 mV

Solved

  1. The trace that rises 3 mV. The membrane moved toward a value above −70 mV, and the channel that does this at a fast excitatory synapse is a cation channel such as the AMPA receptor. It passes Na+ and K+. At −70 mV the force on Na+ is 131 mV inward and the force on K+ is only 25 mV outward, so far more Na+ enters than K+ leaves. Net positive charge enters, and the trace rises. This is an EPSP.
  2. The trace that falls 4 mV. The membrane moved toward a value below −70 mV. With our numbers only EK, at −95 mV, is below rest, so a K+ channel opened and K+ left the cell. A GABA-B receptor does this through a G protein. In a neuron that keeps less chloride inside, a Cl− channel could give the same trace, with Cl− entering. Either way it is an IPSP.
  3. The flat trace, explanation one. Nothing happened at the synapse. The presynaptic neuron did not fire, or it fired and released no transmitter, so no channel opened.
  4. The flat trace, explanation two. A Cl− channel opened in a cell whose chloride equilibrium potential is equal to its resting potential. There is no net force on Cl− at that voltage, so no net charge crosses and the voltage does not move, even though the channels are open.
  5. The second recording that separates them. Fire a nearby excitatory input at the same moment and compare the EPSP with the EPSP you get from that input alone. If the quiet synapse opened no channels, the EPSP is its usual size. If it opened Cl− channels, the EPSP is smaller, because as soon as the membrane rises above ECl, Cl− enters and cancels part of the positive charge. That is how an input can be inhibitory while the membrane potential barely moves.
07

The size problem

One EPSP is far too small to fire the cell

  • One excitatory synapse in the central nervous system typically gives an EPSP of roughly 0.5 mV at the cell body. The value varies from synapse to synapse.
  • Threshold is 15 mV above rest, so it would take about 30 of these arriving together, even if nothing were lost on the way.
  • At the neuromuscular junction one action potential always fires the muscle fiber. In the central nervous system no single synapse decides anything. A spinal motor neuron carries on the order of 10,000 synapses.

Predict

Graded potentials can add. Name two different ways a neuron could collect 15 mV from inputs that are each far smaller than that.

V at the trigger zone = −70 mV + (EPSPs arriving) − (IPSPs arriving)

Arriving means the size of each potential when it reaches the trigger zone, after it has shrunk with distance and with time.

The cell fires if this value reaches threshold, about −55 mV. Adding potentials as plain numbers is a simplification. Real neurons add a little less than the arithmetic says.

What it says: the trigger zone keeps a running sum of everything that reaches it, and only the total is compared with threshold.

08

Summation

Two ways to add: over time and over space

  • Temporal summation. One presynaptic neuron fires several times in quick succession. Each EPSP begins before the one before it has decayed, so each starts from a higher level.
  • Spatial summation. Several presynaptic neurons fire at about the same moment, and their EPSPs arrive at the trigger zone together.
  • Panel 1 is the control. The same input fired four times, 25 ms apart, adds almost nothing, because each EPSP has nearly gone before the next arrives.
  • Real neurons use both at once: many inputs, each firing a train of action potentials.

Worked problem 2

Temporal summation with numbers

One excitatory input gives an EPSP of 5.4 mV at the trigger zone. After its peak, an EPSP falls by half about every 10 ms. The neuron rests at −70 mV and its threshold is −55 mV. Does the cell fire if this one input fires every 25 ms? Every 10 ms? Every 5 ms?

peak after the next EPSP = 5.4 mV + (what is left of the earlier ones)

What is left after 10 ms is one half. After 5 ms it is about 0.71, and after 25 ms it is about 0.18.

What it says: each new EPSP adds to whatever has not yet decayed.

Solved

  1. How far is threshold? From −70 mV to −55 mV is 15 mV. One EPSP of 5.4 mV reaches −64.6 mV, which is 9.6 mV short.
  2. Every 25 ms. After 25 ms an EPSP has been halved two and a half times, so less than one fifth of it is left, about 1 mV. The second EPSP peaks near 6.4 mV above rest, and the later ones go no higher than about 6.6 mV. The cell does not fire however long the train continues.
  3. Every 10 ms. Exactly half is left each time. The peaks are 5.4, then 5.4 + 2.7 = 8.1, then 5.4 + 4.05 = 9.5, then 10.1 mV, and they level off below 5.4 × 2 = 10.8 mV. That is still 4 mV short of threshold.
  4. Every 5 ms. After 5 ms about 71 percent is left, because 0.71 × 0.71 = 0.5. The peaks are 5.4, 9.2, 11.9, 13.8 and then 15.2 mV. The fifth EPSP carries the trigger zone past 15 mV, and the cell fires.
  5. What has to be true about timing. The next EPSP has to arrive before the last one has decayed. The membrane potential takes roughly 10 ms to fall by half, so action potentials more than a few tens of milliseconds apart do not add, and action potentials a few milliseconds apart do. This is how the frequency code from the last lecture is read by the next cell: a faster train in one axon produces a larger depolarization at the trigger zone.
10

Location

Where a synapse sits changes how much it counts

  • A graded potential loses size as it spreads, because charge leaks out through the membrane along the way. A synapse far out on a dendrite therefore has less effect at the trigger zone than the same synapse close to the cell body.
  • Inhibitory synapses are often found on the cell body and on the first part of the axon. Every EPSP from every dendrite has to pass that point, so one well placed inhibitory synapse can cancel the combined effect of many excitatory ones.
  • The rule e−distance ÷ 500 µm is a teaching value. Real dendrites differ, and some have voltage gated channels that boost distant inputs.

Solved

  1. Case A, E1 alone. −70 + 5.4 = −64.6 mV. That is 9.6 mV short of threshold, so the cell does not fire.
  2. Case B, all four excitatory inputs at the same moment. 5.4 + 4.4 + 3.0 + 2.7 = 15.5 mV. −70 + 15.5 = −54.5 mV. That is above −55 mV, so the cell fires. This is spatial summation.
  3. Case C, the same four, with E4 moved out to 800 µm. E4 now arrives as 6 × 0.20 = 1.2 mV. The sum is 5.4 + 4.4 + 3.0 + 1.2 = 14.0 mV, which gives −56.0 mV. The cell does not fire. Nothing about the synapse changed except where it sits.
  4. Case D, all four as in case B, plus I1. I1 makes −5 mV at 250 µm and arrives as −5 × 0.61 = −3.0 mV. The sum is 15.5 − 3.0 = 12.5 mV, which gives −57.5 mV. The cell does not fire. One inhibitory input has canceled the effect of four excitatory ones.
  5. Case E, case D with the inhibitory terminal unable to release. The −3.0 mV is gone, the sum is back to 15.5 mV, and the cell fires again. Keep this case in mind for the patient.
  6. The simplification. Adding potentials as plain numbers is close to right for small potentials and is how these problems are worked in this course. In a real neuron the sum comes out a little smaller than the arithmetic, and an inhibitory synapse that opens Cl− channels on the cell body removes more than its own size suggests. The model on slide 13 shows that case.
12

Circuits

Divergence and convergence

  • In divergence, the axon of one neuron branches and synapses on many target cells, so one signal reaches many places.
  • In convergence, many presynaptic neurons synapse on one target cell, which integrates all of them. A spinal motor neuron is a good example: it receives sensory input, input from the brain and input from local inhibitory interneurons, and its one output decides whether a muscle contracts.
  • The largest example in the body is the Purkinje cell of the cerebellum, which receives on the order of 100,000 to 200,000 synapses. The number is approximate.

14

Control at one terminal

Presynaptic inhibition turns down one input only

  • In presynaptic inhibition a modulatory neuron ends on the axon terminal of another neuron. This is an axoaxonic synapse. Its transmitter reduces the Ca2+ that enters that terminal when an action potential arrives.
  • Less Ca2+ entering means fewer vesicles fuse, so less transmitter is released and the EPSP from that one input is smaller. The postsynaptic cell has not changed, so it responds to its other inputs exactly as before.
  • Postsynaptic inhibition acts on the receiving cell, so it reduces the effect of every input to that cell.
  • The advantage of presynaptic control is selectivity.

Nursing

The strongest pain medicines work partly by stopping pain nerve endings from passing their message on, and the same medicines slow breathing.

Example: Morphine and other opioids bind receptors on the terminals of pain fibers in the spinal cord. Less Ca2+ enters, less transmitter is released, and less of the pain signal reaches the next neuron. Opioid receptors on brainstem neurons slow breathing, so the breathing rate is checked, and naloxone blocks the receptor if it is needed.

Radiologic technology

A patient who was given an opioid for pain may be drowsy and breathing slowly when they arrive for imaging.

Example: A patient with a fracture often receives an opioid before their films or CT. Watch their breathing and how easily they wake, especially while they lie flat and alone in the scanner room.

Medicine

Pain medicine can be placed right where pain fibers make their first synapse, so a very small amount is enough.

Example: Opioids given into the epidural or spinal space act on the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, where pain fibers synapse. The body's own enkephalins act at the same receptors.

Respiratory therapy

An opioid overdose is a breathing emergency before it is anything else.

Example: Breathing becomes slow and shallow and carbon dioxide rises. The respiratory therapist supports ventilation with a bag and mask while naloxone is given to block the receptor.

Predict

A neuron receives three excitatory inputs, A, B and C. You need to cut the effect of A by half and leave B and C exactly as they are. You can put one inhibitory synapse anywhere. Where do you put it, and what goes wrong with the other choice?

On the terminal of input A

Put it on the axon terminal of A. It reduces Ca2+ entry in that terminal, A releases about half as much transmitter, and the EPSPs from B and C are unchanged.

An inhibitory synapse on the cell body fails the requirement. It lowers the effect of A, but it lowers B and C by just as much, because all three EPSPs pass the same stretch of membrane. In the model, press case 5 and compare it with case 4.

  • Presynaptic facilitation is the opposite change. The modulatory transmitter keeps the terminal depolarized a little longer, more Ca2+ enters, and more transmitter is released. It was worked out in the sea slug Aplysia, where serotonin closes K+ channels in a sensory terminal.

Plasticity

Synapses change with use

Short term changes, lasting from milliseconds to a few minutes
What you recordWhy
FacilitationDuring a short rapid train, each EPSP is larger than the one beforeCa2+ from one action potential has not been cleared when the next arrives, so more vesicles fuse
DepressionDuring a long rapid train, the EPSPs get smallerThe terminal is using vesicles faster than it can refill them
  • Both changes are in the presynaptic terminal, and both fade soon after the train stops.
  • Synaptic plasticity means that the strength of a synapse depends on its history. The same presynaptic action potential can produce a larger or a smaller EPSP than it did before.
  • Short term plasticity lasts only seconds to minutes. A memory lasts far longer than that, so there must also be changes that last.
  • Long term potentiation (LTP) is an increase in synaptic strength that lasts hours or longer. It was discovered in the hippocampus, a part of the brain needed for forming new memories, and the next two slides go through it step by step.

No. Look at the terminal first

The simplest explanation is synaptic depression: the terminal has released its readily available vesicles faster than it can replace them. The test is to wait. After a rest of seconds to a minute, the EPSP returns to its starting size, which a loss of receptors would not do.

17

Long term potentiation

How a glutamate synapse becomes stronger

  • Glutamate binds two receptors. The AMPA receptor opens at once and produces the ordinary EPSP. The NMDA receptor also binds glutamate, but near rest its channel is plugged by a Mg2+ ion from the outside.
  • A rapid burst of input produces a large depolarization through the AMPA receptors. A positive interior repels the Mg2+, the plug leaves, and Ca2+ enters through the NMDA channel.
  • Ca2+ is a second messenger. It activates protein kinases, which add more AMPA receptors to the membrane. The postsynaptic cell may also signal back so the terminal releases more glutamate.
  • Afterward the same single action potential gives a larger EPSP. The NMDA receptor opens only when glutamate is present and the cell is already strongly depolarized, which one action potential alone cannot do.
18

Long term potentiation

The experiment, and what it does and does not show

  • With NMDA receptors blocked, ordinary transmission is normal, because AMPA receptors carry it. The burst still causes a brief rise, which is short term plasticity in the terminal. The lasting increase does not happen.
  • Long term depression (LTD) is the reverse. Prolonged slow stimulation lets in a small amount of Ca2+, AMPA receptors are removed from the membrane, and the EPSP becomes smaller for a long time.
  • Most neuroscientists think changes like these are part of how learning and memory work, and blocking NMDA receptors in the hippocampus does impair some kinds of learning in animals. How a particular memory is stored is still being worked out.

AMPA, then NMDA

The AMPA receptor carries the ordinary, moment to moment signal. The NMDA receptor reports that the input was strong enough and well timed enough to be worth strengthening, and it reports it as Ca2+ entry.

19

A different kind of synapse

Electrical synapses pass current directly

  • At an electrical synapse two cells are joined by gap junctions. Each gap junction channel is built from proteins called connexins, six in each membrane, lined up to form one pore from the cytoplasm of one cell to the cytoplasm of the next.
  • Ions carry current straight through, so a depolarization in one cell depolarizes its neighbor with almost no delay. There is no transmitter, no receptor and no cleft to cross.
  • Current can usually pass in either direction, so a group of cells joined this way tends to fire together.
  • You find them in heart muscle, in smooth muscle of the gut and uterus, between glial cells, and between some neurons in the brain.

Predict

Pick the synapse you would build for each job. Job 1: every muscle cell in the wall of the heart must contract at nearly the same moment. Job 2: a spinal motor neuron must be able to ignore a sensory signal on some occasions and respond to it on others.

Electrical for job 1, chemical for job 2

Job 1 is electrical. Gap junctions between heart muscle cells pass the action potential from cell to cell with almost no delay, so the wall contracts as one unit. With chemical synapses at every junction, the delays would add up across millions of cells and the contraction would not be coordinated.

Job 2 is chemical. Only a chemical synapse can be inhibitory, can be turned down at a single terminal, and can be strengthened with use. If you joined the sensory neuron to the motor neuron with gap junctions, every sensory signal would pass through and nothing could block it. The delay of a chemical synapse is the cost of being able to integrate.

Change the conditions

Take the inhibition away

  • A spinal motor neuron receives excitatory input all the time, from sensory neurons and from the brain. Inhibitory interneurons that release glycine or GABA keep it below threshold unless the excitation is strong.
  • Left panel: an excitatory input worth 18 mV and an inhibitory input worth −8 mV arrive together. −70 + 18 − 8 = −60 mV, so the cell does not fire.
  • Right panel: block release from the inhibitory terminal. −70 + 18 = −52 mV, which is above threshold. The same input that did nothing a moment ago now fires the motor neuron. The sizes here are illustrative.

The same result, reached at a different step

The glycine is released normally, but its receptor cannot open its Cl− channel, so there is no IPSP. For the motor neuron that is the same as no glycine at all: the −8 mV is missing and ordinary excitation reaches threshold. Strychnine poisoning produces convulsions set off by small stimuli, very much like the patient on slide 1. One agent acts before the cleft and the other after it, and both remove the same term from the sum.

22

The patient, explained

What the toxin did in his spinal cord

  1. The toxin traveled up his motor axons

    Clostridium tetani grew in the deep wound and released tetanus toxin. Motor axon terminals took it up, and retrograde axonal transport carried it to the spinal cord and brainstem. That trip is part of why a week went by.

  2. It crossed into the inhibitory terminals

    There it cut a protein that vesicles need in order to fuse, so the interneurons that synapse on motor neurons could no longer release glycine or GABA.

  3. The IPSPs disappeared from the sum

    Nothing was added to the excitatory side. The same EPSPs from every touch and sound now reach threshold. That was your prediction on slide 2.

  4. Opposing muscles contract together

    Inhibitory interneurons normally keep the opposing muscle quiet when a muscle contracts. Without them both contract at full force, and the strong jaw closing muscles clamp the jaw shut.

Treat it as an experiment

The variable that failed is inhibitory transmitter release onto motor neurons. Before you open the box, name one way to put inhibition back into the sum without using the blocked terminals.

Three different targets

The GABA-A receptor on the motor neuron. The toxin blocks release, but the receptors are intact. A drug that makes GABA-A channels open more readily, such as a benzodiazepine, increases whatever inhibition remains and puts a negative term back into the sum.

The input side. Every sound and touch adds EPSPs. A quiet, dark room lowers the excitatory side of the same sum.

The toxin itself. Antibody against the toxin binds what has not yet entered neurons. Toxin already inside the terminals cannot be reached, so recovery waits for those terminals to be repaired or replaced, which takes weeks. Vaccination works by having that antibody present before any toxin arrives.

A related toxin, botulinum toxin, cuts the same family of proteins but stays in the motor neuron terminal at the muscle, where it blocks acetylcholine release. The muscle cannot be excited at all, and the result is limp paralysis. The mechanism is the same, the synapse is different, and the effect is the opposite.

Nursing

Tetanus can be prevented with a vaccine, so any dirty wound is a reason to ask when the last tetanus shot was.

Example: Adults are advised to have a tetanus booster every 10 years. For a deep or dirty wound, such as a puncture from a nail, the vaccination history is checked as part of wound care.

Radiologic technology

A puncture wound can leave a piece of metal or glass deep in the tissue where nobody can see it.

Example: A radiograph of the foot is a standard way to look for a retained metal fragment after a puncture wound. Metal and most glass show clearly on a plain film.

Medicine

No quick blood test confirms tetanus. The diagnosis comes from the history and from what the examiner sees.

Example: A locked jaw, muscle rigidity and spasms set off by noise or touch, in a patient who is fully awake and has a recent wound, point to lost inhibition in the spinal cord and brainstem.

Respiratory therapy

If the spasms reach the muscles of the throat and chest, the patient cannot breathe.

Example: Spasm of the larynx and the respiratory muscles is the main danger in severe tetanus. These patients are cared for in intensive care, often with sedation and mechanical ventilation until the terminals recover.
