What this is

Physiology online can get quiet. You watch the lectures on your own, you work the note sheet on your own, and it is easy to go a whole week without saying any of it out loud to another human being. That works for some people and it wears other people down, and the ones it wears down are usually the ones who were doing everything right.

So this is the room where you do the practice parts together. Somebody runs a Kahoot on the week's competencies. Two of you work the Silverthorn problems side by side and argue about step three. Four of you do a brain dump on the same competency and then compare what each of you left out, which is the fastest way to find out that your gap is not the gap you assumed. Somebody teaches a pathway at a whiteboard while everyone else holds the checklist.

There is also a quieter reason it works, and it is worth saying plainly: teaching somebody else is one of the strongest ways to learn something yourself. When you host a session you are not doing anyone a favor. You are getting the best study hour of your week.

What this is not

This is not where graded work gets done together. Your lab write-up, your patient chart entry and your discussion posts are yours, done by you, in your own words. Practicing the same competency in a group and then going away and writing your own answer is exactly right. Sitting in a call filling in the same worksheet together is not, and it is the one line here that matters.

Two ways a session happens

Led by me

Drop-ins and review sessions

When I hold one, it goes on this page and in the Canvas announcements. Drop in, stay for ten minutes or the whole hour, bring a question or bring nothing. There is no sign-up and no attendance.

Led by you

Your own session

Post in the Study With Me thread in Canvas saying when you are meeting and what you want to work on, and let people join you. Your own Zoom, a study room on campus, a coffee shop, whatever suits. You do not need my permission and you do not need to tell me how it went.

Pick one thing to do

The sessions that work are the ones where everybody is producing physiology out loud, not watching one person talk. Pick a single activity and actually do it rather than planning a syllabus for the hour.

  • Brain dump the same competency

    Everyone writes on blank paper for five minutes, then you read each other's and find what you all missed. The overlap is the real gap.

  • Teach it at a whiteboard

    One person talks and draws at the same time, the way the midterm asks for. Everyone else holds the checklist and ticks what actually got covered.

  • Run a Kahoot

    Somebody builds a quick one on this week's competencies before the session and you play it together. Fast, loud, and it surfaces the things nobody knew.

  • Work the book problems together

    Everyone tries the same Silverthorn problem cold, then you compare routes. Two people getting the same answer different ways teaches more than either answer.

  • Quiz from the recall cards

    Open the Mastery OS, read a card out, and let the group answer before anyone looks. Say why the wrong options are wrong, not just which one is right.

  • Trace a pathway out loud

    Pick a loop or a mechanism and hand it round, one step per person, each saying what happens next and why. Whoever stalls has found their own repair target.

A simple run of show

  1. Warm up, five minutes. Everyone names three things from last week without looking. It gets people talking before anything hard starts.
  2. Do your one thing. Spend the bulk of the time on it and take turns, so nobody hides and nobody carries the whole hour.
  3. Go back to the misses. Whatever the group got wrong is the most useful thing in the room. Re-do those before you finish.
  4. Close it out. Each person says one thing they are going to review on their own tonight, then you stop on time.

Making one happen

  • Post it before you need it. A session announced the night before gets one person. Two or three days ahead gets a group.
  • Say what you are working on. "Week 4 membrane potential, brain dump and Kahoot" pulls people in. "Studying, anyone want to join" does not.
  • Start on time and end on time. An hour that ends when it said it would is one people come back to.
  • Small is fine. Two people quizzing each other is a real session. You are not failing if it is not eight.
  • Nobody has to be the expert. The person who understands it least often asks the question that fixes it for everyone.

What a session has to do to count

These sessions earn Scholar Points, up to a 2.5 percent bump on your final course percentage. Because there is a percentage riding on it, an hour has to rest on something I can actually see. None of this is here to be difficult.

  • Record it. Whoever hosts records the session and keeps the link. That is what shows me it happened and that people were working, not just signed in.
  • Cameras on. Everybody, for the whole session. A black square is not attendance, and a session where half the squares are black is not the thing this is trying to build.
  • Stay the whole time. Log the time you were actually present. Arriving late or leaving early is fine, log what you were there for. Do not log an hour you spent in another tab.
  • One session at a time. Sitting in two rooms at once means neither can be verified, so neither counts.
  • Hosts keep the recording and the attendance list. If you ran it, you are the one who can show who was there and for how long.
  • Hosts get one extra hour. Preparing and facilitating is real work. A two hour session you hosted logs as three. Not double, just the one extra hour on top, however long the session ran.

If something about this is a problem for you, cameras especially, come and talk to me rather than skipping the whole thing. There is almost always a way to sort it out.

Logging your hours

The Scholar Points page has the log. Add each session as you finish it, with the date, how long you were there, whether you hosted, and the recording link. It adds itself up and shows you where you are against the thirty hours.

There is a weekly assignment in Canvas for your hours. At the end of each week, open the Scholar Points page, press Save this week for Canvas, and upload that PDF. It carries just that week, plus your running total.

Your log lives in your browser

It is stored on your own device and nowhere else, so it keeps adding up all term and you never start over. Use the same browser on the same device and it will still be there in December. If you are about to switch machines or clear your history, save a PDF first.

Questions people ask

Does any of this count toward my grade?

Your hours earn Scholar Points, which can lift your final course percentage by up to 2.5 at the end of the term. It is not extra credit and it is not made up of assignments. It is a bump for time spent working the physiology with other people, and the Scholar Points page explains the difference and holds the log.

Do I have to go?

No. Plenty of people study better alone and there is nothing wrong with that. If that is you, skip this page entirely and your grade is exactly what your work put it at. Scholar Points can only add.

Why do we have to record it?

Because a percentage on your grade has to rest on something I can see. The recording is how I know the session happened and that people were there start to finish. I am not watching them for content, and I am not grading anything in them.

What if I forget to log a session?

Add it when you remember, with the date it actually happened. The log sorts by date and the weekly cap is worked out from the dates, not from the order you typed them in.

Can we work on the lab or the patient chart together?

You can talk about the physiology behind them as much as you like. What you turn in has to be yours, written by you, in your own words. Practice the competency together, then go away and write your own answer.

What if I host and nobody comes?

Then you got an hour of quiet study you had already committed to, which is not a bad outcome. Post it again the following week. The first few are always small.

Do I need Zoom?

No. In person works, and so does any call you already use. Zoom is just the one most people already have through the college.
