The whole idea in a few sentences

Read this before you record. If these sentences make sense, the rest is detail.

  1. Particles spread from where there are lots of them toward where there are fewer, and that is all a concentration gradient means.
  2. Ions cannot cross a membrane without a protein, and every protein is built from a gene: gene, protein, membrane.
  3. With potassium leak channels in the membrane, K+ moves out down its concentration gradient, and because each K+ is positive, the inside becomes negative.
  4. The negative inside pulls K+ back in, so potassium is in a tug of war, and the point where the push out equals the pull in is the potassium equilibrium potential.
  5. Sodium is the opposite case: both its concentration gradient and the negative inside move it in, but the resting membrane has far fewer ways for Na+ to cross, so K+ has the bigger influence and the inside stays negative, a little less negative than with K+ alone. That is the resting membrane potential.
  6. The leaks never stop, so the cell builds a pump that uses ATP to move 3 Na+ out and 2 K+ in. The pump sets up and maintains the gradients, and the leak channels let ions move down them.

The resting membrane potential, told as a story

60 sec

Today we are going to build a resting cell from nothing, and we are going to do it as a story. I am not going to give you any vocabulary at the start. We begin with a balloon full of little balls, and we add one small piece at a time.

Every time I add a piece, I am going to ask you a question, and you will be able to answer it from what you already have. When you understand an idea, then I will tell you what scientists call it. There are no numbers today. The numbers come in the full lecture.

Get out your draw along sheet, or a blank piece of paper, and two colours. I want you drawing with me the whole way.

DrawNothing yet. Say your pen code out loud: rust for potassium and for every concentration push, navy for sodium, for every electrical pull and for the plus and minus signs.
02

Part 1. The balloon

60 sec

Start with a balloon. Inside the balloon I have put lots of little balls. Outside the balloon there are only a few. Now imagine the balls are always jiggling around, and imagine they could move right through the skin of the balloon.

Here is my question. If these balls could move through the balloon, which way would they naturally move?

Out. Why? Because there are lots of them inside and fewer outside. Each ball is just moving at random. But with more balls inside, more of them happen to cross going outward than coming inward. So overall they spread out, toward where there are fewer. I am not going to name this yet. Just write it the simple way: lots here, fewer there, movement toward fewer.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, draw four or five arrows from balls near the skin out through the skin. Write LOTS inside the balloon and FEW outside it. Under the balloon write: lots here, fewer there, movement toward fewer.
03

Part 2. Turn the balloon into a cell

55 sec

Now let's turn our balloon into a cell. The skin of the balloon becomes the cell membrane. The space inside is the cytoplasm. And down here is the nucleus. The little balls are still here, lots inside and a few outside.

But there is an important difference between our imaginary balloon and a real cell. The particles cannot simply move through the membrane whenever they want. The membrane stops them. So if the membrane stops them, what would they need in order to cross?

They need doors. And here is the part I want you to remember for the rest of the week: nobody hands the cell its doors. Cells have to build those doors.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, label the cell membrane, the cytoplasm and the nucleus. In rust, draw an arrow from one ball up to the membrane and put an X where it hits. Write: no door, no crossing.
04

Part 3. Go to the nucleus

55 sec

So where does a door come from? Go to the nucleus. Inside the nucleus is DNA. DNA contains genes, and a gene is the instructions for making a protein. Some of those proteins will become doors in the cell membrane.

A door is a protein. So where are the instructions for building it, and where does the finished door have to end up?

The instructions are a gene, in the DNA, in the nucleus. The cell reads the gene and builds the protein, and the protein goes to the membrane. Gene, protein, membrane. You are going to hear me say back to the nucleus every time our cell needs something new. We are going to start by building one particular door.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, box one stretch of the DNA and write 1 gene. Draw a squiggle in the cytoplasm above the nucleus and write 2 protein. Draw a dashed door on the membrane above it and write 3 membrane. Join them with two arrows, from the nucleus upward.
05

Part 4. Build a potassium leak channel

60 sec

Now I can tell you that our little balls have an identity. They are potassium ions, K+. We have lots of K+ inside and less K+ outside.

Back to the nucleus. The cell uses a gene to build a potassium leak channel and places it in the membrane. The cell does not make just one copy, so I have drawn three. It is a selective door: this door lets potassium through. And because it is a leak channel, potassium does not need a special signal to use it.

Which way will potassium go?

Out. You already knew that from the balloon. Lots inside, less outside. Now you can give that old idea its grown-up name: a concentration gradient. The concentration gradient is pushing K+ out of the cell.

DoPoint to the small inset at the right and say: gene, protein, membrane. Ask the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, draw an arrow out of the cell through each of the three leak channels, from inside to outside. Write LOTS of K+ inside and less K+ outside. Then write the new name beside the arrows: concentration gradient.
06

Part 5. But potassium has a charge

70 sec

Now I am going to add one new piece of information. These are not ordinary balls. Each K+ carries a positive charge. That is what the little plus means. So every time a K+ leaves, a positive charge leaves with it.

What is going to happen to the inside if positive charges keep leaving?

It becomes more negative compared with the outside. The cell started out balanced: for every positive charge inside there was a negative one. The negative partners are mostly big molecules, like proteins, that cannot leave. So each K+ that goes out takes its plus with it and leaves a minus behind.

Look at where I put the signs. They are right along the membrane: minus on the inside face, plus on the outside face. Say the chain with me: K+ leaves, positive leaves, inside becomes more negative. No millivolts yet.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw three minus signs just inside the membrane, spread out along it, and three plus signs just outside, directly above them. Write: inside becomes more NEGATIVE. Trace one rust arrow again and write a small plus at its tip.
07

Part 6. Uh-oh. Now potassium wants two different things

45 sec

Uh-oh. Now potassium wants two different things. Look at this one K+ sitting in the door. It still has the original problem: there is lots of K+ inside, so the concentration difference keeps pushing K+ out.

But now the inside of our cell has become negative. And K+ is positive. Opposite charges attract. So which way does the negative inside pull a positive K+?

Back in. Here is your tug of war. Concentration says: out. Charge says: in. Right now the concentration push is the bigger one, so I draw it longer.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawStart both arrows at the K+ in the middle door. In rust, a long arrow straight up and out of the cell: write concentration pushes OUT. In navy, a short arrow straight down into the cell: write electrical pull back IN.
08

Part 7. Eventually the tug of war ties

80 sec

At first the concentration push is the stronger one, so more K+ leaves. But every positive K+ that leaves makes the inside a little more negative. And a more negative inside pulls harder on the next K+. So the navy arrow grows, and it keeps growing until the push out equals the pull in.

Does potassium stop moving?

No. Individual K+ ions still move in and out through the leak channels. But now K+ going out is about equal to K+ coming in. So there is no net movement of potassium. The potassium tug of war is tied. And it takes only a very small number of K+ leaving to get here. The inside still has lots of K+, so the push out has not gone away.

Now give that concept its name: the potassium equilibrium potential. You do not need a number yet. For now, it is the point where potassium's concentration force pushing it out is balanced by the electrical force pulling it in.

DoGo slowly here. Ask the question, let them argue about it, then open Show the answer.
DrawAt the K+ in the middle door, draw the rust arrow up and the navy arrow down, this time exactly the same length. Write push OUT = pull IN. At another door draw one thin arrow out and one thin arrow in, side by side, and write: no NET movement. Then write the name: potassium equilibrium potential.
09

Part 8. But our cell is not really a potassium-only cell

40 sec

I have to confess something. We cheated a little. Real cells do not only have potassium. So let's add sodium, Na+. Sodium is the navy squares. Look where they are: there is lots of Na+ outside and less Na+ inside. That is the opposite of potassium.

But sodium cannot cross our membrane either. And our potassium door only lets potassium through. So what do we need?

Another door. One that lets sodium through. And you already know where doors come from.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, write LOTS of Na+ outside and less Na+ inside. Draw a dashed door with a question mark on the membrane to the right of the potassium doors.
10

Part 9. Back to the nucleus: a sodium leak channel

50 sec

Back to the nucleus. The cell uses another gene, builds a sodium leak channel, and puts it in the membrane. Gene, protein, membrane.

Which direction does sodium want to move? In, because there is more sodium outside than inside. That is the concentration gradient again, pointing the other way.

Now something even better. What charge is sodium? Positive. And what charge have we created inside our cell? Negative. So the inside attracts sodium too.

Sodium has two reasons to enter. Concentration says in. Electrical attraction says in. Compare that with potassium, where the two forces were fighting each other. For sodium, the two forces are currently working together.

DoAsk the three questions one at a time and wait after each. Then open Show the answer.
DrawBoth arrows point INTO the cell through the sodium door. In rust, an arrow from outside down to the door: write concentration, IN. In navy, an arrow from the door down into the cell: write negative inside pulls it IN. Put a small plus on the Na+ in the door.
11

Part 10. Now we have a real resting cell

70 sec

Now stand back and look at the whole membrane. We have K+ leaking out and Na+ leaking in. But count the doors. Our cell has a lot more opportunity for potassium to leak than sodium. The scientific way to say that: the membrane at rest is much more permeable to K+ than it is to Na+. Permeable just means easy to cross.

So which ion has the bigger influence on the electrical difference across the membrane? And is the inside negative or positive?

Potassium has the bigger influence, so the inside stays negative. But sodium leaking in brings some positive charge back in. So the inside does not become quite as negative as it would if potassium were the only ion that could cross.

Now name it: the resting membrane potential. No numbers yet. You understand what produced it, and that matters more.

DoHave them count the doors out loud: three for potassium, one for sodium. Then ask the question and open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, three thick arrows out through the potassium doors: write many K+ doors. In navy, one thinner arrow in through the sodium door: write few Na+ doors. Inside the cell write: stays negative, a little less than with K+ alone. Then write the name: resting membrane potential.
12

Part 11. There is a problem

45 sec

There is a problem, and you can find it yourself. Look at the two leaks. Neither one ever stops.

If K+ keeps leaking out and Na+ keeps leaking in, what eventually happens to our nice differences between inside and outside?

Over time, we would start losing them. K+ inside would slowly fall. Na+ inside would slowly rise. And everything we built today depends on those differences. So our cell needs a way to maintain them. We are going to build another protein. But this one is not a channel.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn the empty space at the right, write in navy: K+ inside falls, Na+ inside rises, the differences run down. Draw a dashed box with a question mark on the membrane at the far right and write: not a door.
13

Part 12. Back to the nucleus: build a machine, not a door

100 sec

Back to the nucleus. Gene, protein, membrane. I want to make this distinction very concrete. A channel is a door. Ions move through it according to the forces acting on them. This new protein is a machine. It uses energy to move ions, and the energy comes from ATP. That is why I drew it as a box and not as a door.

Na+ has been leaking in and K+ has been leaking out. So to keep our differences, which way does the machine have to move each ion?

Na+ back out, K+ back in. Each cycle it moves three Na+ out and two K+ in, and it uses ATP to do it, because it is moving both ions toward the side that already has more. Its name is the sodium potassium pump, or the Na+/K+ ATPase.

One honest note. This same pump is also what set up those differences in the first place. It had been running long before our story began, and that is why our cell started with lots of K+ inside and lots of Na+ outside.

So here is the sentence that will save you headaches later. The pump maintains the gradients. The leak channels allow ions to move down those gradients.

DoPoint to the inset: gene, protein, membrane. Ask the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawOn the machine: in navy, an arrow from the box up and out of the cell, write 3 Na+ OUT. In rust, an arrow from the box down into the cell, write 2 K+ IN. Write ATP beside the box on the inside. Cross out the word machine and write PUMP. Under the drawing write: pump maintains, leak channels let ions move down.
14

Part 13. The different kinds of doors

65 sec

You have earned the channel terminology now. You already know a leak channel. Cells can make different kinds of ion channels, and what makes them different is what controls whether the door is open.

Look at doors two and three. Each has a part that door one does not have. What do you think controls each of these doors?

Door one is the leak channel. It is usually available without a special signal. No key required. That is what we have been using. Door two is a ligand gated channel. It needs a chemical key. Something binds to the protein, right here in this cup. Chemical binds, door changes. Door three is a voltage gated channel. It responds to a change in the electrical conditions across the membrane. Electrical change, door changes.

And we stop there. That is all you need right now.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer. Do not start the action potential.
DrawIn navy, write the three names under the doors: leak channel, ligand gated channel, voltage gated channel. At door 2, draw an arrow from the grey dot into the cup and write: chemical key. At door 3, circle the part with the plus signs and write: senses electrical change.
