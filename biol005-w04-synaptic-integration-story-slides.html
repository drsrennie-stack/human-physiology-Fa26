01

The baseline story

Integration at the synapse, told as a story

One neuron receives a great many synapses, and at every moment it has one decision to make: fire, or stay silent. We are going to work out how it decides, and we start with a bucket that has a small hole in it.

  • Each part adds one new piece of information and then asks you a question you can already answer.
  • You draw along on your own sheet: the water levels, the arrows, the plus and minus signs and the labels.
  • A scientific name arrives only after you understand the idea it belongs to. There are no numbers today.

What we are building

A leaky bucket becomes the receiving neuron from the synapse story. Small inputs add, other inputs take away, and only the total at the trigger zone decides whether the cell fires.

02

Part 1

The bucket

Here is a bucket. It has a small hole near the bottom, so the water in it sits at the height of the hole. Near the top someone has drawn a line. You have one small cup of water.

Ask

You tip in one cup. Does the water reach the line?

What happens to that water if you wait?

Answer

No. One cup raises the water a little, and the line is far above it.

Then the extra water runs out through the hole, and the level slowly goes back down to the hole, where it started.

One cup → a small rise → it leaks away.

03

Part 2

Only small cups

The rule is that you may use small cups only, and you cannot plug the hole.

Ask

How could you get the water up to the line? Find two different ways.

Answer

Way 1. One person adds cup after cup, quickly. Each cup arrives before the last one has leaked away, so the level climbs.

Way 2. Many people each add one cup at the same moment.

If the cups come slowly and one at a time, each one has leaked away before the next arrives, and the water never gets to the line.

Small additions reach the line only if they arrive close together: in time, or from many cups at once.

04

Part 3

Turn the bucket into a neuron

From the last story: one message opens key hole doors, and the inside becomes a little more positive at that spot. From the story before it: that small change fades as it spreads, and the cell fires only if the change is big enough at the trigger zone.

Ask

In the neuron, what is the cup? What is the leak? What is the line?

Answer

The cup is one small change, a small depolarization, from one synapse. The leak is the fading: the membrane has leak channels, so the extra positive charge leaks out again. The line is threshold at the trigger zone.

One input alone raises the level a little, and then it fades. One input does not fire this cell.

Where the comparison stops: in a neuron the water is electrical charge, and what is compared with the line is the level at one particular spot, the trigger zone. A bucket has one level everywhere. A neuron does not.

The name: excitatory, because it moves the cell toward firing, and postsynaptic, because it happens in the receiving cell.

One message at one synapse → one small depolarization → it fades.

Now name it: excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP)

05

Part 4

One input is not enough

One EPSP is far too small to reach threshold. But you have already solved this problem with the bucket.

Ask

What are the two ways this neuron could reach threshold using only small EPSPs?

Answer

Way 1. One input sends message after message, fast. Each EPSP arrives before the last one has faded, so each one starts from a higher level.

Way 2. Many inputs each send one message at the same moment, and their EPSPs arrive at the trigger zone together.

Real neurons use both at once. Temporal means in time. Spatial means in space.

EPSPs add. Close together in time, or from many places at once → the trigger zone reaches threshold → the cell fires.

Now name it: temporal summation (one input, fast) and spatial summation (many inputs at once)

06

Part 5

Someone scoops water out

Back to the bucket: while people add cups, someone else scoops water OUT. A neuron can do this too. Back to the nucleus: gene, protein, membrane. The receiving cell builds a different key hole door, one that lets K+ through, and places it under a different input.

Ask

From the first story: when this door opens, which way does K+ go? What does that do to the inside?

Answer

K+ goes OUT, because there is lots of K+ inside and less outside. Positive charge leaves, so the inside becomes more negative. That is farther from threshold, so the cell is less likely to fire.

Where the bucket stops working: water scooped from below the hole would stay gone. In a neuron the level drifts back to its resting level from either direction.

The full lecture shows that this K+ door is opened through a helper protein inside the cell. The result is the same.

Key binds → K+ door opens → K+ leaves → inside more negative → farther from threshold.

Now name it: inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP)

07

Part 6

A second kind of scoop: chloride

Back to the nucleus once more: a key hole door for chloride, Cl−. Chloride is negative, and there is much more of it outside. At rest its tug of war is nearly tied: concentration pushes Cl− in, and the negative inside pushes it back out. So when this door opens at rest, the level hardly moves.

Ask

Now a cup arrives and the inside starts to become more positive. Which way does Cl− move, and what does that do to the cup?

Answer

The inside is now less negative, so it pushes back on Cl− less, and the concentration push is the stronger one. Cl− comes IN. Negative charge coming in cancels the positive charge that the cup brought.

So open chloride doors hold the inside down near where it already is, and every cup raises it much less. In the bucket, it is like cutting a wide opening in the wall right at the resting water level: whatever you add runs straight out.

In some neurons these doors make the inside a little more negative, and in others a little less negative. Either way they hold it below threshold. The full lecture has the detail.

Cl− doors open → the inside is held near its resting level → every cup counts for less.

Now name it: this is also an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP)

08

Part 7

The cell adds everything

Now three cups and one scoop arrive at the same moment, at different places on the neuron.

Ask

What does the trigger zone compare with the line: each input one at a time, or something else?

Answer

Only the total. The EPSPs and the IPSPs spread to the trigger zone and add together there. Cups count up and scoops count down.

If the total reaches threshold, the cell fires. If it does not, the cell stays silent. One neuron can receive a great many inputs, and it gives one answer.

Cups up, scoops down → ONE total at the trigger zone → fire, or stay silent.

Now name it: synaptic integration

09

Part 8

Where the cup comes from matters

Carry a cup from far away and you spill some on the way. Inputs A and C release exactly the same amount, but A is far out on the dendrite and C is close to the cell body.

Ask

Which one counts for more at the trigger zone?

Where would you put a scoop so that it has the most effect?

Answer

C counts for more. The change from A fades as it spreads along the dendrite, because charge leaks out through the membrane on the way, so only a little of it arrives.

The best place for a scoop is on the cell body, right before the trigger zone. Every EPSP from every dendrite has to pass that spot, so one inhibitory input there can cancel many excitatory ones. Inhibitory inputs are often found there.

Farther away → more is lost on the way → counts for less. Inhibition at the cell body acts on everything that passes.

10

Part 9

Turn down one hose without touching the bucket

A third neuron ends on the terminal of input A. Its chemical key fits a key hole on that terminal. Back to the nucleus: neuron A built that key hole protein and sent it down its axon to the terminal. When the third neuron is active, less Ca2+ comes into terminal A when a message arrives.

Ask

From the synapse story: what does less Ca2+ do to the message from A?

Does the receiving cell now respond differently to B and C?

Answer

Less Ca2+ comes in, so fewer bags empty, fewer chemical keys are released, and the EPSP from A is smaller.

No. Nothing about the receiving cell has changed, so B and C count exactly as before. Compare that with a scoop on the cell body, which acts on every input.

The opposite also exists: a third neuron can make a terminal release more. That is presynaptic facilitation.

Third neuron → less Ca2+ into ONE terminal → fewer keys → only that input is turned down.

Now name it: presynaptic inhibition

11

Part 10

An input that is used a lot gets a bigger cup

Synapses are not fixed. When one input is used strongly, again and again, the receiving cell changes that synapse so that the same message counts for more.

Ask

The same message has to give a bigger change. Think about what makes the change in the first place. What could the receiving cell put more of at this synapse?

Answer

More key hole doors. The first extra doors come from a store the cell keeps near the synapse, and keeping the change takes a trip back to the nucleus: gene, protein, membrane.

The same chemical message now opens more doors, more positive charge comes in, and the EPSP is bigger. The cup from this input has grown.

The reverse happens too: a synapse that is used in a slow, weak way can lose doors and count for less.

Most neuroscientists think changes like these are part of how learning and memory work. How a particular memory is stored is still being worked out.

Strong use → more key hole doors at that synapse → same message, bigger EPSP.

Now name it: synaptic plasticity. This lasting increase is long term potentiation (LTP)

12

Part 11

A different kind of connection: a tunnel

Everything so far used a chemical crossing a gap. Some cells are joined in a completely different way. Back to the nucleus, in both cells: each cell builds half of a tunnel protein, and the two halves dock, so the inside of one cell opens into the inside of the next.

Ask

Positive charge has just come into cell 1. What happens in cell 2, and how fast?

What do you gain with a tunnel, and what do you give up?

Answer

The charge spreads straight through the tunnels, so cell 2 becomes more positive almost at once. It usually works in either direction.

You gain speed, and cells that fire together. Heart muscle cells are joined this way, so the wall of the heart contracts as one.

You give up control: no scoops, no turning down one input, no bigger cups. Whatever happens in one cell is passed to the next, a little smaller.

The kind of connection we have used all week, with a chemical crossing a gap, is a chemical synapse.

Tunnels between two cells → charge passes straight through → fast, both directions, little control.

Now name it: gap junction. A connection made of gap junctions is an electrical synapse

13

Part 12

Take away all the scoops

A motor neuron in the spinal cord receives ordinary excitatory input all the time. Inhibitory inputs normally keep it below threshold unless the excitation is strong. Now suppose the inhibitory terminals cannot release their chemical at all.

Ask

Nothing has been added to the excitatory side. What happens when the ordinary inputs arrive?

Answer

The IPSPs are missing from the total, so the same ordinary EPSPs now reach threshold. The motor neuron fires when it should not, and its muscle contracts when it should be quiet.

That is the direction only. The full lecture has a patient in whom exactly this has happened.

No inhibition → same excitation, bigger total → the cell fires when it should not.
