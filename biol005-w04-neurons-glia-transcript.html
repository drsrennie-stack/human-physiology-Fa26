The whole idea in a few sentences

Read this before you record. If these sentences make sense, the rest is detail.

  1. A neuron carries a signal from an input zone (dendrites and cell body) through a trigger zone (axon hillock and initial segment) and a conducting zone (axon) to an output zone (axon terminal), and each zone behaves as it does because of the channels in its membrane: ligand gated, then dense voltage gated Na+, then voltage gated Na+ and K+, then voltage gated Ca2+.
  2. Neurons are sorted by shape (multipolar, bipolar, pseudounipolar, anaxonic) and by job (sensory or afferent, interneuron, motor or efferent), and the two labels pair up in a regular way.
  3. Six glial cells keep neurons working: astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia and ependymal cells in the CNS, and Schwann cells and satellite cells in the PNS. Astrocytes keep the K+ and neurotransmitter around neurons low and help form the blood brain barrier.
  4. Myelin is insulation made of glial membrane. It stops current leaking out of the axon, so the action potential has to be remade only at the nodes, and the signal arrives sooner.
  5. Because Na+ channels are dense only at the nodes, a stretch of axon that loses its myelin both leaks current and cannot remake the action potential, so conduction fails. That is multiple sclerosis in the CNS and Guillain-Barré syndrome in the PNS.
  6. Proteins are made in the cell body and carried along microtubules: kinesin outward and dynein back, fast at hundreds of mm per day and slow at a few mm per day, all paid for with ATP. Some viruses and toxins ride on dynein, and cut axons regrow at the slow transport speed, and only where Schwann cells guide them.

04

Four structural classes

65 sec

First, sorting by shape. The names come from counting the processes, meaning the dendrites and axons, that leave the cell body.

In every drawing the signal goes from top to bottom. Multipolar: many dendrites, one axon. It is the most common by far, and your motor neurons look like this. Bipolar: one dendrite, one axon, cell body in the middle, found in the retina, the nose and the inner ear. Pseudounipolar: one long process, with the cell body off to the side on a short branch. These are your touch, pain and stretch neurons. Anaxonic: many processes, and you cannot pick out an axon.

Why would a sensory neuron keep its cell body off to the side? Follow the path. The signal runs down one continuous axon from the skin to the spinal cord and never has to cross the cell body.

DrawOn the pseudounipolar neuron, trace the straight path from top to bottom in rust and say: the signal never enters the cell body. Then circle the cell body and write: makes the proteins.

Three functional classes, in one reflex

Neuron one carries the signal toward the CNS. Toward is called afferent, and these are sensory neurons. Neuron two lies entirely inside the spinal cord. That is an interneuron. Neuron three carries the signal away from the CNS to the muscle. Away is efferent, and these are motor neurons. Afferent arrives, efferent exits.

About 99 of every 100 neurons are interneurons, so most of what the CNS does is process information between input and output.

06

Input, trigger, conducting and output zones

90 sec

This is the most important figure today, so we take it slowly. Read this motor neuron left to right, the way the signal travels. There are four regions, and each has a job.

The input zone is the dendrites and cell body. It receives signals. The trigger zone is the axon hillock and the first bit of axon. It decides whether a signal is sent. The conducting zone is the axon. It carries the signal over a distance. The output zone is the axon terminal. It hands the signal to the next cell.

There are two kinds of electrical signal. We study both next time, so today you only need the difference. A graded potential is a small change in membrane potential. A bigger stimulus gives a bigger one, and it fades as it spreads, like the ripple from a pebble dropped in a pond. An action potential is always the same size and does not fade. That is what all or none means.

In the small tracings, the input zone has bumps of different sizes, the axon has identical spikes, and the trigger zone is where one becomes the other.

DrawIn rust, bracket each of the four zones on the neuron in order and say the job of each: receive, decide, carry, hand on.
DrawIn the trigger zone box, circle the tracing where the bump turns into a spike, and write: graded becomes all or none here.
07

Each region works because of the channels in its membrane

85 sec

Each region behaves differently because it has different channels, the same four types as last lecture.

The input zone has ligand gated channels. A neurotransmitter binds, the channel opens, and you get a graded potential. The trigger zone is packed with voltage gated sodium channels, more densely than anywhere else in the cell. If the graded potential is still at threshold when it gets there, about minus 55 millivolts, those channels open and an action potential starts. If it has faded below threshold, nothing is sent. The axon has voltage gated sodium and potassium channels that remake the action potential all the way down.

The terminal has voltage gated calcium channels. Calcium is about ten thousand times more concentrated outside the cell, so when the action potential opens those channels, calcium enters, and that makes vesicles release neurotransmitter. The electrical signal has become a chemical one.

Now the question in the box. Shade where graded potentials happen, then where sodium channels are dense. Which region is shaded twice? The trigger zone. That is why it is the decision point.

DrawIn the table, circle the channel in each row in navy. Beside the trigger zone row write in rust: decision point, minus 55.

Block one kind of channel at a time

50 sec

Let us test your map. Each of these toxins blocks one kind of channel. A motor neuron is stimulated at its dendrites and should make a muscle contract. For each toxin, where does the signal stop? Pause and try all three.

Tetrodotoxin blocks voltage gated sodium channels. The input zone still works, but no action potential can start at the trigger zone. The cone snail toxin blocks calcium channels at the terminal, so the action potential arrives and nothing is released. Curare blocks the ligand gated channels on the muscle, so the whole neuron works and the signal stops at the next cell.

Three different places, and the muscle fails every time.

What astrocytes do for the fluid around a neuron

65 sec

Astrocytes do four things. They take up potassium. They take up neurotransmitter, which ends the signal at a synapse. They pass fuel from the blood to neurons. And their end feet tell the capillary cells to form tight junctions, which is the blood brain barrier.

You can calculate why the potassium matters. Every action potential lets a little potassium out, and the space between brain cells is tiny. Worked problem two: the astrocytes stop clearing it, and potassium outside goes from 4 to 8. Use last lecture's equations. Pause and try it.

E K moves from minus 95 to minus 76, and the Goldman equation puts the resting potential at minus 62. Threshold is minus 55. So every neuron nearby is now only 7 millivolts from firing, and nobody stimulated them. That is one way a seizure spreads, and astrocytes normally prevent it.

11

Two glial cells in the nerves, and what myelin is

50 sec

The PNS has two glial cells. Satellite cells surround and support the cell bodies in ganglia. Schwann cells make myelin, and here is the difference from the oligodendrocyte: each Schwann cell wraps one segment of one axon, about a millimeter long. A long axon needs hundreds of them in a row. The gaps between them are the nodes of Ranvier.

What is myelin? Look at the cross section. It is the glial cell's own membrane wound around the axon again and again. Membrane is mostly lipid, and ions cannot cross lipid, so myelin is insulation, like the plastic coating on a wire.

Oligodendrocyte: one cell, several axons. Schwann cell: one cell, one segment.

DrawIn rust, circle one Schwann cell and write: 1 cell, 1 segment. Then point to a node and write: bare membrane.
DrawIn the cross section, trace the spiral outward from the axon with the pen.
12

How insulation makes a signal faster

105 sec

Here is something students find puzzling. Myelin is an insulator. It carries no signal. So how can adding insulation make a signal faster? We will go slowly.

An action potential does not slide down the axon like a bead on a string. Each patch of membrane has to be brought to threshold by current from the patch before it, and then open its own sodium channels. Opening channels takes time. That is the slow step. In the bare axon at the top, it happens at every patch. Every dot is a place where the action potential is made again.

Current flowing along the inside of the axon is fast. The trouble is that in a bare axon it leaks out through the membrane, so it does not reach far. Picture a garden hose with holes all along it. Most of the water is gone before the end. Wrap tape over the holes, and the same push at the tap reaches the far end. Myelin is the tape.

So in the myelinated axon, current from one node reaches the next node, a millimeter away, still strong enough to bring it to threshold. The action potential is remade only at the nodes. Say the chain with me: insulation stops the leak, so current reaches farther, so channels open in fewer places, so the signal arrives sooner.

DrawOn the bare axon, tap several dots in a row in rust and say: slow step, slow step, slow step. On the myelinated axon, tap only the nodes.
DrawWrite the chain in navy: stops the leak, current reaches farther, fewer places to open channels, faster.

14

Why a demyelinated axon fails to conduct

70 sec

Now take the myelin away. You might expect the axon to behave like an unmyelinated one, slower but working. It does not, and here is why.

The small navy marks are voltage gated sodium channels. They are packed densely at the nodes, and under the myelin there are hardly any.

In the bottom drawing the middle segment is stripped, and two things go wrong together. Current leaks out through the bare membrane, shown by the gray arrows. And that bare membrane has too few sodium channels to make a new action potential. So the depolarization fades as it spreads, the next node stays below threshold, and the signal stops. That is conduction block. If the myelin is only thinned, the signal gets through, but late.

There is some good news. Over weeks the axon can add sodium channels to the bare stretch, and the glial cells can put some myelin back, so some function returns.

DrawIn the top drawing, circle the dense channels at one node in navy, then point under the myelin and say: almost none.
DrawIn the bottom drawing, trace the fading dotted line in rust from the second spike to the node, and write: below threshold, stops.
15

Two diseases in which the immune system attacks myelin

50 sec

Here are two diseases in which the immune system attacks myelin. Read across each row. Multiple sclerosis is in the CNS, so the myelin under attack comes from oligodendrocytes. Guillain-Barré syndrome is in the PNS, so the myelin comes from Schwann cells.

The symptoms follow from where the myelin is lost. In multiple sclerosis it depends on which pathway is hit, and vision is a common one. In Guillain-Barré it is weakness that starts in the feet and hands and moves upward. Schwann cells repair myelin well and oligodendrocytes do not, which is why most people recover from Guillain-Barré and recovery in multiple sclerosis is only partial.

16

A neuron has to supply an axon that can be a meter long

60 sec

Here is a different problem. Nearly every protein is made in the cell body, where the nucleus and ribosomes are. But the terminal needs a constant supply of channels, vesicle proteins, enzymes and mitochondria, and proteins wear out in days to weeks.

How far away is the terminal? A big motor neuron's cell body is about a tenth of a millimeter across, and its axon can be a meter long. If the cell body were a tennis ball, the axon would be a tube about 670 meters long, roughly four tenths of a mile.

Could proteins diffuse that far? No. Diffusion time goes up with the square of the distance. Across the cell body takes seconds, and one meter would take hundreds of years. The neuron needs tracks, motors and fuel.

DoRead the predict box, pause, then open the reveal.
DrawWrite in rust: cell body 0.1 mm, axon 1 m = 10,000 times longer.
17

Motor proteins walk along microtubules and carry the cargo

70 sec

Here is that system. The tracks are microtubules running the length of the axon, all pointing the same way, with the plus end at the terminal.

Two motor proteins walk on them, one foot in front of the other, splitting ATP with each step. Kinesin walks toward the plus end, so it carries cargo out to the terminal. That direction is anterograde, which means forward. Dynein walks the other way, back to the cell body. That is retrograde, which means backward.

There are two speeds. Fast transport moves vesicles, membrane proteins and mitochondria at about 400 millimeters a day going out and 200 to 300 coming back. Slow transport moves cytoskeleton proteins and enzymes at only a few millimeters a day. Look at the bars: the slow one is barely visible.

What is the return direction for? Recycling old parts, and carrying information from the terminal back to the nucleus.

DrawTrace the upper track left to right in navy and say kinesin, anterograde, out. Trace the lower track right to left in rust and say dynein, retrograde, back.
DrawCircle the tiny slow transport bar and write: hundreds of times slower.

20

What else rides on dynein, and what if the tracks are taken apart

65 sec

Dynein cannot tell the cell's own cargo from an intruder. Rabies virus enters motor axon terminals near the bite and is carried to the spinal cord and on to the brain. Herpes simplex enters sensory terminals in the lip and is carried to the cell body, where it stays dormant. When it reactivates, new virus goes back down the same axon on kinesin, which is why a cold sore returns in the same place. Tetanus toxin rides to the spinal cord and blocks inhibitory interneurons, so muscles cannot relax.

Now change the conditions. A drug takes microtubules apart everywhere in the body. Which neurons fail first? Pause and think. The ones with the longest axons, because their terminals are farthest from the supply. The person feels numbness and tingling in the toes and fingertips first. That happens with a chemotherapy drug called vincristine.

21

A cut axon can regrow in the PNS, and hardly at all in the CNS

65 sec

If an axon is cut, the piece beyond the cut gets no more supplies, so it degenerates. What happens next depends on the glia.

In the PNS, Schwann cells survive, clear the debris, line up into a tube and release growth factors, and the axon regrows down that tube at about 1 to 2 millimeters a day. In the CNS, oligodendrocytes and astrocytes inhibit regrowth, and astrocytes form a scar. That is why a spinal cord injury is largely permanent.

Worked problem five. A nerve is cut at the wrist, and the fingertip is 200 millimeters away. 200 divided by 2 is 100 days, and 200 divided by 1 is 200 days, so three to seven months. That is the speed of slow transport, because the new axon is built from cytoskeleton proteins that arrive by slow transport.

DrawIn the table, circle Schwann cells in navy and write: guide. Circle the CNS glia cell in rust and write: inhibit.

22

Every neuron is present. Myelin is what is missing.

100 sec

Back to our patient. The optic nerve is part of the CNS, so its myelin comes from oligodendrocytes. She has multiple sclerosis. In her first episode, her immune system stripped myelin from a patch of the right optic nerve. Those axons went into conduction block, and her vision blurred.

Then she recovered. There was some remyelination, and the axons added sodium channels to the bare stretch. Signals get through again, but slowly. That is the 142 milliseconds.

Now the heat. In a healthy axon, each node receives several times more current than it needs, so there is a large margin. In her repaired axons there is almost none. Warm her up. Channels open and close faster when they are warm, so the sodium channels shut sooner, and each action potential is a little shorter and delivers a little less current. A healthy axon does not notice. Her right optic nerve does. Half a degree Celsius is enough to fall below what the next node needs, and those axons stop conducting. She cools down, and they start again. Nothing new was damaged.

Treat it as an experiment. What would make each action potential last longer? Cooling does. Blocking potassium channels does too, because those channels end the action potential. Neither one repairs the myelin. They fix the electrical problem.
