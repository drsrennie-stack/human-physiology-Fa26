01

The baseline story

The chemical synapse, told as a story

The action potential has reached the end of the axon. The next cell is across a gap, and the electrical message cannot cross it. We are going to work out how the message gets across, one small piece at a time. We start with two people and a river.

  • Each part adds one new piece of information and then asks you a question you can already answer.
  • You draw along on your own sheet: the arrows, the labels, and what moves where.
  • A scientific name arrives only after you understand the idea it belongs to. There are no numbers today.

What we are building

The end of one cell, a gap, and the start of the next cell. By the end you can follow one message across in nine steps, say why it only goes one way, and predict what happens when one step is broken.

Open the draw along sheet and print it, or copy each drawing onto your own paper.

02

Part 1

A message, a river and no bridge

Two people stand on opposite sides of a wide river, and there is no bridge. One of them has a message for the other. Shouting does not carry across.

Ask

How could the message get across?

Answer

Put the message into something that can cross the water, for example a note wrapped around a ball, and send that across.

The spoken words never cross. The message changes its form for the crossing, and it is read again on the other side.

A message that cannot cross → put it into something that can.

03

Part 2

The end of the axon, and a gap

Here is our neuron from the last story. The action potential has traveled the whole axon and arrived at the end, full size. It travels along a membrane, because it is made by doors in that membrane opening one after another. At the end of the axon that membrane stops. The next cell has its own separate membrane, and between the two cells there is a narrow gap of fluid.

Ask

What happens to the electrical message when it reaches the gap?

Answer

It stops there. There is no membrane across the gap for it to travel along, so the electrical message cannot cross.

This is the river. The cell has to put the message into something that can cross a gap of fluid, and that something is a chemical.

Electrical message → gap → it cannot cross → send a chemical across.

04

Part 3

The chemical is ready and waiting

The chemical is made ahead of time and packed into little bags made of membrane. The bags are parked at the end of the axon, and some of them sit right against the membrane that faces the gap.

Ask

If the bags emptied at any moment, cell 2 could not trust what it received. What should tell the bags to empty, and only then?

Answer

The arrival of the electrical message, and nothing else.

So the end of the axon needs a part that can sense an electrical change. You already own a door that does exactly that: the voltage sensing door.

Message arrives → bags empty. No message → bags stay full.

Now name it: synaptic vesicle (the bag), neurotransmitter (the chemical), axon terminal (the end of the axon)

05

Part 4

Back to the nucleus: a voltage sensing door for calcium

Back to the nucleus of cell 1: gene, protein, membrane. The cell builds a voltage sensing door that lets only calcium, Ca2+, through, and places it at the end of the axon. Three facts: there is lots of Ca2+ outside and almost none inside, Ca2+ is positive, and the inside of the cell is negative.

Ask

When the action potential opens this door, which way does calcium go, and how many reasons does it have?

Answer

In, and it has two reasons, just like sodium. There is lots of Ca2+ outside and almost none inside, so concentration pushes it in. Ca2+ is positive and the inside is negative, so the electrical pull is inward too.

Both reasons are there all the time. Nothing enters at rest because the door is shut. The action potential supplies the open door.

For the brief moment at the peak of the action potential the inside is positive, so for that moment only the concentration reason is acting. It is large enough by itself, and the full lecture puts numbers on it.

Action potential arrives → voltage sensing door opens → Ca2+ comes IN.

Now name it: voltage gated calcium channel

06

Part 5

Calcium comes in and the bags empty

Ca2+ coming in is the signal. The bags that are parked against the membrane carry a sensor for Ca2+. When Ca2+ reaches them, the membrane of the bag joins the membrane of the cell.

Ask

When the bag's membrane joins the cell's membrane, where does the chemical end up?

And what would happen if there were no calcium outside the cell?

Answer

Outside the cell, in the gap. This was covered in Week 2: a bag joins the cell membrane and what was inside the bag is now outside the cell.

With no calcium outside, the message still arrives and the door still opens, because the door responds to voltage. But nothing comes in, so no bag joins the membrane, nothing is released, and cell 2 receives nothing.

Ca2+ comes IN → parked bags join the membrane → the chemical is released into the gap.

Now name it: exocytosis

07

Part 6

The chemical crosses the gap

Now the chemical is in the gap, and all of it is next to cell 1. Nothing carries it and nothing aims it. This is where the river comparison stops working, because nobody throws anything.

Ask

There is lots of the chemical next to cell 1 and none next to cell 2. Which way does it spread, and which rule from the balloon tells you?

Answer

Toward cell 2. Lots here, fewer there, movement toward fewer. You know this as diffusion.

Each molecule moves at random. The gap is so narrow that spreading across it takes almost no time.

Lots next to cell 1 → none next to cell 2 → the chemical spreads across.

Now name it: synaptic cleft (the gap)

08

Part 7

Back to the nucleus of cell 2: a door with a key hole

Cell 2 has been to its own nucleus: gene, protein, membrane. It has built doors with a key hole and placed them in the membrane that faces the gap. You know this door from the first story. It stays shut until the right key sits in the key hole.

Ask

What is the key?

When this door opens and positive ions come in, is that the large, full size signal or the small, local one?

Answer

The neurotransmitter is the key. Key fits, door opens, ions move through. At this door mostly Na+ comes in, so the inside of cell 2 becomes a little less negative, right at that spot.

That is the small, local change you already know. More neurotransmitter opens more doors and gives a bigger change, and the change fades with distance. It is a graded potential. Whether it leads to an action potential in cell 2 is the next story.

Key fits → door opens → ions move → a small, local change in cell 2.

Now name it: receptor

09

Part 8

Why the message goes one way only

Look at the whole drawing at rest and compare the two sides of the gap.

Ask

Which side has the bags? Which side has the key holes? So which way can a message go?

Answer

Only cell 1 has bags of the chemical parked at the gap, and only cell 2 has key holes facing them. So a message can go from cell 1 to cell 2, and it cannot go back.

Now the names. The whole connection is a synapse. Pre means before and post means after, so cell 1 is the presynaptic cell and cell 2 is the postsynaptic cell.

Bags on one side, key holes on the other → one way only.

Now name it: synapse, presynaptic cell, postsynaptic cell

10

Part 9

The same key can open different doors

A fire alarm rings. The students walk out of the building and the firefighters walk in. It is the same alarm, and what happens depends on who hears it. Acetylcholine is one neurotransmitter. On a skeletal muscle cell its receptor is the door itself. On a pacemaker cell of the heart its receptor is not a door. It passes the message on inside the cell, and a potassium door opens.

Ask

Na+ comes into the muscle cell. K+ leaves the heart cell. What does the same chemical do to each cell?

Answer

Muscle cell: positive charge comes in, the inside becomes less negative, the cell fires, and the muscle contracts.

Heart cell: K+ leaves, positive charge leaves, the inside becomes more negative, the pacemaker cell fires less often, and the heart slows down.

The chemical only says that a message has arrived. Each cell built its own receptor, back in its own nucleus, and the receptor is what turns the message into a response.

Same key, different receptor → different response.

Now name it: the receptor decides the response

11

Part 10

Families of keys

There are many neurotransmitters, and they come in families. At this level you need the family names and one or two members of each.

Ask

Glutamate is the main go signal in the brain and GABA is the main stop signal. From the last part, what makes one a go and the other a stop?

Answer

The receptors do, and not the chemicals themselves. Glutamate's receptors are doors for positive ions, which moves the cell toward firing. GABA's main receptor is a door for chloride, Cl−, which is negative, and that holds the cell away from firing.

There are two kinds of receptor, and you saw both in the last drawing. When the receptor is the door itself, the response is fast and brief. When the receptor is not a door and passes the message on inside the cell, the response is slower and lasts longer.

The full lecture lists seven families. These five are the ones to know first.

The family tells you what the key is made of. The receptor tells you what it does.

Now name it: ionotropic receptor (it is the door), metabotropic receptor (it is not a door)

12

Part 11

A doorbell with a stuck button

A doorbell with a stuck button rings without stopping. It cannot tell you whether one visitor came or ten. Now look at the cleft. The neurotransmitter is still there, the keys are still in the key holes, and the doors are still open.

Ask

What has to happen to the neurotransmitter in the cleft for the next message to mean anything?

Answer

It has to be cleared out of the cleft, and quickly.

A key does not stay in its key hole for long. It comes out again, and if the cleft has been cleared, no new key takes its place. The doors shut, and cell 2 can receive the next message as a new message.

Cleft cleared → key holes empty → doors shut → ready for the next message.

13

Part 12

Three ways to clear the cleft

Three new things are in the drawing. The gray shape and the boxes are proteins, so back to the nucleus: gene, protein, membrane. The box is a machine and not a door, like the pump.

Ask

Follow one molecule of neurotransmitter in the cleft. What are the three things that can happen to it?

Answer

1. An enzyme fixed in the cleft chops it up, and the pieces no longer fit the key hole. For acetylcholine the enzyme is acetylcholinesterase.

2. A transporter carries it back into the cell that released it, where it can be packed into bags again, or into a neighboring glial cell.

3. It drifts out of the open edges of the cleft.

The transporter is a machine in the same sense as the pump: it carries its cargo across the membrane. It is powered by the sodium difference that the pump maintains.

Chopped up, carried back in, or drifted away → the cleft is clear.

Now name it: enzyme breakdown (acetylcholinesterase), reuptake, diffusion

14

Part 13

Break one step

Every step causes the next one. So if you know which step has failed, you can predict what cell 2 receives.

Ask

For each one, does cell 2 get more signal or less?

A: the bags cannot fuse. B: the key holes are plugged. C: the clean-up enzyme is blocked.

Answer

A: less. No neurotransmitter reaches the cleft.

B: less. The neurotransmitter is released, but it cannot open the doors.

C: more, and it does not switch off, because the neurotransmitter stays in the cleft.

More is not better. Cell 2 needs a signal that turns on and then turns off. The full lecture names a toxin or a drug for each of these.

Find the broken step → everything after it changes.

One message has crossed one synapse.
