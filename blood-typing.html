What it is

A type and screen shows which blood a patient can safely receive.

The type finds the antigens on the red blood cells. The screen looks for unexpected antibodies in the plasma.

What you read

Clumping means an antibody found its antigen.

ClumpedPositive
SmoothNegative

The clumping of red cells is called agglutination.

Three parts

Every type and screen has three parts.

  • ABO group: A, B, AB or O.
  • Rh type: positive or negative.
  • Antibody screen: positive or negative.

ABO group

Forward and reverse typing must agree

Your plasma has antibodies against the ABO antigens your own red cells lack. That is why the two tests mirror each other.

Forward typing

  • Uses the patient's red cells.
  • Adds a antibody: anti-A in one well, anti-B in another.
  • Clumping means that antigen is on the cells.

Reverse typing

  • Uses the patient's .
  • Adds reagent red cells: A cells in one well, B cells in another.
  • Clumping means the plasma has an antibody against those cells.

When they do not agree

  • This is called an ABO discrepancy.
  • No ABO group is reported until the lab finds the cause.
  • One cause is a very low antibody level, which leaves a reverse reaction missing. This can happen in very old age or with an immune deficiency.
  • If red cells are needed before the cause is found, group O red cells are given.

Rh type

One well, one antigen

Rh typing is a forward test only. The patient's red cells are mixed with anti-D.

Reading it

  • Clumped with anti-D: Rh positive.
  • Smooth with anti-D: Rh negative.

Why there is no reverse test

  • Anti-A and anti-B are present from early life.
  • Anti-D is not. An Rh-negative person makes it only after exposure to Rh-positive red cells.
  • Exposure happens through a transfusion or a pregnancy.
  • Rh immune globulin is given to Rh-negative mothers to prevent this.

Antibody screen

Looking for the unexpected

The screen checks the plasma for red cell antibodies other than anti-A and anti-B.

How it is done

  • The patient's plasma is mixed with two or three group O reagent cells.
  • Group O cells have no A or B antigens, so anti-A and anti-B cannot react.
  • The cells carry other known antigens, such as D and K.

Negative screen

  • Every screening cell stays smooth.
  • No unexpected antibody was detected.
  • Red cells can be chosen by ABO and Rh type.

Positive screen

  • At least one screening cell clumps.
  • The lab runs an antibody panel to name the antibody.

Two rules

  • ABO: the donor cells must not carry an A or B antigen the patient lacks.
  • Rh: an Rh-negative patient is given Rh-negative red cells, so that they do not form anti-D.

The two extremes

  • O negative red cells carry no A, B or D antigens. They are used in an emergency before the type is known.
  • An AB positive patient has no anti-A or anti-B and can receive red cells of any type.
