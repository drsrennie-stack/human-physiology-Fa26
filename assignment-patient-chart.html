Your patient chart, all term.

Two patients, one at a time. Each chart is turned in when that patient’s file closes, with the analysis that reads it.

Part 1, Camila: due Sunday, November 1, 10:00 pm Pacific, with Midterm 1.
Part 2, Dale: due Wednesday, December 16, 10:00 pm Pacific, with Midterm 2.

Camila Reyes, 19 · Weeks 1 to 8

Second year kinesiology student and a midfielder on the college soccer team. No prior medical history, no medications. You follow her from her preseason physical in Week 1 to the clinic visit that closes her file in Week 8.

Dale Whitcomb, 68 · Weeks 9 to 15

Retired long haul driver with a failing heart, a damaged kidney and type 2 diabetes, who arrives already carrying all three and then gets an infection on top of them. You pick him up in Week 9 and keep him to the end of the term.

What this is

Both charts are in your chart book from the start, but you work on one patient at a time. Until Midterm 1 you work only on Camila. Each week you open that week’s case, write the new numbers into her chart, add to the problem list, draw the control loop, and answer the week’s questions. Nothing is collected week to week. When her file closes in Week 8, her chart and her analysis come in together, and Midterm 1 includes a question about her. Then you pick up Dale in Week 9, and his chart and analysis come in at the end of the term.

The chart is graded on the trend: whether you can read a row of columns side by side and tell a whole story, and whether your thinking changed as the evidence did. Say when you changed your mind. A chart that was never wrong scores lower than one that was wrong in Week 4, noticed it in Week 7, and said so.

The chart is the evidence. The analysis is the argument you build out of it, recorded as a chart walk: you on camera, reasoning out loud from your own chart. It has its own page, its own sections and its own rubric for each patient. Each part is worth half of the 5 percent.

Exactly what you upload

Part 1, Camila, due Sunday, November 1: the chart, as one PDF, in this order

  1. Camila's face sheet. Her name, your name, your entry point (the same one you use all term), and the August baseline.
  2. Camila's vitals flowsheet, with every week from 1 to 8 filled in, with units.
  3. Camila's intake and output flowsheet.
  4. Camila's lab flowsheet, units written the first time each test appears.
  5. Camila's problem list, numbered, with the week each problem was found, the numbers that were your evidence, and the week it resolved if it did.
  6. Camila's medication and intervention log.
  7. Her eight week pages, Week 1 through Week 8, each with the control loop drawn, the five questions answered, and your entry point question answered.
  8. Your AI disclosure on the last page, even if it is one line saying you used none.

Plus your chart walk for Camila: a 10 to 15 minute video of you reasoning out loud from her chart, with nothing else to read from.

Part 2, Dale, due Wednesday, December 16: the chart, as one PDF, in this order

  1. Dale's face sheet, then his four flowsheets in the same order, with the hemodynamic sheet last.
  2. Dale's problem list and medication log.
  3. His seven week pages, Week 9 through Week 15, filled in the same way.
  4. Your AI disclosure on the last page.

Plus your chart walk for Dale, recorded the same way.

Everything on the chart is handwritten, on the chart you printed. If you lose a page along the way, print that page again from the chart and fill it in; do not type it.

How to make the PDF

  1. Put the pages in the order above and check that every page has your name on it. The chart prints your name line on every page; fill it in if you have not.
  2. Scan or photograph every page in good light, straight on, one page per image. A phone scanning app (Notes on iPhone, Google Drive or Adobe Scan on Android) will make one PDF from all of them.
  3. Open the PDF and page through it once. Every number on the flowsheets must be readable. If a column is blurry, rescan that page. I cannot grade what I cannot read, and a blurry column counts as a blank one.
  4. Name the chart file with the patient in it: LastName_FirstName_Camila_Chart.pdf for Part 1 and LastName_FirstName_Dale_Chart.pdf for Part 2.
  5. Upload the chart PDF and your chart walk video to that part’s Canvas assignment and wait for the confirmation screen. If you upload the same file twice, the later one is the one I grade.

What a strong chart looks like

  • The flowsheets are complete. Every week, every value that week gave you, with units. A trend you cannot see is a trend you cannot reason about.
  • The problem list grew and changed. Problems appear the week you could name them, with the numbers that made you name them, and some of them resolve.
  • Each loop names its parts. The regulated variable, the sensor, the integrating center, the effector, and the direction of the response, drawn for that week's physiology, in your hand.
  • The week pages read as one person thinking. Week 8 reads Camila’s seven entries before it as one file. Week 15 reads both patients’ first blood gases again with everything the term taught you.
  • Your thinking changed where the evidence did, and you said so on the page.

A correct number with no explanation earns half credit. That was true every week, and it is true here.

Using AI on this one

Open, with disclosure, the same as the weekly cases. Use it to check a mechanism or push back on your own reasoning. The chart is handwritten, and the chart walk is you reasoning out loud from it, which is very visible when it is missing. Say what you used it for on the last page. Undisclosed use is the only version of this that is a problem.

If you fall behind on the chart during the term

Nothing is collected week to week, which means nobody will notice a gap until the chart is due except you. Catch a missed week the following week while the case is still fresh. Do not leave eight weeks of flowsheets for the last weekend; the point of the chart is that you were watching the patient week by week, and a chart filled in from memory reads exactly like one.
