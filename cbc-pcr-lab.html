Start here

A CBC counts blood cells. It does not give a diagnosis.

Every number on a complete blood count (CBC) answers one of three questions: How many cells are there? How big are they? What are they doing? The report does not tell you what is wrong with the patient. It tells you what the bone marrow, the spleen, the kidneys and the immune system are doing right now. Your job is to work backward from that behavior to the cause.

Working backward is the main skill in this lab, and here is why the lab is set up this way. If I give you a case and ask what is wrong, an AI can give you a confident answer in four seconds, and you learn nothing. So in this lab you write down the mechanism (how the body produced these numbers) before the choices appear. You build a case that has to pass a consistency check. You finish with a drawing that you explain out loud.

The three questions

  • How many? RBC, hemoglobin, hematocrit, white count, platelet count. These tell you how many cells there are. They change when the body makes more or fewer cells, when cells are destroyed, or when the blood is diluted.
  • How big and how full? MCV, MCH, MCHC, RDW. These describe the average red cell and how much the cells vary in size. They point to what the marrow was missing while it made the cells.
  • What are they doing? The differential (the percent of each type of white cell) and the reticulocyte count (young red cells). These tell you which part of the body’s defense is active and whether the marrow is making new cells to keep up.

Four habits that help you see the pattern

  1. Look at each cell line, not each number. There are three lines: red cells, white cells and platelets. Is one line abnormal, two lines, or all three? This one question narrows the possible causes more than any other step.
  2. Check cell size before the cause. If the patient has anemia, look at the MCV first. Small, normal and large cells each lead to a completely different set of causes.
  3. Ask whether the marrow is keeping up. The reticulocyte count tells apart two problems: a marrow that cannot make cells, and a marrow that is making cells as fast as it can while something destroys them.
  4. Check that the numbers agree. The red cell indices are calculated from each other, so they have to match. When they do not, the problem is the sample or the report, not the patient.

WBC4.5 to 11.0 ×103/µLTotal white cells in the blood. The count depends on how many cells the marrow releases, how many are stuck along the vessel walls (margination), and how many move out into the tissues. So it can rise because the body made more cells, or just because cells let go of the vessel walls during stress or after steroids.
RBC4.7 to 6.1 (M), 4.2 to 5.4 (F) ×106/µLRed cell count. It depends on erythropoietin (the kidney hormone that tells the marrow to make red cells), the supply of iron and other building materials, and the red cell lifespan of about 120 days.
Hemoglobin13.5 to 17.5 (M), 12.0 to 15.5 (F) g/dLHow much oxygen a given volume of blood can carry. This is the number that matches most closely with how the patient feels.
Hematocrit41 to 53 (M), 36 to 46 (F) %The percent of blood volume made up of packed red cells. Because it is a share of the volume, changes in plasma (the liquid part of blood) change it. Dehydration raises it even though no new cells were made.
MCV80 to 100 fLAverage red cell size (volume), calculated as hematocrit divided by RBC count. Low means the marrow ran short of the materials it needs to build hemoglobin and divided the cells an extra time. High means DNA copying fell behind the growth of the cytoplasm, so the cell got big before it could divide.
MCH27 to 33 pgAverage amount (mass) of hemoglobin in each cell. In most cases it rises and falls with the MCV.
MCHC32 to 36 g/dLHow concentrated the hemoglobin is inside the cell. This value has a firm upper limit. Hemoglobin cannot pack tighter than roughly 37 g/dL without forming crystals, so a value above that is almost always a measurement problem.
RDW11.5 to 14.5 %How much the red cell sizes vary. A rising RDW means the marrow is making new cells that differ in size from the older ones. That is why the RDW often changes before the MCV does.
Platelets150 to 450 ×103/µLCell fragments that break off from megakaryocytes (large cells in the marrow). The count falls when the marrow fails, when the immune system destroys platelets, when clotting uses them up, or when an enlarged spleen traps them. It rises with iron deficiency and with inflammation.
Reticulocytes0.5 to 2.5 %Young red cells released within about the last day. They still carry ribosomal RNA. This count shows how the marrow is responding to an anemia, and it is the single most useful test to add to a CBC.
Neutrophils40 to 70 %The first responders of innate defense (the defense you are born with). They rise fast in bacterial infection, tissue injury and stress. Bands are immature neutrophils. A rising band count means the marrow has used up its stored mature neutrophils.
Lymphocytes20 to 45 %T and B cells of adaptive defense (the defense that learns to target specific germs). They rise in many viral infections and in pertussis (whooping cough). They fall early in some acute viral illnesses and with cortisol.
Monocytes2 to 10 %They become macrophages in the tissues. They rise in long-lasting (chronic) infections, in infections that form granulomas (clusters of immune cells), and while the marrow recovers after being suppressed.
Eosinophils1 to 4 %They defend against parasites made of many cells, such as worms, and play a major role in allergic inflammation.
Basophils0 to 1 %White cells with granules that contain histamine. On their own they rarely point to a diagnosis, but a rise that lasts should make you ask about a myeloproliferative disorder (a marrow that overproduces cells).
ANC1.8 to 7.7 ×103/µLAbsolute neutrophil count, calculated as WBC times the sum of segmented neutrophils and bands. This number predicts infection risk. A normal percentage on a very low white count can still mean a dangerously low number of neutrophils.

      Section 4

      What PCR does

      A CBC tells you how the patient’s body (the host) is responding. It almost never tells you what the body is responding to. Neutrophils rise with strep, with a ruptured appendix and with a bad burn, and the blood count cannot tell those apart. To name the organism, you need a test that looks for the organism itself. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) finds it fastest and in the smallest amounts.

      PCR copies one specific stretch of DNA over and over until there is enough to detect. The primers are what make the test specific. Primers are short single strands of DNA made to match the sequences on each side of the target. If the organism’s genetic material is in the sample, the primers find it and copying begins. If it is not there, nothing gets copied.

      The PCR cycle: three steps, repeated about thirty to forty times

      1. Denature (separate the strands), near 95 °C (203 °F). Heat breaks the hydrogen bonds that hold the double helix together, and the two strands come apart. The DNA is not destroyed, because heat can break hydrogen bonds without damaging the sugar phosphate backbone.
      2. Anneal (primers attach), roughly 50 to 65 °C (122 to 149 °F). Cooling lets the primers bind to their matching sequences. This temperature controls how specific the test is. If it is too low, primers stick to sequences that only partly match, and that is where false positives come from.
      3. Extend (build the new strand), near 72 °C (162 °F). A heat stable DNA polymerase builds the matching (complementary) strand from free nucleotides. The polymerase has to survive being heated to 95 °C (203 °F) again and again, which is why it comes from bacteria that live in hot springs.

      Each cycle doubles the target. Thirty cycles starting from a single copy make more than a billion copies, which is why PCR can find an organism long before the patient's own antibodies would show up on a blood test.

      Three variations to know

      RT-PCR. Influenza, RSV, SARS-CoV-2, hepatitis C and HIV carry RNA, not DNA, and the polymerase cannot copy RNA. So the test starts with reverse transcriptase, an enzyme that builds a DNA copy of the RNA. Then the normal cycles run. When you see RT-PCR on a report, the organism was an RNA organism.

      Quantitative PCR and the Ct value. A glowing (fluorescent) signal gets stronger as copies build up. The Ct (cycle threshold) is the cycle where the signal becomes strong enough to detect. More starting material means the signal is detected sooner. So a low Ct means a lot of the organism, and a high Ct means very little. Ct does not measure how sick the patient is. It measures how much target was in that one swab, and the quality of the swab alone can change it by several cycles.

      Multiplex panels. Several sets of primers run in one reaction, so a single respiratory or gastrointestinal panel can report twenty or more organisms at once. This is convenient, but it also creates the problem described below.

      What a positive PCR does and does not prove

      It does tell you

      • Genetic material from that organism was present in that specimen.
      • Roughly how much was there, if the test is quantitative.
      • Sometimes whether a specific gene is present, such as a gene for drug resistance. That can change treatment without waiting for a culture.

      It does not tell you

      • That the organism is alive. PCR copies genetic material (nucleic acid) from dead organisms too, which is why a test can stay positive for weeks after someone recovers.
      • That the organism is causing the illness. Many people carry organisms without being sick, so a positive result on a multiplex panel may be an organism that is present but not causing the illness.
      • That the organism is absent when the test is negative. A test can be negative if the swab missed, if the sample was taken too early, or if the organism lives somewhere other than where you sampled.

      This is why your case needs both the CBC and the PCR. The CBC shows the body’s response, and the PCR names a likely organism. You need both, plus the patient’s story, to make a strong argument. If your case rests only on a positive PCR, a classmate can point out that the patient might just be a carrier.

      PCR

      Finds the organism’s genetic material (nucleic acid). Fast, within hours. Extremely sensitive. It cannot tell living organisms from dead ones. It does not show which drugs will work, except for the specific resistance genes it tests for.

      Culture

      Grows the organism. Slower, one to several days, and less likely to find the organism once antibiotics have started. Proves the organism is alive and gives full susceptibility testing (which drugs work against it).

      Serology

      Measures the patient’s antibodies. Antibodies take time to develop, so this test is poor early in an illness, and it can stay positive long after exposure. Useful when the organism is hard to sample directly.

      Check yourself before you build

      A patient finished treatment three weeks ago and feels well. A repeat PCR is still positive. What happened?

      Genetic material left over from organisms that are no longer alive is still in the sample, and PCR copies it anyway. This is why a cure is usually confirmed by culture or by how the patient is doing, rather than by repeating a PCR.

      A respiratory panel returns positive for two viruses. The patient has one illness. How do you argue which one matters?

      Go back to the body’s response and the timeline. Ct values give a rough idea of which organism is present in larger amounts. The differential pattern and the day the symptoms started have to fit the organism you choose. One of the two viruses may simply be left over from a recent illness. Your argument comes from all of the evidence together, not from the panel alone.

      Two patients, same organism, Ct of 18 and Ct of 34. Which one is sicker?

      You cannot tell. The patient with a Ct of 18 had far more target in that sample. But how the swab was taken, how many days into the illness it was taken, and where it was taken all change the Ct, separately from how sick the person is. Ct tells you about the sample more than about the patient.

      Why can PCR detect an infection days before serology can?

      PCR looks for the organism, which is present from the moment of infection. Serology looks for the patient’s antibody, which takes days to a couple of weeks to show up on a test. B cells first have to be activated, switch the type of antibody they make (class switch), and multiply. Early in an illness, the organism is there but the antibody is not yet.

        Where AI can help, and where it cannot

        You are expected to use AI in this lab. It is good at some parts and cannot do others, and learning which is which is part of this lab.

        Use it for

        • Asking why a mechanism works the way it does, after you have written your own answer.
        • Arguing the other side. Ask it to make the strongest case it can for the choice you rejected, then decide whether its argument is better than yours.
        • Practicing your explanation. Ask it to tell you where you were unclear.
        • Testing your own case. Ask it to find any contradiction, and fix any it finds.

        It cannot produce

        • Your path audit. It is a record of what you did, chart by chart, and only your own work can create it.
        • A drawing made by your own hand on paper.
        • An honest account of what changed in your own thinking, which is the graded part of every chart.
        • Your assigned organism, since most classmates have a different one and there is no published answer.

        Try this. Paste the numbers from chart 13 into any AI tool and ask what condition the patient has. It will very likely give you a confident diagnosis, because AI tools tend to go along with the question, and these numbers look like real lab results. Then run the same values through the index checker in section 3. Include that exchange in your log, along with the contradiction you found. It shows clearly why a tool that answers every question cannot be trusted to notice when the data in a question is impossible.

        Required decision points

        Your chart must include all six of these questions somewhere, in whatever order you found yourself using them.

        1. Do the numbers agree with each other? This one goes first for a reason, and one of the thirteen charts will show you why.
        2. How many cell lines are affected? One line, two lines, or all three.
        3. What size are the cells? Low, normal or high MCV. Look at the RDW at the same time.
        4. Is the marrow responding? Reticulocyte count, high or low.
        5. Which white cell is elevated? Neutrophils with or without bands, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils, or immature cells that should never be in the blood at all.
        6. Are the platelets involved? Are platelets the only problem, or part of a wider failure?
