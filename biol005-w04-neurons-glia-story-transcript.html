The whole idea in a few sentences

Read this before you record. If these sentences make sense, the rest is detail.

  1. A message may have to go a meter, so the cell grows one long extension and becomes a neuron with four regions: the dendrites and cell body take the message in, the trigger zone decides, the axon carries it and the axon terminal hands it on.
  2. Each region has the door that fits its job, and every door comes from a gene: ligand gated doors where chemical messages arrive, voltage gated sodium and potassium doors along the axon and packed most closely at the trigger zone, and voltage gated calcium doors at the terminal, where calcium moving in makes the terminal release its neurotransmitter.
  3. Neurons are sorted by the direction they carry the message (sensory toward the CNS, interneurons inside it, motor away from it) and by shape, which is a count of the extensions that leave the cell body.
  4. Glia keep the neuron working: astrocytes mop up K+ and used neurotransmitter and pass on fuel, microglia clean up, ependymal cells line the fluid spaces, and satellite cells look after cell bodies in the PNS.
  5. A bare axon leaks like a hose with holes, so its message has to be made again at every short step. Myelin, made by oligodendrocytes in the CNS and Schwann cells in the PNS, covers the leaks so the message is made again only at the nodes of Ranvier. Where myelin is lost the message is late or lost, which is multiple sclerosis in the CNS and Guillain-Barré syndrome in the PNS.
  6. All proteins are made in the cell body, so kinesin carries new parts out along microtubules and dynein carries worn out parts back, each step using ATP, and some viruses and tetanus toxin ride back with dynein.

The axon is a wire that is attached to the cell.Say it on Part 1 and again on Part 2: the axon is the cell. It is the same membrane and the same cytoplasm, with one nucleus back in the cell body.2, 3

02

Part 1. A message has a long way to go

50 sec

Here is the resting cell we built in Story 1, drawn small. Put it in your spinal cord. Its job is to tell a muscle down in your foot to contract, and that muscle is about a meter away. So far, our cell can only affect what is right beside it.

How could one cell carry a message that far?

The cell reaches the whole way itself. It grows one long, thin extension of its own membrane and cytoplasm, all the way to the muscle. Now the message can travel the whole distance inside one cell, which still has one nucleus. A cell built to carry messages like this is called a neuron.

DoAsk the question, wait, take a few ideas, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw two long parallel lines from the right side of the cell all the way to the muscle, and close them with a small bulb just before the muscle. Draw one arrow along it toward the muscle. Write: one long extension. Then write the name: neuron.
03

Part 2. One cell, four jobs

65 sec

Here is our cell after it has stretched. It is the same cell, with the leak doors and the pump from Story 1 still on the cell body, plus some short branches.

Follow one message through it. It has to come in somewhere. The cell has to decide whether to send it on. It has to travel the distance. And it has to be handed on to the next cell. Four jobs, four numbered places. Which does which?

Messages come in at place one, the branches and the cell body. Place four sits beside the next cell, so it hands the message on. Place three carries it. That leaves place two, where the long extension begins. Everything that came in arrives there, so the cell decides there. The message goes one way only.

The names of the jobs: input zone, trigger zone, conducting zone, output zone.

DoAsk the question. Let them match all four before you open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, under the four brackets write: comes IN, decide, TRAVELS, hand ON. Draw one long arrow above the axon from the cell body to the far end and write: one way. Then add the zone names under the jobs: input zone, trigger zone, conducting zone, output zone.
04

Part 3. Give the parts their names

60 sec

Now the parts get names, and you can work them out. Dendrite comes from the Greek word for tree. Axon comes from the Greek word for axle, a long straight rod. A terminal is the end of a line.

So which part of our neuron is the dendrites, which is the axon, and which is the axon terminal?

The short branches look like a tree, so those are the dendrites. The long extension is the axon. The swollen far end is the axon terminal. The part that holds the nucleus keeps its plain name, the cell body. And this funnel, where the cell body narrows into the axon, is the axon hillock. The trigger zone is the axon hillock and the first stretch of the axon.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, write dendrites beside the branches, axon above the long extension, and axon terminal beside the far end. Above the four brackets write: dendrites and cell body, axon hillock, axon, axon terminal. At the top write: the whole cell is a NEURON.
05

Part 4. Back to the nucleus: a door where messages arrive

55 sec

Now each region needs the right door. Story 1 ended with three kinds: the leak door, the door that needs a chemical key, and the door that senses an electrical change.

A message reaches the dendrites as a chemical, the grey dots. Another cell releases it, and it crosses a very narrow gap to our neuron. So which of the three doors belongs in the dendrites and the cell body, and why?

The ligand gated door, because the message arrives as a chemical. The chemical is the key. It binds, the door changes, and ions move through. Now the message is inside our neuron as a small electrical change.

Where does the door come from? Back to the nucleus. Gene, protein, membrane.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer. Point to the gene box in the nucleus and say: gene, protein, membrane.
DrawIn navy, in the close look frame, draw the ligand gated door over the question mark: two posts, a cup on top of the left post, and one grey dot sitting in the cup. Then draw three small copies on the membrane of the cell body and dendrites. In the nucleus box one gene, and draw an arrow from it to a squiggle and on to the membrane.
06

Part 5. Back to the nucleus: doors along the axon

65 sec

Inside the neuron the message is electrical: a change in the charge across the membrane. It has to travel the whole axon, where there are no chemical keys.

So which kind of door can respond to an electrical change? And where would you put it?

The voltage gated door. Back to the nucleus. The neuron builds voltage gated channels for {NAP} and for {KP} and places them all along the axon, so an electrical change at one spot can open the doors at the next spot.

Look where they are packed most closely: the trigger zone. That is what makes it the deciding spot. If the electrical change that arrives there is big enough, those doors open and the message is sent. If not, nothing is sent. How those doors make the message is the next story.

DoAsk the two questions one at a time. Then open Show the answer. Do not start the action potential.
DrawIn the second close look frame draw two voltage gated doors over the question mark: a navy one for Na+ and a rust one for K+, each with a small paddle carrying plus signs on its left post. Then draw small navy and rust doors along the top of the axon, three close together at the start of the axon. Write: packed closely at the trigger zone.
07

Part 6. A new ion and a new door at the far end

65 sec

At the axon terminal there is a gap before the next cell, and the electrical change cannot cross it. So the message has to become a chemical again. The terminal holds small packets of chemical key.

One new ion: calcium, {CAP}, the gold diamond. Lots outside, almost none inside. Back to the nucleus: the neuron builds a voltage gated door for calcium and places it in the terminal.

The electrical message arrives and this door opens. Which way does calcium move?

In. The concentration gradient pushes it in, and calcium is positive and the inside is negative, so the electrical pull is also in. Two reasons, the same as sodium.

The {CAP} that enters is the signal to the packets. They release their chemical key into the gap, and the message is handed on. The packets are synaptic vesicles, and the chemical key is a neurotransmitter.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn the third close look frame: in rust, an arrow from the diamonds outside down to the calcium door, for the concentration push. In navy, an arrow from the door into the cell, for the electrical pull. Write IN. At the terminal, draw three small grey dots in the gap beside the next cell and write in navy: Ca2+ IN, neurotransmitter released.
08

Part 7. Three neurons, one reflex

55 sec

You step on a tack, and your leg pulls back before you have thought about it. Three neurons in a row did that. One thing first: your brain and spinal cord together are the central nervous system, the CNS.

Follow the message from the tack to the muscle. Compared with the spinal cord, which way does each neuron carry it?

Neuron one carries it toward the spinal cord. Neuron two lies entirely inside the spinal cord. Neuron three carries it away, out to the muscle.

Now the names. Toward the CNS is a sensory neuron, also called afferent. Inside is an interneuron. Away from the CNS is a motor neuron, also called efferent. Most of the neurons you own are interneurons.

DoTrace the path with the cursor from the tack to the muscle. Ask the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw an arrow along each neuron in the direction of the message. Beside neuron 1 write TOWARD, sensory (afferent). Beside neuron 2 write INSIDE, interneuron. Beside neuron 3 write AWAY, motor (efferent).
09

Part 8. Sort neurons by shape: count the extensions

45 sec

Neurons also come in different shapes. To sort them, find the cell body and count the extensions that leave it.

How many extensions leave the cell body of each of these four neurons?

Neuron one, many. Neuron two, two. Neuron three, one, which then splits. Neuron four, many, but none of them is a long axon. The names say just that: multipolar, bipolar, pseudounipolar, anaxonic.

In the reflex, the sensory neuron was shape three, with its cell body off to the side. The other two were shape one, by far the most common.

DoAsk the question and have them count out loud for each neuron. Then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, circle each cell body. Under each neuron write the count: many, two, one that splits, no clear axon. Then write the names under the counts: multipolar, bipolar, pseudounipolar, anaxonic.
10

Part 9. A busy cell needs a support crew

70 sec

A cell this long and this busy needs a support crew. Every message lets a little {KP} out into the very narrow space around the neuron. Used chemical keys are left there too, the neuron needs fuel, and debris has to be cleared away.

From Story 1: what would happen to our resting cell if {KP} piled up just outside it?

With more {KP} outside, the push on {KP} to leave gets weaker. Less positive charge leaves, the inside becomes less negative, and the neuron could send messages that nothing asked for. Somebody has to mop up that {KP}.

That is cell one, the astrocyte. It mops up {KP} and used chemical keys, and passes fuel from the blood to neurons. Cell two, microglia, clean up debris and microbes. Cell three, ependymal cells, line the fluid filled spaces and move the fluid. Together they are called glia.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, draw arrows from two of the K+ circles to the astrocyte arm that touches the neuron. Draw one arrow from the blood vessel down into the astrocyte and write fuel. Write the three names and jobs beside the cells: astrocyte, microglia, ependymal cells. At the top right write: GLIA, the support cells.
11

Part 10. A hose with holes

85 sec

Our long axon has a problem. Its membrane is full of leak doors from Story 1, and as the electrical change spreads along the inside, charge leaks out through them. So the change reaches only a short way, and the voltage gated doors have to make the message again at every short step. That is slow.

Here is a garden hose with holes. Turn on the tap. How far along does a strong flow reach? Without touching the tap, how would you make it reach farther?

Not far, because water leaks out at every hole. So wrap the hose. The axon gets the same fix. Wrap it in insulation, and the electrical change reaches much farther before it has to be made again, so the message arrives sooner. The wrapping is called myelin.

Here is where the comparison stops. Water in a hose is never made again. The wrapping on an axon has short gaps, and at each gap voltage gated doors make the message again, full size. The gaps are nodes of Ranvier, and the neuron keeps its voltage gated doors packed there.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer. Say the sentence about where the comparison stops slowly.
DrawOn the hose, in navy: a thick arrow in at the tap, an arrow of water leaking out of every hole, and a very thin arrow at the far end. On the axon, in rust: draw four long sleeves around it, leaving three short gaps where the voltage gated doors are. Write myelin under a sleeve and node of Ranvier under a gap. In navy, draw curved arrows hopping from gap to gap and write: made again at each gap.
12

Part 11. Who does the wrapping

65 sec

Myelin is made by support cells. A cell winds its own membrane around the axon, again and again. Membrane is mostly fat, which scientists call lipid, and ions cannot cross it, so many layers make good insulation.

Cell one wraps pieces of several axons. Cell two wraps one piece of one axon. If one cell of each kind dies, which loss affects more axons?

Losing cell one. Every axon it served loses a piece of its wrapping.

Cell one, in the CNS, is the oligodendrocyte. Cell two works in the nerves, the peripheral nervous system or PNS. It is the Schwann cell. The PNS has one more support cell, cell three, the satellite cells. They look after neuron cell bodies the way astrocytes do in the CNS. That makes six kinds of glia, four in the CNS and two in the PNS.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, write oligodendrocyte beside cell 1 and: one cell, several axons. Write Schwann cell beside cell 2 and: one cell, one piece, one axon. Write satellite cells beside cell 3. Under the left panel write CNS, and under the right panel write PNS.
13

Part 12. Strip the insulation

60 sec

Here is the myelinated axon again. Suppose the myelin is stripped from one stretch. What is left there is bare membrane with its leak doors, and very few voltage gated doors, because the neuron had packed those at the gaps.

The message reaches the stripped stretch. What happens to it? Use the hose.

It leaks away. The electrical change fades across the bare stretch, and there are too few voltage gated doors to make it again. If enough is left to open the doors at the next gap, the message arrives, but late. If not, it does not arrive at all.

Losing myelin is called demyelination. In the CNS it is multiple sclerosis. In the PNS it is Guillain-Barré syndrome. The full lecture follows a patient.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw curved arrows hopping across the first two sleeves. Over the bare stretch draw a thin dotted curve, and short arrows leaking out through the bare membrane. Write: leaks away. At the far end write: LATE, or NOT AT ALL. In rust, at the bottom write: CNS, multiple sclerosis. PNS, Guillain-Barré syndrome.
14

Part 13. The supply line

70 sec

Back to the nucleus, one more time. Every door in this story came from a gene, and all the protein factories are in the cell body. The axon terminal is a meter away and has none. Proteins wear out, so the far end always needs new ones, and drifting there would take a protein longer than a lifetime.

How do the new doors get to the far end?

They are carried, like railway deliveries. A track runs the whole length of the axon. An engine picks up a packet of new proteins and moves along the track toward the far end, and it costs fuel, which is ATP.

The comparison stops here: the engine has no wheels. It is a protein with two feet, and it walks along the track, using ATP for every step. The track is a microtubule. The outward engine is kinesin. Outward is called anterograde. The whole system is axonal transport.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw one long line down the middle of the axon from the cell body to the far end: the track. In navy, draw a small circle above the track near the cell body with two legs down to the track, and an arrow pointing to the far end. Write ATP under its feet. Write: track = microtubule, engine = kinesin, OUT = anterograde.
15

Part 14. The return trip

60 sec

Worn out parts go back to the cell body to be broken down and reused, and news from the far end travels back the same way. A second engine walks the same tracks in the opposite direction, also using ATP for every step.

That engine picks up its load at the axon terminal, which is out in the body, in your skin or your muscle. What else could get on it?

Anything that gets into the terminal and can attach to the engine. Some viruses do this, such as rabies and herpes simplex, and so does tetanus toxin. That is how something that enters at a wound can reach the nervous system.

The names: the return engine is dynein, and back toward the cell body is called retrograde. Kinesin out, dynein back.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, draw a second engine hanging under the track near the far end, holding the worn out part, with an arrow pointing back to the cell body. Write ATP beside its feet. Write: engine = dynein, BACK = retrograde. Draw a small six sided virus outside the terminal with an arrow into it and write: viruses and tetanus toxin ride too.
