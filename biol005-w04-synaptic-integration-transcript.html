The whole idea in a few sentences

Read this before you record. If these sentences make sense, the rest is detail.

  1. Every synapse produces a small graded potential in the receiving cell. An EPSP is a small depolarization, usually from a cation channel letting sodium in. An IPSP comes from opening potassium or chloride channels, and it moves the membrane away from threshold or holds it below threshold.
  2. A chloride synapse can inhibit even when the voltage barely moves, because the chloride equilibrium potential is below threshold and the open channels let excitatory charge leak away. With this week's numbers the chloride equilibrium potential is minus 64 mV, slightly above rest. In many real neurons it is below rest. Either way it is below threshold.
  3. One EPSP is about half a millivolt and threshold is 15 mV away, so EPSPs have to add. They add over time, when one input fires rapidly, and over space, when many inputs fire together. They shrink with distance, so synapses near the cell body count for more.
  4. The trigger zone compares the running total with threshold. Take away the inhibitory inputs and ordinary excitation reaches threshold. That is tetanus.
  5. Presynaptic inhibition reduces calcium entry and transmitter release at one terminal, so it turns down one input and leaves the others alone. Postsynaptic inhibition turns down the whole cell.
  6. In long term potentiation, strong depolarization removes the magnesium block from NMDA receptors, calcium enters, and more AMPA receptors are added, so the same input gives a bigger EPSP afterward. Electrical synapses pass current directly through connexin channels. They are fast and keep cells firing together, and they cannot inhibit, amplify or be adjusted the way chemical synapses can.

Integration at the synapse

85 sec

This is the last lecture of Week 4, and it is where the week comes together. You know how a neuron holds a resting potential, how it fires an action potential, and how one synapse passes a signal across. Today's question is what happens when a neuron has thousands of synapses on it, some telling it to fire and some telling it not to. How does it decide?

We will do three things. First we look at the two kinds of small potential a synapse can make, the EPSP and the IPSP. Then we add them up with real numbers and decide whether the cell fires. Then we look at three ways the rules can change.

Here is the patient for today. A gardener steps on a rusty nail. A week later his jaw is clamped shut, and the slightest noise or touch throws his whole body into painful spasms. He is awake the whole time. His muscles and his motor neurons are structurally normal. A toxin from the wound has reached his spinal cord. What did it do there?

03

From many synapses to one decision

60 sec

This map is the whole lecture, and we read it from the top down. At the top, one neuron with thousands of synapses. Each synapse makes a small graded potential. Graded potentials were covered in the action potential lecture, and you know two things about them: they shrink as they spread, and they can add.

Some synapses are excitatory and some are inhibitory. Both kinds of potential spread toward the trigger zone, which is where the axon leaves the cell body. The trigger zone adds up whatever reaches it. If the total gets to threshold, about minus 55 millivolts, the cell fires one action potential. If it does not, the potentials fade and nothing is sent.

The name for this whole process is synaptic integration. The neuron takes in many inputs and gives one answer.

04

A synapse can depolarize the cell or hold it back

100 sec

Here are three synapses side by side. Look at panel one first. This is an excitatory synapse. Glutamate is released and binds the AMPA receptor, which is a channel for small positive ions. Sodium comes in. A little potassium goes out through the same channel, but much less, because at minus 70 the force on sodium is far bigger. So net positive charge enters, and the trace below goes up a few millivolts. That small depolarization is an EPSP, an excitatory postsynaptic potential.

Panel two is an inhibitory synapse. Here the receptor opens potassium channels. Potassium leaves, the cell loses positive charge, and the trace goes down, toward the potassium equilibrium potential at minus 95. That hyperpolarization is an IPSP, an inhibitory postsynaptic potential. It moves the cell away from threshold.

Panel three is also inhibitory, but look at the trace. It barely moves, and it moves slightly up. This receptor opens chloride channels. GABA A receptors and glycine receptors work this way, and they are the most common inhibitory receptors you have. Why a trace like that counts as inhibition is the next slide, and it is the hardest idea today, so we will take it slowly.

One more point. The transmitter does not decide whether a synapse excites or inhibits. The receptor does. You saw that last lecture with acetylcholine.

05

A chloride channel inhibits even when the voltage barely moves

2 min 20

Take your time with this slide. We need one rule from the first lecture this week: when you open channels for an ion, the membrane moves toward that ion's equilibrium potential. Open sodium channels and it moves toward plus 61. Open potassium channels and it moves toward minus 95.

Now find chloride on this scale. With the concentrations we have used all week, the chloride equilibrium potential is minus 64. Our cell rests at minus 70. So if I open chloride channels, the membrane moves toward minus 64. That is up, by a few millivolts at most. So why is that inhibition?

Look at where threshold is: minus 55. Minus 64 is below it. Opening chloride channels ties the membrane to a value that is below threshold. That is the first reason.

The second reason is the important one. Suppose an EPSP arrives while those chloride channels are open. Sodium comes in and the membrane starts to rise. As soon as it rises above minus 64, chloride, which is negative, flows in through the open channels and cancels part of that positive charge. It is like filling a bathtub with the drain open. The water still runs in, but the level does not rise the way it would with the drain closed. The EPSP comes out smaller. That is called shunting inhibition.

One honest caution. Many real neurons keep less chloride inside than our teaching cell does, which puts the chloride equilibrium potential below rest, around minus 75 or minus 80. In those cells chloride enters and you see a real hyperpolarization, and that is how your textbook draws it. Both pictures are right. The rule that always holds is this: if the channel moves the membrane toward a value above threshold, the synapse is excitatory, and if that value is below threshold, it is inhibitory.

DrawIn navy, circle rest at minus 70 and draw an arrow up to minus 64. Write: only 6 mV, and upward.
DrawIn rust, draw a bracket from minus 64 to the threshold line and write: still 9 mV below threshold.
DrawIn rust, write at the bottom: open Cl channels = drain open. EPSP gets smaller.

Three recordings from one neuron: which channel opened?

The flat trace is the interesting one, and it has two explanations. The first is that nothing happened: no transmitter, no channel. The second is that chloride channels opened in a cell where the chloride equilibrium potential equals the resting potential. There is no net force on chloride, so nothing moves, and yet the channels are open.

How would you tell them apart? Fire an excitatory input at the same time. If the EPSP is its normal size, nothing had opened. If the EPSP is smaller, chloride channels were open and shunting it.

07

One EPSP is far too small to fire the cell

80 sec

Here is the size problem. One excitatory synapse in the central nervous system gives an EPSP of about half a millivolt at the cell body. That is an approximate figure and it varies. To get from minus 70 to minus 55 you need 15 millivolts. So you would need about 30 of those at once, and that is if nothing is lost on the way.

This is very different from the neuromuscular junction, where one action potential in the nerve always fires the muscle. In the brain and spinal cord no single synapse decides anything. A motor neuron has something like ten thousand synapses on it.

The equation on the right is just bookkeeping. Start at minus 70, add the EPSPs that arrive, subtract the IPSPs that arrive, and compare the total with minus 55. Adding them as plain numbers is a simplification, but it is a good one and it is how we will work the problems. Before the next slide, think of two different ways a cell could collect 15 millivolts from small inputs.

08

Two ways to add: over time and over space

70 sec

Here are the two ways. Start with panel two. One input fires five times, 5 milliseconds apart. Look at the trace. The first EPSP rises and starts to fall, but before it has fallen far the second one arrives and starts from that higher level. Then the third, then the fourth. It climbs like a staircase, and the fifth one reaches threshold. That is temporal summation: one input, added over time.

Now panel three. Four different inputs fire at the same moment. Their EPSPs reach the trigger zone together and add to about 15 and a half millivolts, and the cell fires right away. That is spatial summation: many inputs, added across space.

Panel one is the control. Same input as panel two, but the action potentials are 25 milliseconds apart. Each EPSP has almost gone before the next arrives, so nothing builds. Temporal summation only works when the EPSPs come close together in time.

DrawIn rust, trace the staircase in panel 2 step by step and count aloud, one to five. In panel 1, mark the gap between two EPSPs and write: too late.
DrawIn navy, circle the four filled inputs on the little neuron in panel 3 and write: all at once.
09

Temporal summation with numbers

75 sec

Let us put numbers on panel two. One EPSP is 5.4 millivolts at the trigger zone, and after its peak it falls by half about every 10 milliseconds. Threshold is 15 above rest. Try the three cases yourself before I open the work.

If the input fires every 10 milliseconds, half of each EPSP is left when the next arrives. So the peaks go 5.4, then 5.4 plus 2.7 is 8.1, then 9.5, then 10.1, and they level off under 10.8. Never 15. The cell does not fire.

If it fires every 5 milliseconds, about 71 percent is left each time. Now the peaks go 5.4, 9.2, 11.9, 13.8, 15.2. The fifth one gets there, and the cell fires.

So what has to be true about timing? The next EPSP has to arrive before the last one has decayed. And this is how the frequency code is read. A faster train of action potentials in the presynaptic axon gives a bigger depolarization in the next cell.

Where a synapse sits changes how much it counts

65 sec

Graded potentials shrink as they spread, because charge leaks out through the membrane along the way. So location matters. Here are three identical synapses. Each makes 6 millivolts at its own site. The one close to the cell body arrives at the trigger zone as 5.4. The one in the middle arrives as 3.3. The one far out on the dendrite arrives as only 1.2.

Now look at the inhibitory synapse on the cell body. Every EPSP from every dendrite has to pass that spot to reach the trigger zone. So an inhibitory synapse there is in a position to cancel a great deal of excitation at once. Inhibitory synapses really are concentrated on the cell body and the first part of the axon.

The exact rule for how fast potentials shrink is a teaching value. Real dendrites vary.

12

Divergence and convergence

40 sec

Two wiring words. Divergence is one neuron branching to reach many cells. Convergence is many neurons ending on one cell, and that one cell integrates them all. A spinal motor neuron is the classic case of convergence. Sensory input, commands from the brain and local inhibitory interneurons all end on it, and its one output decides whether a muscle contracts.

The record holder is the Purkinje cell in the cerebellum, with something on the order of one to two hundred thousand synapses on a single cell.

14

Presynaptic inhibition turns down one input only

80 sec

So far all the inhibition has been on the receiving cell. There is another place to put it. Look at the left panel. Three excitatory terminals, A, B and C, end on one neuron. A modulatory neuron ends on terminal A itself. That is an axoaxonic synapse, axon onto axon.

Its transmitter reduces the calcium that enters terminal A when an action potential arrives. You know from last lecture that calcium entry is what makes vesicles fuse. Less calcium, fewer vesicles, less transmitter, smaller EPSP from A. Now look at the bars. A is cut in half. B and C have not changed at all, because the receiving cell has not changed.

Compare the right panel. Here the inhibitory synapse is on the receiving cell. Now all three bars fall. So presynaptic inhibition is selective, and postsynaptic inhibition is global. The body's best known use of this is pain. Your own enkephalins, and opioid drugs, act on the terminals of pain fibers in the spinal cord and reduce what they release.

15

Cut one input by half and leave the others untouched

45 sec

Here is a design problem. A neuron has three excitatory inputs. You need to cut A by half and leave B and C exactly as they are. You get one inhibitory synapse. Where do you put it? Pause and decide.

It has to go on the terminal of A. Anywhere on the receiving cell, and you lower B and C too. The word for what presynaptic control gives you is selectivity.

There is also the opposite, presynaptic facilitation, where the modulatory input lets more calcium into the terminal and more transmitter is released.

16

Synapses change with use

55 sec

Now the second way the rules change. A synapse is not a fixed connection. Its strength depends on its history, and that is called synaptic plasticity.

Some changes are brief. In a short rapid train, each EPSP can be bigger than the last, because calcium is still in the terminal from the action potential before. That is facilitation. In a long train, the EPSPs get smaller, because the terminal is running short of ready vesicles. That is depression. Both are in the terminal, and both are gone within seconds to minutes.

But you can remember things for years, so something has to last. The best understood lasting change is long term potentiation, LTP, and the next slide takes it one step at a time.

DrawIn navy, put an up arrow beside Facilitation and a down arrow beside Depression. In rust write: both fade in minutes.

17

How a glutamate synapse becomes stronger

115 sec

This is the second place to slow down. Read the three panels left to right. In panel one, a single action potential arrives. Glutamate crosses and binds two receptors. The AMPA receptor opens, sodium comes in, and you get an ordinary small EPSP. The NMDA receptor also has glutamate on it, but look at its channel. There is a magnesium ion sitting in it like a plug. The inside of the cell is negative, magnesium is positive, and that attraction holds the plug in place. So almost nothing passes through the NMDA receptor.

Panel two. Now the input fires a rapid burst. Lots of glutamate, lots of AMPA receptors open, EPSPs adding on top of each other, and the membrane depolarizes strongly. The inside is no longer negative enough to hold the magnesium, so the plug leaves. The NMDA channel still has glutamate on it, so now it is open, and calcium comes in.

Picture a door that needs two people at the same moment, one to turn the handle and one to pull out the doorstop. Glutamate turns the handle. Depolarization pulls out the doorstop. One action potential by itself only does the first.

Calcium is a second messenger. It switches on protein kinases, and they put more AMPA receptors into the membrane. Panel three is an hour later. Same synapse, same single action potential, same amount of glutamate, but more AMPA receptors to catch it, so the EPSP is bigger. The synapse has been strengthened.

18

The experiment, and what it does and does not show

95 sec

Here is LTP as an experiment. We test the synapse with a single action potential once a minute and measure the EPSP. Before the burst it is steady at 100 percent. At time zero we give one burst of rapid stimulation. The EPSP jumps, settles at a larger size, and stays there for the rest of the hour.

Now the control. Block the NMDA receptors and repeat it. Ordinary transmission is normal, because AMPA receptors carry it. After the burst there is a brief rise, which is that short term facilitation in the terminal, and then it goes right back to 100 percent. No lasting change. So AMPA carries the everyday signal, and NMDA carries the signal that something should change.

There is also the reverse, long term depression, where slow prolonged stimulation leads to AMPA receptors being taken out of the membrane.

A word of caution about what this shows. Most neuroscientists think changes like these are part of how we learn and remember, and there is good evidence for that in animals. But nobody can yet point to a synapse and tell you which memory it holds. LTP is a well supported mechanism, and the full story of memory is still being worked out.

19

Electrical synapses pass current directly

75 sec

The third change to the rules is a synapse with no transmitter at all. At an electrical synapse the two cells are joined by gap junctions. Each channel is built from proteins called connexins, six in one membrane and six in the other, lined up to make one pore straight from the inside of one cell to the inside of the next.

Ions flow directly through. So if cell 1 depolarizes, cell 2 depolarizes almost immediately. Look at the time axis. The electrical synapse responds almost at once. The chemical synapse takes half a millisecond to a millisecond, because calcium has to enter, vesicles have to fuse, and transmitter has to cross and bind.

Current can usually go either way through a gap junction, so cells joined like this tend to fire together. That is exactly what you want in the heart, and you also find gap junctions in smooth muscle, between glial cells and between some neurons.

DrawIn navy, trace an ion straight through a connexin channel from cell 1 to cell 2. In rust, mark the two gap widths: 3.5 nm on the left and 20 to 40 nm on the right.
DrawOn the time axis, circle the gold dot and the navy bar and write the word delay over the bar in rust.

Chemical and electrical synapses compared

50 sec

Here they are side by side. The electrical synapse is faster and keeps cells in step. But look at what it cannot do. It cannot turn excitation into inhibition. It cannot amplify. And there is very little to adjust.

The chemical synapse is slower, and in exchange it can do everything we covered today: EPSPs and IPSPs, presynaptic control, plasticity. Try the two jobs in the predict box, then open the answer.

The heart wall needs speed and unity, so it uses gap junctions. A motor neuron that has to ignore a signal on some occasions and respond on others needs chemical synapses. The delay is the price of being able to integrate.

Take the inhibition away

65 sec

Now back toward the patient. This is a spinal motor neuron. It is receiving excitation all the time, from sensory neurons and from the brain. It is also receiving inhibition from interneurons that release glycine or GABA, and that inhibition is what keeps it below threshold most of the time.

22

What the toxin did in his spinal cord

100 sec

So here is the gardener, explained. The bacteria in the wound made tetanus toxin. Motor nerve endings took it up, and retrograde axonal transport carried it up the axon to the spinal cord. That transport system was covered in the neuron lecture. That trip is part of why it took a week.

In the spinal cord the toxin crossed into the inhibitory terminals that end on the motor neuron, and it cut a protein that vesicles need in order to fuse. Those terminals could no longer release glycine or GABA.

So the IPSPs dropped out of the sum. Your prediction on slide 2 should have been that the toxin removes inhibition. Every ordinary touch and sound now brings his motor neurons to threshold. And because the inhibition that normally keeps opposing muscles from contracting together is gone, they all contract at once. The jaw closing muscles are strong, so the jaw locks.

Think about it as an experiment. If the missing piece is inhibition, what could put inhibition back? The GABA A receptors on the motor neuron still work, so a drug that strengthens them helps. And less noise and touch means fewer EPSPs in the first place. Open the box for the comparison with botulinum toxin, which does the same thing at a different synapse and gives the opposite result.
