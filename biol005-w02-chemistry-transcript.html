04

Why water dissolves some things and not others

60 sec

Water is polar. Oxygen pulls the shared electrons toward itself, so the oxygen end is slightly negative and the two hydrogens are slightly positive.

That is the whole basis of dissolving. Those partial charges let water grab anything else that carries charge, surround it, and carry it away from its neighbours. A molecule with no charge anywhere gives water nothing to hold, so water pushes it aside and it stays clumped with its own kind. That is why oil beads up.

Three words carry this all term. Hydrophilic, dissolves. Hydrophobic, does not. Amphipathic, both ends in one molecule, and that last one is the entire reason a cell membrane exists.

DoRead the prediction box out loud and pause. Do not answer it here; the next slide answers it.

05

Solubility decides where a molecule can go

55 sec

Here is the answer, and it is more useful than it first looks.

The hydrophobic drug slips straight through a membrane, because the middle of a membrane is oily and so is the drug. But it cannot dissolve in plasma, so it has to travel bound to a carrier protein.

The hydrophilic one is the mirror image. It dissolves in plasma without help, and then it cannot get through a membrane without a protein to carry it.

Neither one is better. They are different problems. And notice what solves both of them. A protein.

A window the body defends harder than any other

60 sec

pH. The scale runs 0 to 14, and arterial blood lives in a window from 7.35 to 7.45, which is narrower than anything else the body defends.

The part people miss is that this scale is logarithmic. Each step is ten times the hydrogen ion concentration of the step beside it. Going from 8 to 6 is not twice the acid. It is a hundred times the acid.

And notice the other compartments. Your stomach runs at 1 to 3 on purpose, because the enzyme that works there needs it.

13

Twenty building blocks, and one variable part

65 sec

Four levels of structure. Primary is the order, secondary is local folding, tertiary is the full three dimensional shape, quaternary is several chains together.

Here is the thing I want you to notice, and it is the reason this competency exists. Only the primary structure is held by strong covalent bonds. Everything above it is held by weak bonds.

That is deliberate, because it lets a protein flex when something binds it. But it also means heat and pH can destroy the fold without touching the chain at all.

DoClick any one of the four figures to open it larger if you want to point at the beta sheets.
14

Four levels of structure

55 sec

Denaturation is losing the shape while keeping the chain. Same amino acids, same order, wrong fold, no function.

Heat does it by shaking the weak bonds apart. pH does it by changing which R groups carry a charge, so the ionic bonds that held the fold either break or form where they should not.

Now answer the question on the right. Our patient is at pH 7.18. Which step of that chain is happening in them right now?

DoPause on the prediction. The answer is all four steps, and it is worth letting them sit with that.
15

The shape is the job

50 sec

Binding. A ligand is anything that binds a protein: a substrate, a hormone, a drug, an ion.

Specificity is which one it binds, and that comes down to shape and charge matching. Affinity is how tightly it holds on once bound. And binding is reversible, which sounds like a footnote and is not, because reversibility is the only reason a signal can ever be switched off.

Underneath, every protein job in the body is the same three steps. Something binds, the protein changes shape, the shape change does the work. Enzymes, transporters, receptors, motors. All the same mechanism wearing different clothes.

16

Binding is how a protein does anything

50 sec

Bond angles, ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces. All of them pulling on the chain at the same time, and the shape they settle into is what the protein can do.

The one covalent bond that helps hold a fold together is the disulfide bond, sulfur to sulfur between two cysteines. Insulin is two chains held that way. A permanent wave works by breaking and re-forming them in hair.

17

What an enzyme is, and what it is not

60 sec

An enzyme speeds a reaction up without being used up by it. It does that by lowering the hill in the middle, the activation energy.

Look at what the enzyme does not change. The substrate starts at the same energy. The product ends at the same energy. The enzyme has not made the reaction release more energy and it has not made an impossible reaction possible.

All it has done is make the hill lower, so at body temperature far more molecules have enough energy to get over it. Enzymes are about rate, and rate is what physiology is made of.

DrawTrace both curves with the pen, dashed first, then solid, and land on the gap between the two peaks.
18

How fast, and what sets the ceiling

60 sec

Rate against substrate. It climbs, it bends, and then it stops climbing.

The ceiling is V max, and that is where every binding site is busy. Past that point, adding more substrate does nothing at all. K m is a measure of grip: the concentration that fills half the sites, so a small K m means the enzyme grabs substrate even when there is barely any around.

I want to be very clear about this. You are never going to calculate either of those in this class. What I will ask you is which way the curve moves and what that means for the cell.

Turning an enzyme on

50 sec

Three ways to turn an enzyme on. Cut it, give it a cofactor, or bind a modulator somewhere else.

Allosteric just means other site. Anything that binds away from the working site and changes the fold is allosteric, and it can switch a protein on or off.

And the obvious question is why build a protein that does not work. Open the box.

23

Turning an enzyme off, two different ways

55 sec

Two ways to turn one off, and telling them apart is worth real points.

A competitive inhibitor sits in the binding site itself. They are competing for the same spot, so flooding the system with substrate wins it back.

An allosteric inhibitor binds somewhere else and bends the site. More substrate does not help, because the site itself is the problem. Look at the two curves: on the left the ceiling never moved. On the right it fell and stayed fallen.

26

What the cell actually spends energy on

60 sec

Now the second half of the lecture: energy.

A reaction that releases energy is exergonic. One that needs energy put in is endergonic, and on its own it simply does not happen. Coupling is the trick: run the two together so one pays for the other.

And ATP pays for exactly three kinds of work. Building things, moving things uphill, and physically moving parts. All three are happening in you right now, and all three stop within minutes if ATP stops.

27

ATP is a rechargeable battery, not a fuel tank

60 sec

This is the thing about ATP that students most often have backwards, so I am going to say it slowly.

ATP is a rechargeable battery, not a fuel tank. You hold about fifty grams of it at any moment, and you turn over something close to your whole body weight of it in a day.

That only works because it is recycled constantly. Spend it to ADP, recharge it back to ATP, thousands of times a day per molecule. So everything in the rest of this lecture is not making ATP out of nothing. It is recharging.

DoRead the prediction box. The answer is seconds to a couple of minutes, and their instinct will be hours.

29

Stage 1, glycolysis

55 sec

Stage one, glycolysis. Cytosol, no oxygen, net two ATP and two NADH, and it leaves you with two pyruvate.

It costs two ATP to get started and makes four, so the net is two. That investment at the front is why a cell that is already out of ATP is in serious trouble.

And it is the only stage that runs without oxygen, which is what makes it the emergency route. A mature red blood cell has no mitochondria at all, so this is the only way it makes ATP for its entire life.

DoOpen the reveal on the NAD question. It sets up the lactate slide, so do not skip it.
30

Stages 2 and 3, into the mitochondrion

60 sec

Stages two and three, both in the mitochondrial matrix.

Stage two is a link reaction, not a cycle. Pyruvate loses a carbon as carbon dioxide and becomes acetyl CoA. Two NADH, no ATP. And because carbon has left, you cannot rebuild glucose from what is left, which is why fat does not convert back into glucose in any useful amount.

Stage three, the citric acid cycle, turns twice. Two ATP, six NADH, two FADH two, four carbon dioxide. And look at that: two ATP is almost nothing. The cycle's real job is stripping electrons off carbon and loading them onto carriers.

31

Stage 4, where the oxygen goes

70 sec

Stage four, and this is where the oxygen finally shows up.

The carriers hand their electrons to a chain of proteins in the inner membrane. As the electrons move down the chain, those proteins pump hydrogen ions out of the matrix, building a gradient. Then the hydrogen ions flow back in through ATP synthase, and that flow is what spins it and makes the ATP.

Oxygen sits at the very end and accepts the spent electrons, making water. That is its entire job. Nothing else.

So here is the question. If oxygen only works at the end, why do stages two and three stop when oxygen runs out?

DoPause on the question, then open the reveal. This is the single most important reveal in the deck.
DrawDraw the chain as four boxes with electrons flowing right, then put an X at the end and show it backing up.
32

Adding it up, and why your book may disagree

50 sec

The arithmetic, and an honest note about your textbook.

Four ATP made directly, twenty-eight from the carriers, which is about thirty-two.

If your book says thirty-six or thirty-eight, you did not read it wrong. Those older numbers assumed each NADH buys exactly three ATP. The measured values are lower and not whole numbers, because the membrane leaks some protons. For this course, thirty to thirty-two, and be able to explain why it is a range.

SaySay directly that being able to explain a disagreement between two sources is worth more to you than memorizing either number.

With oxygen and without

55 sec

With oxygen and without. Same pyruvate, two completely different fates.

With oxygen, about thirty to thirty-two ATP, and the products are carbon dioxide and water, both easy to clear. Without it, two ATP, and the product is lactate.

And I want to correct something you have probably heard. Lactate is not a waste product and it is not a poison. Making it is what lets glycolysis keep running when the mitochondrion cannot help. But it is an acid, and making a lot of it fast is exactly how our patient got to 7.18.

35

The patient, start to finish

80 sec

Now go back and check the prediction you made at the start.

Oxygen delivery fell first, because the blood pressure was too low to push blood through the tissue. The lactate rose second, because the cell fell back on glycolysis. The bicarbonate fell third, because it was spent buffering that acid. And the pH fell because the buffer ran out before the acid did.

But look at the dashed arrow at the bottom. Falling pH slows every enzyme in the cell, including the enzymes of glycolysis, which is the only pathway still making any ATP. So the cell gets worse at the one thing keeping it alive.

That is positive feedback, and positive feedback does not correct itself. Which is why the treatment is not giving bicarbonate. Giving a buffer treats the number on the report. Restoring blood pressure treats the first box in the chain.
