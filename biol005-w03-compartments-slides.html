BIOL 005 · Week 3

Compartments, cells and tissues

Start

Last week was the molecules. This week is where they live. A cell is not a bag of chemistry, it is a set of rooms, and the walls between the rooms are what let physiology happen.

  • Today we go from the whole body down to a single organelle, then back up through junctions, tissues and one organ, the skin.
  • The theme is compartmentation: keeping things apart so they can be controlled. Every membrane you meet today is a wall with a job.
  • Six problems are worked on the slides. Try each one before the answer opens.

Silverthorn chapter 3. Week 3 competencies 1 and 2, and the anatomy behind transepithelial transport in Week 4.

The problem for today

A patient has a burn over 40 percent of their body. They lost very little blood. Yet by the next morning their blood pressure has fallen, their hematocrit has climbed, they have been given six liters of IV fluid, and their face and legs are swollen. Where did all that water go, and why did damaged skin send it there? By the end of this lecture you will be able to trace every step.

03

Compartments

One body, many rooms

Mechanism
  • A compartment is any space with a boundary between inside and outside. The body has them at every size, from the cranial cavity down to a single vesicle inside a cell.
  • Compartments keep processes apart. Digestive enzymes stay in the gut. Calcium stays out of the cytosol until a signal lets it in. Acid is made inside a lysosome and nowhere else.
  • Inside the three big cavities sit smaller fluid filled ones: the circulatory system, the eyes, the cerebrospinal fluid, and the thin films in the pleural and pericardial sacs.
  • The rule: wherever the body needs two different sets of conditions, it builds a wall. Find the wall and you have found where the control happens.
04

Compartments

Some lumens are outside the body

Mechanism
  • A lumen is the hollow inside of any hollow organ: the heart, a blood vessel, the airways, the gut.
  • Here is the part that surprises people. The lumen of the gut and the lumen of the airways are open to the outside world at both ends. Anything sitting in them has not yet entered the body. It is in a tunnel that runs through the body.
  • The lumen of the heart and blood vessels is different. It is sealed, and the fluid in it, plasma, is extracellular fluid. That lumen is inside.
  • So a molecule in the gut has to cross the gut wall to get in, and a molecule in the blood has already made it. That one distinction organizes all of digestion and all of breathing.

Not yet

No. The stomach lumen is a widened part of the tunnel that runs from mouth to anus, and the coin is in the tunnel. It will pass straight through without ever crossing a cell membrane. Compare that with the glucose in the same stomach: to be used it has to be absorbed across the gut wall, and only then is it in the body.

The same logic says that air in the lungs is still outside. Oxygen becomes part of the body only when it crosses the wall of an alveolus into the blood.

Hold onto this

Every time a slide this week says "crosses the epithelium", it means crossing from the outside world into the extracellular fluid. Which epithelium, and what the crossing costs, is the rest of the week.

05

Fluid compartments

Where the body keeps its water

Mechanism
  • Intracellular fluid (ICF) is the water inside all the cells put together. It is one compartment on paper, but it is really trillions of separate rooms.
  • Extracellular fluid (ECF) is everything outside the cells. It has two parts. Plasma is the fluid part of blood, inside the vessels. Interstitial fluid bathes the cells, outside the vessels.
  • The wall between plasma and interstitial fluid is the capillary wall. The wall between interstitial fluid and ICF is the cell membrane. Two walls, three compartments.
  • Last week's control loops were all about keeping the ECF steady. Cells only ever feel the interstitial fluid around them, so the ECF is the internal environment that homeostasis defends.

Worked problem 1

How much water, and in which room?

Solve

A 70 kg adult is about 60 percent water. Divide that water into the compartments.

The fractions you are given
Total body water60 percent of body weight, and 1 kg of water is 1 L
Intracellular fluidtwo thirds of total body water
Extracellular fluidone third of total body water
Plasmaabout one quarter of the ECF
Interstitial fluidthe rest of the ECF

Find total body water, ICF, ECF, plasma and interstitial fluid, in liters. Then say how much blood the patient has if blood is 40 percent cells by volume.

Solved

  1. Total body water. 70 kg × 0.60 = 42 L.
  2. Intracellular fluid is two thirds of that. 42 × 2/3 = 28 L. Most of the body's water is inside cells.
  3. Extracellular fluid is the remaining third. 42 − 28 = 14 L.
  4. Plasma is about a quarter of the ECF. 14 × 1/4 = 3.5 L, usually rounded to about 3 L. Interstitial fluid is the other three quarters, about 10.5 to 11 L.
  5. Blood is plasma plus cells. If cells are 40 percent of blood volume, plasma is 60 percent, so blood volume = 3 L ÷ 0.60 = 5 L. That is the number to remember: about five liters of blood, three of them plasma.
  6. The picture to draw: a 42 L tank, two thirds of it inside cells, and of the outside third, only about a quarter is in the vessels. The blood is the smallest compartment and the only one we can sample easily.

Worked problem 2

What a blood test is actually measuring

Solve

A nurse draws blood and the lab reports sodium 140 and potassium 4.0, both in mmol/L. Which compartment do those numbers describe, and what do they let you say about the other two?

Typical values, so you can see the pattern
IonECF, mmol/LICF, mmol/L
Sodium, Na+140about 15
Potassium, K+4about 140
Chloride, Cl−105about 5
Bicarbonate, HCO3−24about 10
Phosphate and protein anionslowhigh

Solved

  1. The needle is in a vein, so the sample is blood. The lab spins the cells out and measures the liquid, so the numbers describe plasma.
  2. Plasma and interstitial fluid are separated by a capillary wall that lets ions and water through freely. So for small ions, plasma is a fair stand-in for the whole ECF. Sodium 140 in plasma means sodium about 140 in the fluid around every cell.
  3. The ICF is a different story. Sodium is low inside and potassium is high, the reverse of outside. A plasma potassium of 4.0 tells you almost nothing about the 140 inside the cells, because the cell membrane keeps the two apart.
  4. Say it as a rule: a blood test reads the ECF directly and the ICF only by inference. When a patient is low on potassium, the plasma number is the tip of the iceberg.
  5. One trap. If the sample is handled roughly and red cells break open, their ICF spills into the plasma and the potassium reads high, sometimes 6 or 7. Nothing is wrong with the patient; a wall was broken in the tube. The lab calls it a hemolyzed sample.

Nursing

A plasma electrolyte tells you about the fluid outside the cells, not inside them.

Example: A patient with a normal plasma potassium can still be badly short of potassium inside their cells after days of vomiting. Replacement is watched over days, not fixed with one bag.

Radiologic technology

Iodinated contrast stays in the extracellular fluid, so it lights up the vessels first and the tissue spaces second.

Example: On a CT with contrast, a kidney tumor enhances in the same phases as the blood supply that feeds it, because contrast is following the plasma and then leaking into the interstitial fluid.

Medicine

A hemolyzed sample is a broken compartment, not a sick patient.

Example: A potassium of 6.8 drawn through a tiny needle with a lot of suction gets repeated before anyone treats it.

Respiratory therapy

Fluid in the pleural sac is fluid in a compartment that should be nearly empty.

Example: A pleural effusion of a liter sits between the lung and the chest wall and stops the lung expanding, which shows up as breathlessness before the blood gases change.

08

Membranes

Two things called a membrane

Mechanism
  • A tissue membrane is a sheet of cells, usually one layer of flattened cells on a thin bed of connective tissue. The pericardium, the pleura and the lining of the gut are tissue membranes. They separate body compartments.
  • A cell membrane is the phospholipid bilayer around a single cell, about 8 nanometers thick. It separates the ICF from the ECF. Every tissue membrane is built from cells that each have one.
  • When a slide this week says membrane without saying which, it means the cell membrane. That is the one that decides what gets in.
09

Membranes

What the cell membrane is for

Mechanism

Four jobs, and every one is a physiology chapter

  • Physical isolation. The bilayer keeps the ICF and the ECF from mixing. Without it, the ion differences from problem 2 could not exist.
  • Regulation of exchange. Ions, nutrients, waste and water all cross, but only where the membrane allows. Next lecture is entirely about this.
  • Communication. Receptors in the membrane are how the cell hears a hormone or a neurotransmitter. Week 4 and Week 8.
  • Structural support. The membrane is tied to the cytoskeleton inside and to other cells and the matrix outside, which is how cells hold a shape and stay in a tissue.

The membrane is mostly lipid and protein with a little carbohydrate, arranged as the fluid mosaic model: a fluid sheet of lipid with proteins floating in it like tiles in a mosaic.

Worked problem 3

Read the composition and guess the job

Solve

Three membranes, three very different recipes. Before you open the answer, match each recipe to what the membrane does.

Table 3.1, composition of selected membranes
MembraneProteinLipidCarbohydrate
Red blood cell membrane49%43%8%
Myelin around a nerve fiber18%79%3%
Inner mitochondrial membrane76%24%0%

Which one is mostly insulation, which one is mostly machinery, and why does one of them have no sugar at all?

Solved

  1. Lipid is the barrier. Protein is the working part. So a membrane that is nearly all lipid is built to block, and a membrane that is nearly all protein is built to do things.
  2. Myelin at 79 percent lipid is insulation. It wraps a nerve fiber in layer after layer of membrane so that current cannot leak out. Almost no work happens in it, so it needs almost no protein. Week 4.
  3. The inner mitochondrial membrane at 76 percent protein is where ATP is made. It is packed with the electron carriers and the ATP synthase, so it is more machine than wall.
  4. The red cell membrane is in between: transporters and structural proteins on one side, a flexible lipid sheet on the other, so the cell can squeeze through capillaries narrower than itself.
  5. Carbohydrate sits only on the outer face of a cell membrane, where it labels the cell for other cells to read. A mitochondrion is inside a cell and never meets another cell, so it has no labels and no carbohydrate. The red cell, which has to be recognized as self by the immune system, has the most. Your blood type is written in those sugars.
11

Membrane lipids

Phospholipids build the wall by themselves

Mechanism
  • A phospholipid is a water loving head on two water hating tails. In water the tails hide together and the heads face out, so the membrane builds itself with no energy spent.
  • Two rows back to back make a bilayer with an oily middle that water and ions cannot cross. That oily middle is why a cell can keep its ICF different from its ECF.
  • A micelle is a solid droplet, heads out; the gut makes them to carry digested fat. A liposome is a hollow sphere with water inside, a tiny compartment used to carry drugs.
  • The other membrane lipids are sphingolipids, which are longer, and cholesterol, which wedges between the tails.
12

Fluid mosaic

The membrane up close

Mechanism
  • Integral proteins are built into the bilayer. Most are transmembrane, crossing it once or many times: channels, carriers, pumps and receptors. Lipid anchored proteins hang on a fatty acid or a GPI anchor.
  • Peripheral proteins sit loosely on one face and can be lifted off. Many are the link to the cytoskeleton.
  • Cholesterol does two opposite things: warm the membrane and it stops the tails swinging freely, cool it and it stops them freezing solid. A fluidity buffer, not a stiffener.
  • A double bond kinks a tail, and kinked tails cannot pack tightly, so more unsaturated tails means a more fluid membrane. Fluid matters: proteins have to drift sideways to meet each other.
13

Fluid mosaic

Lipid rafts, and why the mosaic is not random

Mechanism
  • Sphingolipids and cholesterol cluster into raised patches called lipid rafts, and lipid anchored proteins gather on them. A receptor, its enzyme and its signaling partners end up in one place, so a signal passes in a fraction of a second instead of waiting for random collisions.
  • So the mosaic is organized: proteins are grouped where the job needs them, not scattered evenly.

Carbohydrates: the outside only

  • Sugars on lipids (glycolipids) and on proteins (glycoproteins) form the glycocalyx, a coat on the outer face. It protects the surface and it is the label other cells read: blood group antigens and the markers for self are all here.

Nursing

The sugars on the outside of a red cell are the blood type, and the wrong type is destroyed.

Example: A type O patient given type A blood makes antibodies that bind the A sugars, and the transfused cells burst inside the vessels within minutes. That is why two nurses check the bag.

Radiologic technology

Contrast agents and tracers are designed around whether they can get past the oily middle of a membrane.

Example: Gadolinium contrast for MRI stays in the extracellular fluid because it is charged and water loving; it cannot slip through the bilayer into cells.

Medicine

Cholesterol in a membrane is normal and necessary; cholesterol in a vessel wall is the problem.

Example: Every cell needs cholesterol to keep its membrane fluid. Statins lower blood cholesterol, and cells keep making their own for membranes regardless.

Respiratory therapy

Surfactant in the lung is a phospholipid layer doing the same trick as a membrane, heads to water and tails to air.

Example: A premature baby without surfactant has alveoli that collapse on every breath, because nothing is spreading across the water surface to lower its tension.

Inside the cell

The map of a cell

Mechanism
  • Every cell carries the same DNA. Cells differ because each switches on a different set of genes. That is differentiation: a muscle cell fills with contractile protein, a gland cell with secretory vesicles.
  • The cytoplasm is everything inside the membrane except the nucleus, and it holds four things.
  • Cytosol, the semi-gelatinous fluid. This is the ICF from problem 2.
  • Organelles, the working structures, most wrapped in their own membrane: compartments inside a compartment.
  • Inclusions, particles with no membrane: stored nutrients and ribosomes.
  • The cytoskeleton, protein fibers that give the cell shape and move things around.
15

Inside the cell

A cell, cut open

Mechanism

How to use this picture

  • Find the nucleus first, then follow the membrane that comes off it: that is the endoplasmic reticulum, rough where ribosomes sit on it, smooth where they do not.
  • Between the ER and the cell membrane sits the Golgi, the packing room. Vesicles leave it heading for the surface or for storage.
  • Mitochondria are scattered wherever ATP is used most. In a muscle cell they line up beside the contractile fibers. In this cell there are only a few.
  • The free surface with microvilli tells you this cell faces a lumen and absorbs. That surface is the apical membrane, and it comes back on slide 30.

Follow the product

Antibody is a protein that leaves the cell, so it is made on ribosomes attached to the rough ER, then finished and packaged in the Golgi. A plasma cell is mostly rough ER, stacked in layers, with a large Golgi beside the nucleus. You can tell what a cell does from which organelle it has overbuilt.

16

Inside the cell

The nucleus

Mechanism
  • The nuclear envelope is two membranes, so the nucleus is a compartment with a double wall. It is continuous with the ER, which is why the ER is drawn coming off it.
  • The envelope is pierced by nuclear pore complexes, protein gates that let RNA and proteins pass in both directions under control. The compartment is sealed to most things and open to the right ones.
  • Inside, DNA is wound around proteins as chromatin. The dark spot, the nucleolus, has no membrane; it is where ribosomal RNA is made and ribosomes are assembled.
  • The nucleus is the control center in the sense that it holds the instructions. It does no chemistry of its own beyond copying DNA and writing RNA.

Why put DNA in a room of its own

Separation lets the cell edit the message before it is used.

Messenger RNA is written in the nucleus, trimmed and checked there, and only then sent out through a pore to the ribosomes. If the DNA sat in the cytosol, ribosomes would start building from an unfinished message. A wall between writing and building is quality control.

18

Organelles

Protein synthesis is compartmentation in action

Mechanism
  • Every protein starts the same way: a gene is copied into messenger RNA in the nucleus, and the RNA leaves through a pore to find a ribosome.
  • Then the path splits. A protein that will work in the cytosol is built on a free ribosome and simply released. A protein that will be secreted, or sit in a membrane, or work in a lysosome, is built on a ribosome that docks on the rough ER and is fed straight into the ER lumen.
  • From there it moves in vesicles: ER to Golgi, Golgi to the surface or to a lysosome. It is sorted and modified at each stop, and it stays inside membrane the whole way.

One line

Nucleus, pore, ribosome on rough ER, ER lumen, transport vesicle, Golgi, secretory vesicle, fusion with the cell membrane when blood glucose rises, extracellular fluid, capillary, plasma. Every arrow after the ribosome is a membrane bound compartment. The cell never lets a secreted protein loose in its own cytosol.

19

Inclusions

Things that sit loose in the cytosol

Mechanism
  • Inclusions have no membrane, so they sit in direct contact with the cytosol.
  • Two of them store fuel. Glycogen granules are branched chains of glucose, ready to be broken back down when the cell needs sugar. Liver and muscle are full of them. Lipid droplets are triglyceride, the long term store. A fat cell is one giant droplet with the rest of the cell squeezed to the edge.
  • Ribosomes are RNA plus protein and they build proteins. Free ribosomes in the cytosol build proteins for the cytosol. Fixed ribosomes on the rough ER build proteins for export. Ten to twenty ribosomes reading one RNA at once are a polyribosome.
  • Proteasomes are barrel shaped protein complexes that chew up proteins the cell has tagged for destruction: defective ones, and signals whose job is done.

Perturb it

A drug blocks proteasomes. Which builds up first, worn out proteins or the short lived signaling proteins the cell was about to destroy? What would a cell that depends on quickly removing a growth signal do next? (This is how bortezomib works against myeloma, and the answer is that the cell dies once it cannot clear what it makes.)

21

Cytoskeleton

Motor proteins walk the tracks

Mechanism
  • A motor protein grips cargo with its tail and a fiber with its two heads, and burns ATP to swing one head past the other. So it walks.
  • Myosin walks on actin. In muscle it is the pull.
  • Kinesin and dynein walk on microtubules carrying vesicles and organelles, in a nerve fiber up to a meter each way. Kinesin mostly goes out, dynein mostly comes back.
  • Dynein is also the motor inside every cilium and flagellum: it slides neighboring microtubules past each other, and the sliding becomes a bend.

Why this matters later

Everything a cell secretes reaches the surface on a motor protein, and everything a nerve ending needs travels down the axon on one. Week 4, axonal transport, is these two motors on a very long track.

22

Cytoskeleton

Cilia and flagella

Mechanism
  • Cilia are short and many. They beat in waves and push fluid across the surface: mucus up out of the airways, an egg along the oviduct. The single still primary cilium on most cells is a sensor.
  • A flagellum is long and single. The sperm is the only human cell with one, and it moves the cell through fluid instead of moving fluid past the cell.
  • Both share one core: nine pairs of microtubules around a central pair, 9 + 2, driven by dynein, growing from a basal body, which is a centriole.
  • The centrosome beside the nucleus holds two centrioles, each a barrel of nine triplets. It organizes the microtubules and directs the spindle in cell division.
  • Break dynein and every cilium in the body stops. Hold onto that for slide 41.
23

Tissues

From cells to tissues: the matrix between them

Mechanism
  • A tissue is a group of cells with a shared job, plus the material they secrete around themselves. Histology is the study of tissues.
  • That material is the extracellular matrix. The cells make it and secrete it, and the recipe changes from tissue to tissue: watery in blood, a gel under the skin, rock in bone.
  • The matrix has two parts. Ground substance is a gel of proteoglycans and glycoproteins that holds water. Protein fibers run through it: collagen for strength, fibronectin and laminin to tie cells to the matrix, elastin where the tissue has to recoil.
  • The matrix is not packing material. It carries signals, it decides how stiff a tissue is, and cells anchor to it to know where they are. A cell that loses its grip on the matrix usually dies.

Two things hold a tissue together

Cells grip each other, and cells grip the matrix.

Both grips are made by membrane spanning proteins called cell adhesion molecules, and the grips are organized into junctions. The next two slides are the catalog. It matters because when a junction fails, the tissue fails at exactly that joint, and the failure tells you which junction it was.

Epithelia

What every epithelium has

Mechanism
  • An epithelium is a sheet of cells, one layer or several, covering a surface or lining a cavity or tube. Every surface where the body meets the outside world is one.
  • It sits on a basal lamina, the basement membrane, a thin matrix layer the cells secrete and are pinned to by hemidesmosomes. Blood vessels stop at it, so an epithelium is fed by diffusion from below.
  • Classified by structure: simple or stratified by layers, squamous, cuboidal or columnar by shape. Or by function, which is the classification physiology uses.
  • The most useful single word: tight or leaky. A tight epithelium, sealed by tight junctions, controls everything that crosses. A leaky one lets water and small solutes slip between the cells.
28

Epithelia

Five functional kinds

Mechanism
  • Read the table as cause and effect. An epithelium built for exchange is one layer of flat cells with gaps, because thin and leaky is fastest. One built for protection is many layers riveted with desmosomes, because it has to be rubbed and not tear.
  • A transporting epithelium is one layer of tall cells, sealed with tight junctions and covered in microvilli, because it has to move chosen substances one way and stop everything else. That is the one the rest of this week is about.
29

Epithelia

Where each kind is

Mechanism
  • Follow one molecule of oxygen. It crosses the airway lining (ciliated), then the alveolar wall (exchange), then the capillary wall (exchange again, called endothelium in a vessel), and it is in the plasma.
  • Follow one glucose. It crosses the gut lining (transporting), then a capillary wall (exchange), then later leaves the blood across another capillary wall to reach a muscle cell.
  • Every route in and out of the body is a sequence of epithelia, and the rate limiting one is usually the transporting or exchange step. That is why the same five words come back in every organ system.
30

Epithelia

Exchange and transporting, side by side

Mechanism
  • Exchange epithelium is as thin as a cell can be, with pores. Built for speed, both directions, no selection. Gas exchange in the lung and every capillary bed use it.
  • The transporting cell is polarized: the apical membrane facing the lumen carries different proteins from the basolateral membrane facing the ECF, and the tight junction stops them mixing. Microvilli multiply the apical surface; mitochondria pay for transport against a gradient. Week 4 asks you to trace glucose across this cell.

Nursing

The gut wall is a transporting epithelium, so what gets absorbed depends on which transporters are there.

Example: Oral rehydration solution works because glucose and sodium share the same apical transporter; the glucose in the drink is what pulls the salt and water in.

Radiologic technology

Capillary walls in the brain are the exception: no pores, and tight junctions instead, so contrast normally stays out.

Example: Enhancement on a brain MRI means the blood brain barrier has opened, because gadolinium can only reach brain tissue where that exchange epithelium has been damaged.

Medicine

Polarized cells put a pump on one side and a channel on the other, and drugs work by hitting one of them.

Example: A loop diuretic blocks one apical transporter in one segment of the kidney tubule, and the whole kidney loses salt.

Respiratory therapy

The alveolar wall is an exchange epithelium one cell thick, and thickening it slows every gas.

Example: In pulmonary edema, fluid between the alveolar cell and the capillary makes the barrier thicker, and oxygen, which crosses less easily than carbon dioxide, falls first.

31

Epithelia

Ciliated, protective and secretory

Mechanism
  • Ciliated epithelium lines the nose, trachea and upper airways and the female reproductive tract. It moves fluid and whatever is in it.
  • Protective epithelium is stratified and sits wherever there is rubbing or chemical attack: skin, mouth, esophagus, vagina. It prevents exchange rather than doing it.
  • Secretory epithelium makes and releases a product. Cells can be scattered, like goblet cells, or grouped into glands. Exocrine glands release to the outside world, usually down a duct: sweat, saliva, digestive enzymes, mucus. Their secretions are watery (serous) or sticky (mucous). Endocrine glands have no duct and release hormones into the ECF, where the blood picks them up.
32

Epithelia

How a gland is built, and why it decides exocrine or endocrine

Mechanism
  • Every gland begins as ordinary surface epithelium that pushes down into the connective tissue below.
  • If the stalk stays and hollows out, the gland keeps a duct to the surface. Its product goes back out to the lumen or the skin. Exocrine.
  • If the stalk is lost, the secretory cells are stranded in the connective tissue next to capillaries. Their only way out is into the ECF and then the blood. Endocrine.
  • So the difference between a sweat gland and the thyroid is whether a tube survived. And it tells you why a hormone has to be a molecule the whole body can be exposed to, while a digestive enzyme can be as harsh as it likes: one is released into the internal environment and the other into the tunnel that is outside.

Two populations

Most of the pancreas is exocrine: clusters of cells with ducts that carry digestive enzymes to the gut lumen, outside the body. Scattered among them are the islets, endocrine cells with no duct that release insulin and glucagon into the capillaries beside them. Same organ, two glands, decided by whether each cluster kept its tube.

Connective tissue

Cells in a matrix they made

Mechanism
  • Connective tissue is the opposite of an epithelium: cells scattered, matrix everywhere. The matrix is the tissue.
  • Fixed cells live there. Fibroblasts build the matrix. Adipocytes store fat. Their names carry a code: a -blast is building, a -cyte is maintaining, a -clast is breaking down. Osteoblast, osteocyte, osteoclast.
  • Mobile cells pass through: the blood cells, and macrophages that patrol for invaders.
  • The ground substance ranges from water (blood) through gel (under the skin) to rock (bone). The fibers set the mechanics: collagen resists pulling, elastin snaps back, fibrillin makes sheets and filaments, fibronectin glues cells on.

Tissues

Muscle and neural tissue, briefly

Mechanism

Muscle

  • Muscle tissue is contractile: it generates force and movement. Three kinds. Skeletal muscle moves the skeleton and is under conscious command. Cardiac muscle is the heart, branched cells joined by gap junctions and desmosomes so they pull together. Smooth muscle lines hollow organs and vessels and works without your attention.
  • Week 7 is muscle. Today, notice only that cardiac muscle needs both junctions from problem 4 at once: a tunnel to share the signal and a rivet to survive the pull.

Neural

  • Neural tissue has two cell types. Neurons carry signals as electrical and chemical messages, sometimes over a meter. Glial cells outnumber them and support them: insulating, feeding, cleaning up.
  • Muscle and neural tissue are both excitable, meaning the membrane you studied on slide 12 can generate an electrical signal. How it does that is Week 4, and it depends on the ion differences from problem 2 and the transporters from next lecture.

Tissue remodeling

Tissues are rebuilt all the time

Mechanism
  • Cells die two ways. Necrosis is death by injury: the cell swells, bursts, and spills its contents, which sets off inflammation. Apoptosis is programmed death: the cell tidily shrinks and packages itself, and neighbors eat the pieces with no inflammation. Your skin surface, gut lining and blood cells are replaced this way continuously.
  • New cells come from stem cells that divide by mitosis. Totipotent cells can become anything including placenta, only in the first days of an embryo. Pluripotent cells can become any tissue of the body. Multipotent adult stem cells, in bone marrow, skin, gut and elsewhere, are limited to a family of cell types.
  • Plasticity is a tissue's ability to reorganize: the brain rewiring after injury, or a tissue changing its cell type in response to a chronic irritant.

Two deaths

At the burn itself, cells were killed outright by heat. That is necrosis, and the leaked cell contents are one reason the area becomes inflamed and the nearby capillaries start to leak. In the intact skin around it, the epidermis keeps turning over exactly as before, old surface cells dying by apoptosis and being pushed off, with new ones rising from the multipotent stem cells at the base. Healing of the burn depends on those stem cells, which is why a deep burn that destroys the base layer cannot re-cover itself and needs a graft.

Organs

The skin: all four tissues in one organ

Mechanism
  • An organ is two or more tissues working together. The skin has all four: a protective epithelium, connective tissue in the dermis and hypodermis, smooth muscle at each hair, and sensory nerves throughout.
  • The epidermis is stratified protective epithelium. Cells are born at the base, fill with keratin as they rise, die, and are shed from the top. It has no blood vessels; it is fed from the dermis.
  • The dermis is loose connective tissue carrying the blood supply, the nerve endings, the sweat and sebaceous glands and the muscles that raise hairs.
  • The hypodermis is adipose tissue: insulation, padding and a fuel store.

Organs

The epidermis up close, and what holds it on

Mechanism
  • Two things stop water leaving the body through the skin: the lipid at the surface, and the sheer number of cell layers. Burn the epidermis off and both are gone. Water then evaporates from the exposed dermis at many times the normal rate.
  • Two things stop the skin coming apart: desmosomes between cells, and hemidesmosomes onto the basal lamina. Each can fail on its own, and slide 41 shows what each failure looks like.

Nursing

A burn wound has lost its barrier, so it leaks water out and lets bacteria in until it is covered.

Example: Burn dressings are chosen to hold moisture in and keep organisms out, doing the job of the epidermis until the base layer regrows or a graft takes.

Radiologic technology

Skin is the first thing the beam meets, and it gets the highest dose of any tissue in a long procedure.

Example: After a long fluoroscopy case the operator checks the entry site over the following weeks, because the basal stem cells are the most radiation sensitive cells in the skin and the redness shows up late.

Medicine

The depth of a burn is the depth of which layer was destroyed.

Example: A burn that spares the base of the epidermis heals from its own stem cells; one that reaches the dermis heals from the hair follicles and glands; one through the whole dermis cannot heal itself.

Respiratory therapy

The skin and the airway are both barriers, and inhalation injury burns the airway one.

Example: A patient pulled from a house fire is watched for airway swelling for a day, because the ciliated epithelium of the trachea can slough and swell just as skin does.

Worked problem 6

Back to the patient: where the water went

Perturb

Blood pressure 88/50, hematocrit up from 40 to 50, six liters of fluid given, five kilograms heavier, swollen. Write the chain on paper before you open the box. Every arrow has to be something from today.

What you know
Blood volume on arrival5 L, of which 3 L plasma and 2 L cells (problem 1)
The burned skinEpidermis destroyed over 40 percent of the body
The capillaries near a burnInflamed, and their exchange epithelium now leaks plasma protein as well as water

Part A. Where did the water go, compartment by compartment? Part B. Use the hematocrit to work out how much plasma left the vessels before any fluid was given.

Solved

  1. Two walls failed. The protective epithelium is gone over 40 percent of the skin, so water evaporates straight off the dermis and leaves the body. That is real loss, and the patient would have weighed less if nothing had been given.
  2. The exchange epithelium of the capillaries near the burn is inflamed and leaking. Normally water and small solutes cross but protein stays in the plasma. Now protein crosses too, so nothing is holding water in the vessels. Plasma pours out of the blood into the interstitial fluid. That is the swelling.
  3. Part B, the hematocrit. The cells never left, so they are still 2 L. Hematocrit is cells divided by blood volume. At 50 percent, blood volume = 2 ÷ 0.50 = 4 L, so plasma is now 2 L. One liter of plasma, a third of it, moved into the tissue spaces before anyone hung a bag.
  4. Less plasma means less blood volume, so the heart has less to pump, so pressure falls and the heart rate climbs to compensate. That is the 88/50 and the 128.
  5. The six liters of IV fluid went into the vessels, and most of it followed the leak straight out into the interstitial space. The patient is heavier and more swollen, and still under filled in the compartment that matters for pressure. Fluid keeps being given until the capillary walls seal, usually over the first day, after which the interstitial water slowly returns to the plasma and is passed as urine.
  6. The answer to slide 2: the interstitial fluid. Not the cells, whose membranes are intact, and not the gut. The water is in the ECF, in the wrong half of it.

Perturb

Break one part and predict the failure

Perturb

Perturbation 1: the motor

A child is born without working dynein arms. No other protein is affected. Using slides 21 and 22, predict every place in the body where something stops moving, and what each stopped movement causes over the years.

Primary ciliary dyskinesia

  1. Dynein is the motor of every cilium and flagellum. Without it the 9 + 2 structure is built but cannot bend.
  2. Airway cilia do not beat, so mucus and trapped bacteria sit in the lungs and sinuses instead of being swept out. Chronic cough, sinus infections and repeated pneumonia from infancy.
  3. Sperm flagella do not beat, so the sperm cannot swim. Cilia in the oviduct do not sweep, so an egg struggles to reach the uterus. Infertility in both sexes.
  4. In the embryo, cilia set up the left right layout of the organs. In about half of these children the heart and other organs end up on the mirror side, called situs inversus. Same protein, one defect, four systems.

Perturbation 2: the rivets

Two patients have blistering skin. Patient A makes antibodies against a cadherin found in desmosomes. Patient B makes antibodies against a protein of the hemidesmosome. Using slide 39, predict where in the skin each one splits.

Same symptom, different wall

  1. Patient A. Desmosomes hold epidermal cells to each other. When they fail the cells let go of their neighbors and the skin splits within the epidermis. The blister roof is thin, breaks easily, and rubbing intact skin makes it slide. The disease is pemphigus.
  2. Patient B. Hemidesmosomes hold the bottom layer to the basal lamina. When they fail the whole epidermis lifts off the dermis and the split is under the epidermis. The blister is tense and full thickness and does not break with a touch. The disease is bullous pemphigoid.
  3. The lesson is the one from problem 4 in reverse: tell me where the tissue came apart and I will tell you which junction was attacked.
