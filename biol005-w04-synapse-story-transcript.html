The whole idea in a few sentences

Read this before you record. If these sentences make sense, the rest is detail.

  1. The action potential travels along a membrane, so it stops at the end of the axon, where there is a gap of fluid before the next cell.
  2. The cell sends a chemical across. The neurotransmitter waits in synaptic vesicles parked at the axon terminal.
  3. The arriving action potential opens voltage gated calcium channels, and Ca2+ comes in for two reasons: there is far more of it outside, and the inside is negative.
  4. Ca2+ is the signal that makes the parked vesicles join the membrane and empty into the cleft, which is exocytosis, and the neurotransmitter then spreads across the cleft by diffusion.
  5. On the postsynaptic cell the neurotransmitter is the key for a receptor. When that receptor is a ligand gated channel, ions move and the cell gets a small, local change, a graded potential. The message goes one way because only one side has vesicles and only the other has receptors, and the receptor decides the response.
  6. The signal ends when the neurotransmitter is cleared by an enzyme, by reuptake or by diffusion, so blocking release gives less signal and blocking the clean-up gives more signal that does not switch off.

Part 1. A message, a river and no bridge

40 sec

Two people are standing on opposite sides of a wide river. There is no bridge. This person has a message for that person. She shouts, and the sound does not carry across.

How could the message get across?

Put the message into something that can cross. Write a note, wrap it around a ball, and send the ball over. Notice that the spoken words never crossed the river. The message changed its form for the crossing, and the other person reads it on the far side.

DoAsk the question, take two or three suggestions, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw two or three sound curves from the first person's mouth and stop them at the water with an X. In rust, draw a small ball with the note on it and one long curved arrow from the left bank to the right bank. Write: put the message INTO something that can cross.
03

Part 2. The end of the axon, and a gap

65 sec

Now turn the river into our cell. This is the far end of our neuron's axon. I am calling it cell 1. Down here is the next cell, cell 2. Between them is a narrow gap of fluid. The two cells do not touch. I have drawn the gap much wider than it really is so that we have room to draw.

Remember how the action potential travels. It moves along a membrane, because it is made by doors in that membrane opening one after another. At the end of the axon, that membrane stops.

So what happens to the electrical message when it reaches the gap?

It stops. There is no membrane across the gap for it to travel along. That is our river. So the cell puts the message into something that can cross a gap of fluid, and that something is a chemical.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw one arrow down the axon to the end and write: electrical message arrives. Put a navy X in the gap and write: cannot cross. In rust, in the empty space at the right, write: send a chemical across.
04

Part 3. The chemical is ready and waiting

65 sec

The chemical has to be ready before the message gets there. The cell makes it ahead of time and packs it into little bags made of membrane. The bags are parked at the end of the axon. Look at these three: they sit right against the membrane that faces the gap.

Here is the problem. If these bags emptied at any moment, cell 2 could not trust what it received. So what should tell the bags to empty, and only then?

The arrival of the electrical message, and nothing else. So the end of the axon needs a part that can sense an electrical change. And you already own one: the voltage sensing door.

Now the names. The bag is a synaptic vesicle. The chemical is a neurotransmitter. And the end of the axon is the axon terminal.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, draw a line from one bag to the right margin and write: bag = synaptic vesicle. Under it write: chemical = neurotransmitter. In rust, write: must empty ONLY when a message arrives.
05

Part 4. Back to the nucleus: a voltage sensing door for calcium

75 sec

Back to the nucleus. Gene, protein, membrane. The nucleus of cell 1 is a long way back, in the cell body, so this protein is built there and carried down the axon. It is a voltage sensing door that lets only calcium through. Calcium is the gold diamond.

Three facts before my question. There is lots of calcium outside the cell and almost none inside. Calcium is positive. And the inside of the cell is negative. At rest this door is shut, so the calcium waits outside.

When the action potential arrives and opens this door, which way does calcium go, and how many reasons does it have?

In, and it has two reasons, just like sodium. Concentration pushes it in, because there is lots outside and almost none inside. And the negative inside pulls it in, because calcium is positive. Both reasons were there the whole time. The only thing missing was an open door.

Its name is the voltage gated calcium channel.

DoPoint to the inset at the right and say: gene, protein, membrane. Ask the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawAt each calcium door: in rust, a short arrow from outside up to the door, and write 1, lots outside, pushed IN. In navy, a short arrow from the door into the cell, and write 2, negative inside pulls it IN. Then write the name under the inset: voltage gated calcium channel.
06

Part 5. Calcium comes in and the bags empty

60 sec

Calcium coming in is the signal the bags have been waiting for. The bags parked against the membrane carry a sensor for calcium. When calcium reaches them, the membrane of the bag joins the membrane of the cell. They become one membrane.

When the bag's membrane joins the cell's membrane, where does the chemical end up?

Outside the cell, in the gap. This was covered in Week 2, and it has a name: exocytosis.

Here is a second question. What would happen if there were no calcium outside the cell at all?

The message still arrives. The door still opens, because it responds to voltage and not to calcium. But nothing comes in. So nothing is released, and cell 2 receives nothing. Calcium is the link between the electrical message and the chemical one.

DoAsk the first question, wait, open Show the answer, then ask the second question before you read its answer.
DrawIn rust, draw two calcium diamonds just inside each door, next to the parked bags. Redraw each parked bag as an open cup joined to the membrane, and draw its dots falling into the gap. Write: calcium in, bags join the membrane, chemical OUTSIDE. In navy write the name: exocytosis.
07

Part 6. The chemical crosses the gap

50 sec

Now the neurotransmitter is in the gap, and all of it is up here, next to cell 1. This is where my river comparison stops working. Nobody throws anything. Nothing carries the chemical and nothing aims it.

So use what you know. There is lots of it next to cell 1 and none next to cell 2. Which way does it spread, and which rule from the balloon tells you?

Toward cell 2. Lots here, fewer there, movement toward fewer. That is diffusion. Each molecule moves at random, and the real gap is so narrow that spreading across it takes almost no time.

And the gap gets its name now: the synaptic cleft.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, dot the neurotransmitter all the way across the gap and draw two arrows from cell 1 down to cell 2. Write: LOTS up here, none down there, it spreads. Nothing carries it. In navy write: the gap = synaptic cleft.
08

Part 7. Back to the nucleus of cell 2: a door with a key hole

80 sec

Now cell 2 has to be able to read the message. So back to the nucleus, and this time it is the nucleus of cell 2. Gene, protein, membrane. Cell 2 has built doors with a key hole and put them in the membrane that faces the cleft. You know this door from the first story. It stays shut until the right key sits in the key hole.

So what is the key?

The neurotransmitter. Key fits, door opens, ions move through. At this door mostly Na+ comes in, and you know why: two reasons. So the inside of cell 2 becomes a little less negative, right at this spot.

Is that the large, full size signal, or the small, local one?

The small, local one. More neurotransmitter opens more doors and gives a bigger change, and it fades with distance. You know its name: a graded potential. Whether it becomes an action potential is the next story. And this door with a key hole is called a receptor.

DoPoint to the inset: gene, protein, membrane, in cell 2 this time. Ask the two questions one at a time.
DrawIn rust, draw a dot in each key hole. In navy, open each gate and draw an arrow through each door into cell 2. Write: key fits, door opens, Na+ comes IN. Under it write: small, local change = graded potential. Write receptor under the inset.
09

Part 8. Why the message goes one way only

45 sec

Stand back and look at the whole thing at rest. Compare the two sides of the cleft.

Which side has the bags? Which side has the key holes? So which way can a message go?

Only cell 1 has bags parked at the cleft. Only cell 2 has the key holes facing them. So the message goes from cell 1 to cell 2, and it cannot go back.

Now the names. The whole connection is a synapse. Pre means before and post means after. So cell 1 is the presynaptic cell and cell 2 is the postsynaptic cell.

DoAsk the three short questions in a row and let them answer each one.
DrawIn rust, bracket the bags and write: only on this side. Bracket the key holes and write: only on this side. Draw one thick rust arrow from cell 1 down to cell 2. In navy, cross out Cell 1 and write presynaptic, cross out Cell 2 and write postsynaptic, and write synapse beside the arrow.
10

Part 9. The same key can open different doors

85 sec

A fire alarm rings in a building. The students walk out and the firefighters walk in. It is the same alarm. What happens depends on who hears it.

Here is one neurotransmitter, acetylcholine, arriving at two different cells. On the skeletal muscle cell, the receptor is the door itself, the one we just drew. On a pacemaker cell of the heart, the receptor is not a door. It is a solid protein with the same key hole. When the key fits, it passes the message on inside the cell, and a potassium door opens.

So Na+ comes into the muscle cell, and K+ leaves the heart cell. What does the same chemical do to each cell?

Muscle cell: positive comes in, the inside becomes less negative, the cell fires, the muscle contracts. Heart cell: K+ leaves, positive leaves, the inside becomes more negative, the pacemaker cell fires less often, and the heart slows down.

Same key, opposite results. Each cell went back to its own nucleus and built its own receptor. The receptor decides the response.

DoTell the fire alarm comparison first, then point to the left cell, then the right cell. Ask the question and open Show the answer.
DrawLeft cell: rust dot in the key hole, navy arrow in through the door, write Na+ IN, fires, CONTRACTS. Right cell: rust dot in the key hole, a gray or navy arrow inside the cell from the receptor to the potassium door, a rust arrow out through the potassium door, write K+ OUT, more negative, heart SLOWS. Across the top write: the receptor decides.
11

Part 10. Families of keys

85 sec

There are many neurotransmitters, and they come in families. Fill in the boxes with me. One: acetylcholine, a family of one. Two: the amines, such as dopamine, norepinephrine and serotonin. Three: the amino acids, glutamate, GABA and glycine. Four: the peptides, which are short chains of amino acids. Five: a gas, nitric oxide, which is made when it is needed and is not kept in bags.

Glutamate is the main go signal in the brain, and GABA is the main stop signal. From the last part, what makes one a go and the other a stop?

The receptors. Glutamate's receptors are doors for positive ions, so the cell moves toward firing. GABA's main receptor is a door for chloride, which is negative, and that holds the cell away from firing.

Two more names, and you have already seen both. When the receptor is the door itself, the response is fast and brief. That is an ionotropic receptor. When the receptor is not a door and passes the message on inside the cell, the response is slower and lasts longer. That is a metabotropic receptor.

DoFill in the five boxes first, then ask the question. Keep this part light. The full lecture has the detail.
DrawIn navy, write one family name in the top strip of each box and the members underneath. In rust, under the boxes, write: the receptor makes it a go or a stop.
12

Part 11. A doorbell with a stuck button

50 sec

A doorbell with a stuck button rings and rings. It cannot tell you whether one visitor came or ten.

Now look at our cleft. The neurotransmitter is still there. The keys are still in the key holes. The doors are still open.

What has to happen to the neurotransmitter in the cleft for the next message to mean anything?

It has to be cleared out of the cleft, and quickly. A key does not stay in its key hole for long. It comes out again, and if the cleft has been cleared, no new key takes its place. The doors shut, and cell 2 can receive the next message as a new message.

DoAsk the question, wait, then open Show the answer.
DrawIn navy, circle the three open doors and write: still open. In rust, write in the empty space at the right: has to be CLEARED before the next message.
13

Part 12. Three ways to clear the cleft

80 sec

There are three new things in this drawing: a gray shape fixed in the cleft, a box in the presynaptic membrane, and a glial cell with a box of its own. They are proteins, so back to the nucleus. Gene, protein, membrane. The box is a machine and not a door, like the pump.

Follow one molecule of neurotransmitter. What are the three things that can happen to it?

One. The gray shape is an enzyme, and it chops the neurotransmitter up. The pieces no longer fit the key hole. For acetylcholine that enzyme is acetylcholinesterase. Two. The machine, which is called a transporter, carries it back into the cell that released it, where it can be packed into bags again, or into the glial cell. That is reuptake. Three. It spreads out of the open edges of the cleft. That is diffusion again.

One note about the transporter. It is powered by the sodium difference across the membrane, and you know what maintains that difference: the pump.

DoPoint to the inset: gene, protein, membrane. Ask the question and let them find the three routes before you open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust: 1, an arrow from a dot into the mouth of the gray shape and two small pieces coming out, write enzyme. 2, an arrow up through the box into the presynaptic cell and an arrow through the box into the glial cell, write reuptake. 3, an arrow out of the left edge of the cleft, write diffusion. In navy, write transporter under the inset.

Part 13. Break one step

55 sec

Every step in this story causes the next one. So if I tell you which step has failed, you can predict what cell 2 receives: more signal or less.

A: the bags cannot fuse. B: the key holes are plugged. C: the clean-up enzyme is blocked.

A is less. No neurotransmitter reaches the cleft. B is less. The neurotransmitter is released, but it cannot open the doors. C is more, and it does not switch off, because the neurotransmitter stays in the cleft.

And more is not better. Cell 2 needs a signal that turns on and then turns off. The full lecture gives you a real toxin or drug for each of these.

DoTake A, B and C one at a time and have them call out more or less before you open Show the answer.
DrawIn rust, write LESS beside A, LESS beside B, and MORE, does not switch off beside C. At C, draw extra dots filling the cleft.
