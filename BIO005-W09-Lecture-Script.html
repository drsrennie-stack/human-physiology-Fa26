A. Smooth muscle: how it contracts and what controls itabout 12 min in

You should be able toSmooth muscle contraction mechanism. Trace smooth muscle contraction through calcium entry and release to calmodulin and myosin light chain kinase and explain why the mechanism is slower and more economical than in skeletal muscle. Smooth muscle types and regulation. Compare single unit and multi unit smooth muscle and list the stimuli that alter smooth muscle tone including stretch and local chemical signals.

Say firstLet me start with a question about muscle. Skeletal muscle needs a motor neuron and a troponin switch before it can contract. Smooth muscle needs neither, and that one difference explains most of how it behaves.

Label the drawing: Smooth muscle cell: the calcium switch

Context boxOne smooth muscle cell, cut open. The membrane is the outline, the small Ca²⁺ store (SR) sits inside, and the contractile proteins are at the lower right. There is no troponin and no sarcomere, so the switch is different: it is on myosin, and it runs through an enzyme. First we name the parts. Then we follow what happens, step by step.

Pin 1This is the voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel. The electrical door: an L-type channel that opens when the membrane depolarizes. Lets Ca²⁺ in from the extracellular fluid, the main Ca²⁺ source for smooth muscle. Why it matters: calcium channel blockers shut only this door, so they relax vessels and lower blood pressure without stopping tissues that run on the receptor route. What triggers it: Anything that moves the voltage: stretch opening stretch-sensitive cation channels, a pacemaker slow wave arriving through gap junctions, high extracellular K⁺, or a transmitter that opens a cation channel or closes a K⁺ channel.

Pop-up drawingClick the small drawing in this card and it opens full size. This is the electrical door. Nothing sits on this channel to open it; it opens when the voltage across the membrane changes. Three things change that voltage in smooth muscle: stretch, which opens stretch-sensitive cation channels; a slow wave from a pacemaker cell, arriving through gap junctions; and a transmitter that opens a cation channel or closes a potassium channel. Any of the three depolarizes the membrane, the L-type channel opens, and calcium comes in from the extracellular fluid. That is why calcium channel blockers work on blood vessels: they shut this one door, and the vessel relaxes. Keep this picture in mind for the next pin, which is the other door.

Pin 2This is the receptor-operated Ca²⁺ channel. The chemical door: Ca²⁺ entry and SR release started by a ligand on a G protein-coupled receptor, not by voltage. Lets Ca²⁺ rise with no action potential at all (pharmacomechanical coupling). Why it matters: which door a tissue uses is set by the receptors and channels it was built with: stretch and pacemakers use the voltage door, hormones the receptor door, and nerves usually both, since norepinephrine also closes K⁺ channels and depolarizes the cell. Relaxing signals are the real contrast: epinephrine on β₂ raises cAMP and switches MLCK off, and nitric oxide raises cGMP and lowers Ca²⁺. What triggers it: A ligand on a Gq-coupled receptor. Take norepinephrine on an α₁ receptor as the example: the receptor activates phospholipase C, IP₃ is made, IP₃ opens the Ca²⁺ channels on the SR, and receptor-operated channels in the membrane let more Ca²⁺ in. Acetylcholine on M₃, angiotensin II, oxytocin and endothelin use the same chain with their own receptor, so learn the chain once.

Pop-up drawingClick the small drawing in this card and it opens full size. This is the chemical door drawn out once. Trace it on the screen as you talk: norepinephrine lands on the α₁ receptor; the receptor turns on its G protein, Gq; Gq turns on phospholipase C; phospholipase C cuts a membrane lipid and releases IP₃; IP₃ floats to the SR and opens its Ca²⁺ channel, so the store empties into the cytoplasm; at the same time the receptor-operated channel in the membrane lets more Ca²⁺ in from outside. No action potential anywhere in that chain. Then point to the list in the corner: acetylcholine on M₃, angiotensin II on AT₁, oxytocin, endothelin. Tell them these are not four more pathways to learn. They are the same road with a different first step. Learn the road once, then only match the ligand to its receptor. The contrast worth holding onto is the relaxing side, which we meet on the mind map: β₂ and nitric oxide do not use this road at all; they raise cAMP or cGMP and switch the enzyme off.

Pin 3This is the sarcoplasmic reticulum (smooth muscle). A small internal Ca²⁺ store, much less developed than in skeletal muscle. Releases Ca²⁺ through IP₃ receptors and ryanodine receptors when signaled. Why it matters: smooth muscle leans on extracellular Ca²⁺ because this store is small.

Pin 4This is the calmodulin. The Ca²⁺-binding protein of smooth muscle; it takes the place troponin has in striated muscle. Binds four Ca²⁺, then activates myosin light chain kinase. Why it matters: regulation is on the thick filament (myosin), not on actin.

Pin 5This is the myosin light chain kinase (MLCK). The enzyme switched on by Ca²⁺-calmodulin. Puts a phosphate on the myosin light chain, which turns on myosin ATPase so crossbridges can cycle. Why it matters: this extra enzyme step is one reason smooth muscle contracts slowly.

Pin 6This is the phosphorylated myosin light chain. Myosin with a phosphate on its regulatory light chain. Only phosphorylated myosin can bind actin and cycle. Why it matters: dephosphorylated myosin can stay attached in the latch state: tension held with almost no ATP.

Pin 7This is the dense bodies. Protein anchors in the cytoplasm and on the membrane, the smooth muscle version of Z discs. Actin filaments attach here, so shortening pulls the whole cell in like a drawstring. Why it matters: no sarcomeres, no striations, and the cell can shorten far more than skeletal muscle.

Pin 8This is the myosin light chain phosphatase. The enzyme that removes the phosphate from the myosin light chain. Ends crossbridge cycling as Ca²⁺ falls and calmodulin releases MLCK. Why it matters: signals that change this enzyme's activity change tone without changing Ca²⁺ (Ca²⁺ sensitization).

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing. Two enzymes fight over one phosphate. MLCK puts it on when calcium and calmodulin are up; the phosphatase takes it off, and it is always there working. Tone is the balance between the two. Two consequences follow. First, some signals raise tension without raising calcium: they block the phosphatase through Rho kinase, so more myosin stays switched on at the same calcium. Second, the latch: if the phosphate comes off while the head is still bound to actin, the head stays bound and keeps holding, and it spends almost no ATP doing it. That is how a sphincter or an artery wall holds tone for hours.

How it happens: Smooth muscle cell: the calcium switch

Context boxThis is the same drawing, and now we follow the sequence. Each click lights up where the step happens and draws a gold line to it, and the close-up in the box shows that moment. Have them write the step name in the numbered box on the sheet and the detail under it.

Step 1Ca²⁺ rises, by one of two doors. Electrical door: the cell depolarizes (stretch, a pacemaker cell, or a neurotransmitter opening a cation channel) and voltage-gated L-type Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ enters from the ECF. Chemical door: a neurotransmitter or hormone binds a Gq-coupled receptor. With norepinephrine on α₁ as the example, the receptor activates phospholipase C, IP₃ is made, IP₃ opens Ca²⁺ channels on the SR, and receptor-operated channels in the membrane let more Ca²⁺ in, all with no action potential. Acetylcholine on M₃, angiotensin II, oxytocin and endothelin run the same chain through their own receptor.

Step 2Ca²⁺ binds calmodulin. Four Ca²⁺ per calmodulin, in the cytoplasm. This is the smooth muscle version of Ca²⁺ meeting troponin; there is no troponin here.

Step 3Ca²⁺-calmodulin activates myosin light chain kinase. MLCK is an enzyme that was sitting idle; Ca²⁺-calmodulin binds it and turns it on. Because this is an enzyme step, it takes time, which is one reason smooth muscle is slow to start.

Step 4MLCK phosphorylates the myosin light chain. MLCK moves a phosphate from ATP onto the regulatory light chain of myosin. That phosphate switches on myosin ATPase; unphosphorylated myosin cannot bind actin.

Step 5Crossbridges cycle and the cell shortens. Phosphorylated myosin binds actin and pulls it toward the dense bodies, so the whole cell draws in like a drawstring. Cycling is 10 to 100 times slower than skeletal muscle.

Step 6Ca²⁺ falls and myosin phosphatase turns it off. Ca²⁺ is pumped out of the cell and back into the SR; calmodulin lets go of MLCK. Myosin light chain phosphatase removes the phosphate, so cycling stops. If a crossbridge is still attached when the phosphate comes off it can stay attached (latch state), holding tension for almost no ATP. Signals that inhibit the phosphatase (through Rho kinase) raise tension without raising Ca²⁺.

EffectThe effect, what changes because of this: Slow, graded contraction that can be held for hours as tone. Two doors let Ca²⁺ in: the voltage door (stretch, pacemaker slow waves, high K⁺, a depolarizing transmitter) and the receptor door (a ligand on a G protein-coupled receptor, IP₃, no action potential needed); single-unit muscle leans on the first, multi-unit on the second, and most tissues have both. Tone changes whenever Ca²⁺ entry changes through either door or whenever the phosphatase is turned up or down. That is how norepinephrine constricts an arteriole (α₁, Ca²⁺ up), epinephrine relaxes a bronchiole (β₂, cAMP inhibits MLCK), nitric oxide relaxes a vessel (cGMP lowers Ca²⁺ and activates the phosphatase), and calcium channel blockers lower blood pressure. Give them a minute to write the effect box in their own words before you move on.

Fill in: Why smooth muscle is slower and cheaper to run

Line 1Regulation is on myosin (thick filament), through an enzyme step, not a troponin switch on actin.

Line 2Myosin ATPase is slow: 10 to 100 times slower crossbridge cycling than skeletal muscle.

Line 3The latch state crossbridges stay attached after dephosphorylation, holding tension with almost no ATP.

Line 4Ca²⁺ mostly comes from the ECF, so contraction can be graded by how much Ca²⁺ enters.

Mini mind map: What changes smooth muscle tone?

CenterCenter of the map: Smooth muscle tone. One branch per click; each branch has its own small drawing of how that one works. Have them draw the map on the sheet, with the branch name first and the explanation under the picture.

Branch 1Stretch. Myogenic response: stretch opens channels, the cell depolarizes and contracts. Keeps blood flow steady when pressure rises.

Branch 2Local chemicals. In arterioles: low O₂, high CO₂, H⁺, adenosine and nitric oxide relax it; histamine relaxes it too (H₁ on the endothelium releases nitric oxide, H₂ on the muscle), which is the hypotension of anaphylaxis; endothelin tightens it. The same histamine contracts airway and gut smooth muscle (H₁ on the muscle): bronchospasm in the same patient. Tissues set their own blood flow this way; the receptor and the tissue decide the direction.

Branch 3Autonomic neurons. Norepinephrine and acetylcholine released from varicosities; the receptor decides contract or relax. α₁ contracts, β₂ relaxes vascular smooth muscle.

Branch 4Hormones. Epinephrine, angiotensin II, vasopressin, oxytocin and others. Oxytocin on the uterus is a positive feedback example.

Branch 5Single-unit vs multi-unit. Single-unit: cells joined by gap junctions contract together, some are pacemakers (gut, uterus, small vessels). Multi-unit: each cell has its own nerve supply, fine control (iris, airways, large arteries). Which kind decides whether the tissue beats as one.

B. Cardiac muscle, and the three muscle types side by sideabout 34 min in

You should be able toCardiac muscle properties. Explain how intercalated discs and gap junctions allow the myocardium to act as a functional syncytium and state why cardiac muscle cannot be tetanized. Comparison of the three muscle types. Build a comparison of skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle by calcium source, regulatory protein, control, and speed of contraction.

Say firstNow the muscle we really care about today. Here are two cardiac cells joined end to end, and most of what makes the heart work as a pump comes down to the junction between them.

Label the drawing: Two cardiac cells and the intercalated disc

Context boxTwo cardiac muscle cells end to end. The stepped dark line between them is the intercalated disc, with its gap junction channels and desmosome anchors. Inside the left cell, a T-tubule drops in from the membrane and meets the sarcoplasmic reticulum. First we name the parts. Then we follow what happens, step by step.

Pin 1This is the intercalated disc. The stepped junction where one cardiac cell ends and the next begins. Holds the cells together and carries the electrical signal across. Why it matters: because of it the myocardium works as one unit, a functional syncytium.

Pin 2This is the gap junction. Channels (connexons) in the intercalated disc that join the cytoplasm of two cells. Let ions and the action potential pass straight to the next cell. Why it matters: this is why one SA node signal spreads to every atrial cell; the ventricles form a second syncytium.

Pin 3This is the desmosome. A strong protein anchor in the intercalated disc. Holds cells together through the pull of every beat. Why it matters: without it the cells would tear apart under cardiac force.

Pin 4This is the T-tubule (cardiac). An inward fold of the cell membrane, larger than in skeletal muscle. Carries the action potential deep into the cell to the L-type Ca²⁺ channels. Why it matters: this is where the electrical signal first lets Ca²⁺ in.

Pin 5This is the L-type Ca²⁺ channel. A voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel in the T-tubule membrane (the dihydropyridine receptor). Opens during the action potential plateau and lets a little Ca²⁺ in from the ECF. Why it matters: that trigger Ca²⁺ opens the ryanodine receptor: calcium-induced calcium release.

Pin 6This is the ryanodine receptor (cardiac SR). The Ca²⁺ release channel on the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Opens when trigger Ca²⁺ binds it, releasing the SR store, about 90% of the Ca²⁺ for contraction. Why it matters: cardiac EC coupling needs both ECF and SR Ca²⁺; skeletal muscle needs SR only.

Pin 7This is the mitochondrion (cardiac). Up to a third of the cardiac cell's volume is mitochondria. Makes ATP aerobically, all day, from fatty acids, glucose and lactate. Why it matters: the heart cannot run an oxygen debt; cut its oxygen and the muscle dies (infarction).

Pin 8This is the branching, single nucleus. Cardiac cells are short, branched and usually have one central nucleus. The branches let each cell touch several neighbors. Why it matters: this is how you tell cardiac muscle from skeletal (long cells with many nuclei at the edge) and from smooth (spindle-shaped cells with one nucleus and no striations).

How it happens: Two cardiac cells and the intercalated disc

Context boxThis is the same drawing, and now we follow the sequence. Each click lights up where the step happens and draws a gold line to it, and the close-up in the box shows that moment. Have them write the step name in the numbered box on the sheet and the detail under it.

Step 1An action potential arrives from the neighbor. Positive current flows through the gap junction channels (connexons) in the intercalated disc and depolarizes this cell to threshold. No nerve and no neurotransmitter are involved between cardiac cells.

Step 2The signal runs down the T-tubule. Deep into the cell, to the L-type Ca²⁺ channels.

Step 3Trigger Ca²⁺ enters. The L-type channel opens during the plateau and a little Ca²⁺ comes in from the ECF.

Step 4The SR releases the rest: calcium-induced calcium release. Trigger Ca²⁺ opens the ryanodine receptors and the store empties into the cytoplasm.

Step 5Both cells contract together, held by the desmosomes. Ca²⁺ binds troponin C, tropomyosin moves off the myosin-binding sites on actin, crossbridges cycle; force is graded by how much Ca²⁺ was released. The desmosomes keep the cells from tearing apart under that pull.

Step 6Ca²⁺ is pumped away and the cells relax. SERCA refills the SR and the Na⁺-Ca²⁺ exchanger pushes Ca²⁺ out; mitochondria pay for all of it.

EffectThe effect, what changes because of this: The whole chamber contracts as one unit, with force graded by how much Ca²⁺ was released. Lose the gap junctions and the heart cannot act as one; lose the oxygen supply and the mitochondria stop and the cells die, which is a heart attack. Give them a minute to write the effect box in their own words before you move on.

C. The adrenal medulla: a sympathetic ganglion that secretes into bloodabout 62 min in

You should be able toAdrenal medulla. Explain why the adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion and compare the duration of its circulating catecholamine effect with direct sympathetic innervation.

Say firstThis section is short, because it finishes something from last week: why the adrenal medulla counts as part of the sympathetic system, and why its signal lasts so much longer than a nerve signal.

Steps in order: From the spinal cord to every tissue

Step 1Sympathetic preganglionic neuron fires. Leaves the thoracic cord and releases acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors, like any sympathetic preganglionic neuron.

Step 2Chromaffin cells respond. These are modified postganglionic neurons with no axons; that is why the medulla counts as a ganglion.

Step 3Catecholamines enter the blood. About 80% epinephrine, 20% norepinephrine, released as hormones.

Step 4Hormone reaches every tissue with adrenergic receptors. Including cells with no sympathetic nerve at all, such as skeletal muscle arterioles with β₂ receptors and liver cells.

Step 5Effect lasts minutes, not milliseconds. Cleared slowly by the liver; a sympathetic nerve signal ends in milliseconds by reuptake at the varicosity.

Fill in: Nerve versus hormone

Line 1Direct sympathetic nerve: speed and reach milliseconds, one target tissue, ended by reuptake.

Line 2Adrenal medulla: speed and reach seconds to start, whole body, lasts minutes.

Line 3Why both? the nerve gives fast local control; the hormone gives the whole-body fight or flight response and reaches tissues no nerve supplies.

D. The pacemaker and the cardiac action potentialabout 104 min in

You should be able toPacemaker potential. Explain the unstable pacemaker potential of the sinoatrial node by the funny current, calcium entry, and potassium exit and state what sets intrinsic heart rate. Contractile cell action potential. Diagram the ventricular action potential and identify the ion movement responsible for the rapid upstroke, the plateau, and repolarization. Cardiac refractory period. Say firstWhy does the heart beat on its own, with no nerve telling it to? Because the SA node cell never rests. Let me show you its membrane potential over two beats.

Label the drawing: SA node cell: pacemaker potential

Context boxMembrane potential of one SA node cell over two heartbeats. Notice there is no flat resting line: as soon as one beat ends the cell starts drifting toward the next. First we name the parts. Then we follow what happens, step by step.

Pin 1This is the pacemaker potential (slow depolarization). The slow upward drift from about −60 mV to threshold between beats. Makes the SA node fire on its own, with no nerve input. Why it matters: its slope sets heart rate: steeper slope, faster heart.

Pin 2This is the funny current, I_f. A current through HCN channels that open when the cell hyperpolarizes (the opposite of most channels). Lets Na⁺ in, starting the pacemaker potential. Why it matters: sympathetic cAMP opens more of these channels; the drug ivabradine blocks them to slow the heart.

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing. The HCN channel is called funny because it does the opposite of every other voltage-gated channel: it opens when the cell is hyperpolarized, around minus sixty millivolts. It lets mostly sodium in, so the cell drifts up toward threshold. cAMP binds the channel directly and opens it more. Sympathetic input raises cAMP, the slope gets steeper, and the next beat comes sooner; the vagus lowers cAMP, the slope flattens, and the next beat comes later. The slope of that drift is the heart rate.

Pin 3This is the T-type Ca²⁺ channels (SA node). Transient Ca²⁺ channels that open as the cell nears threshold. Add Ca²⁺ entry that finishes the climb to threshold. Why it matters: pacemaker depolarization is driven by Na⁺ and Ca²⁺, not by Na⁺ alone.

Pin 4This is the threshold in the SA node. About −40 mV in a pacemaker cell. Reaching this voltage opens the L-type Ca²⁺ channels. Why it matters: the SA node reaches threshold first (about 70 times a minute with vagal tone), so it sets the rhythm.

Pin 5This is the upstroke of the SA node action potential. The rapid rise to about 0 mV. The rise is carried by Ca²⁺ entering through L-type channels, not by Na⁺. Why it matters: pacemaker cells have no fast Na⁺ channels, so the upstroke is slower than in a ventricular cell.

Pin 6This is the repolarization of the SA node cell. The fall back toward −60 mV. K⁺ leaves through voltage-gated K⁺ channels as the Ca²⁺ channels close. Why it matters: as soon as the K⁺ channels close, I_f starts the next pacemaker potential.

Pin 7This is the minimum potential (about −60 mV). The lowest point of the cycle; there is no true resting potential. Hyperpolarization here is what opens the HCN channels. Why it matters: parasympathetic K⁺ channels push this lower, so the climb to threshold takes longer: slower heart.

How it happens: SA node cell: pacemaker potential

Context boxThis is the same drawing, and now we follow the sequence. Each click lights up where the step happens and draws a gold line to it, and the close-up in the box shows that moment. Have them write the step name in the numbered box on the sheet and the detail under it.

Step 1At −60 mV the funny channels open. HCN channels are unusual: they open when the membrane is hyperpolarized, not depolarized, and cAMP makes them open more. They pass mostly Na⁺ in (a little K⁺ out), so the cell drifts upward: the funny current, I_f.

Step 2T-type Ca²⁺ channels add to the climb. As the cell nears threshold the small, transient (T-type) voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open briefly and Ca²⁺ entry finishes the pacemaker potential.

Step 3Threshold, about −40 mV. The long-lasting (L-type) voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; these are the same channel type a calcium channel blocker acts on.

Step 4Upstroke: Ca²⁺ in. Depolarization to about 0 mV carried by Ca²⁺, not Na⁺; there are no fast Na⁺ channels here.

Step 5Voltage-gated K⁺ channels open: repolarization. The L-type Ca²⁺ channels inactivate, delayed-rectifier K⁺ channels open, K⁺ leaves down its gradient and the potential falls back to −60 mV.

Step 6Back at −60 mV, it starts again. K⁺ channels close, the funny channels reopen; this is the next beat starting.

EffectThe effect, what changes because of this: The slope of the pacemaker potential sets heart rate. Sympathetic cAMP steepens it (faster); parasympathetic ACh flattens it and drops the floor (slower). That is the whole mechanism of heart rate control, and the SA node wins because its slope is steepest. Give them a minute to write the effect box in their own words before you move on.

Label the drawing: Conduction system of the heart

Context boxThe heart from the front, so the patient's right is on your left. Pacemaker and conducting tissue are in color; the dashed line is the septum and the faint curve divides atria from ventricles. First we name the parts. Then we follow what happens, step by step.

Pin 1This is the sinoatrial (SA) node. A patch of pacemaker cells in the right atrial wall near the superior vena cava. Fires first, about 70 times a minute, and starts every normal beat. Why it matters: a rhythm set by the SA node is called sinus rhythm.

Pin 2This is the internodal pathways. Conducting fibers running through the atria from SA node to AV node. Spread the signal across both atria (P wave) and deliver it to the AV node. Pin 3This is the atrioventricular (AV) node. Pacemaker and conducting tissue at the floor of the right atrium. Slows the signal by about 0.1 s so the atria finish contracting before the ventricles start. Why it matters: the AV delay is the PR segment; its backup rate is 40 to 60 a minute.

Pop-up drawingClick the drawing. The AV node is the only electrical path through the fibrous skeleton, and it is slow on purpose. Its cells are small with few gap junctions, and their upstroke runs on slow calcium channels rather than fast sodium channels, so the signal crawls through and takes about a tenth of a second. That delay lets the atria finish contracting and emptying before the ventricles start. On the ECG the delay is the PR interval; the vagus lengthens it and sympathetic input shortens it.

Pin 4This is the bundle of His (AV bundle). The conducting bundle that pierces the fibrous skeleton into the septum. Carries the signal from the AV node to the bundle branches. Why it matters: it is the only electrical route through the fibrous skeleton, which otherwise insulates the atria from the ventricles.

Pin 5This is the bundle branches. Left and right branches running down the interventricular septum. Carry the signal fast to the apex. Why it matters: depolarization starts at the apex, so the ventricles squeeze from the bottom up toward the outflow valves.

Pin 6This is the purkinje fibers. Large, fast-conducting fibers that spread from the apex through the ventricular walls. Deliver the signal to the contractile cells almost at once (QRS lasts under 0.12 s). Why it matters: if every pacemaker above them fails, the Purkinje fibers can still drive the ventricles at 25 to 40 beats a minute.

Context boxLead II, one beat, on standard ECG paper. A small box is 0.04 s, a big box 0.20 s. The flat line is the baseline; every wave is read against it. First we name the parts. Then we follow what happens, step by step.

Steps in order: The cardiac cycle, phase by phase

Step 1Atrial systole. Atria contract and top up the ventricles to EDV; AV valves open, semilunar valves closed.

Step 2Isovolumic ventricular contraction. Ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure: AV valves close, S1. Not yet above aortic pressure, so all valves closed and volume fixed.

Line 7Change HR or SV CO rises or falls in proportion; raising both at once is how exercise gets a fourfold increase.

Mini mind map: What sets cardiac output?

CenterCenter of the map: Cardiac output = HR × SV. One branch per click; each branch has its own small drawing of how that one works. Have them draw the map on the sheet, with the branch name first and the explanation under the picture.

Branch 1Heart rate: sympathetic up. Norepinephrine and epinephrine on β₁ receptors in the SA node speed the pacemaker potential. cAMP opens more I_f channels and Ca²⁺ channels.

Branch 2Heart rate: parasympathetic down. Acetylcholine on M₂ receptors opens K⁺ channels and slows the drift to threshold. Resting vagal tone is why we sit at 70, not 100.

Branch 3Preload. End-diastolic volume, set by venous return; more stretch means more force (Frank-Starling). Raise venous return and SV rises on the next beat.

Branch 4Contractility. Force at a given EDV; raised by sympathetic input and epinephrine through β₁ and more Ca²⁺. Digoxin raises it too; β-blockers lower it.

Branch 5Afterload. The arterial pressure the ventricle must beat to open the semilunar valve; higher afterload means lower SV at the same force. Hypertension and aortic stenosis raise it.

Steps in order: Sympathetic input at the receptor level

Step 1Norepinephrine or epinephrine binds β₁. A G protein-coupled receptor on pacemaker and contractile cells.

Step 2Gₛ activates adenylyl cyclase. Cytoplasmic cAMP rises.

Step 3In the SA node, cAMP acts on HCN channels. More I_f, steeper pacemaker potential, faster rate (chronotropy).

Step 4In contractile cells, protein kinase A phosphorylates targets. L-type Ca²⁺ channels let in more Ca²⁺, more SR release, stronger contraction (inotropy).

Step 5PKA also phosphorylates phospholamban. SERCA pumps Ca²⁺ back faster, so relaxation is quicker and there is time to fill at high rates.

Step 6Parasympathetic does the reverse at the SA node. ACh on M₂ receptors: Gᵢ opens K⁺ channels (hyperpolarizes) and lowers cAMP; little direct effect on ventricular force.

Fill in: Frank-Starling in one line each

Line 1The law the more the ventricle is stretched by filling (EDV), the harder it contracts, so stroke volume matches venous return.

Line 2Why at longer sarcomere length more crossbridges can form and the filaments are more sensitive to Ca²⁺.

Line 3What raises venous return skeletal muscle pump, respiratory pump, sympathetic venoconstriction, more blood volume.

Line 4Why it matters the two sides of the heart stay matched beat to beat without any nerve telling them to.

Walk through why each option is right or wrong before you move on. The three class sets ask different questions; here are all three.

Set A. EDV 150 mL, ESV 90 mL, HR 80. Stroke volume, ejection fraction and cardiac output?

A. 60 mL, 40%, 4.8 L/min

B. 60 mL, 67%, 4.8 L/min

C. 90 mL, 60%, 7.2 L/min

D. 150 mL, 40%, 12 L/min

Key: A. SV = 150 − 90 = 60; EF = 60 ÷ 150 = 40%; CO = 80 × 60 = 4800 mL/min. An EF of 40% is low: this ventricle is not emptying well.

Not B: 67% would be 60 ÷ 90, the wrong denominator.

Not C: ESV is what stays behind, not what leaves.

Not D: EDV is not the stroke volume.

Set B. A patient's venous return rises suddenly when they lie down. Without any nerve input, what happens to the next stroke volume and why?

A. It falls, because afterload rises

B. It rises, because greater EDV stretches the fibers and they contract harder (Frank-Starling)

C. It is unchanged until the baroreflex acts

D. It rises, because heart rate increases

Key: B. Frank-Starling: more filling, more stretch, more force, more ejected; this matches output to return beat by beat with no nerve.

Not A: Lying down does not raise arterial pressure in that instant.

Not C: The length-tension response is intrinsic and immediate.

Not D: Rate is a separate variable and was not changed here.

Set C. A β₁ blocker is given. Predict the effect on heart rate and contractility, and name the step that is interrupted.

A. Both fall; norepinephrine can no longer raise cAMP in SA node and contractile cells

B. Rate falls, contractility rises; K⁺ channels close

C. Both rise; the drug mimics epinephrine

D. Only contractility falls; the SA node has no β₁ receptors

Key: A. β₁ receptors on both cell types work through Gₛ and cAMP; block them and the pacemaker potential is less steep and less Ca²⁺ enters contractile cells.

Not B: Contractility falls too, and K⁺ channels belong to the M₂ pathway.

Not C: A blocker prevents, it does not mimic.

Not D: The SA node is rich in β₁ receptors.
