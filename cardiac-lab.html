    One beat, labeled

    • P wave: the atria (upper chambers) depolarize, which means the electrical signal spreads across them. The atria contract right after.
    • QRS complex: the ventricles (lower chambers) depolarize. The ventricles contract right after, and you hear S1.
    • T wave: the ventricles repolarize, which means they reset electrically. The ventricles relax right after.
    • The atria repolarize at the same time as the QRS, so the QRS hides it and you cannot see it.
    • PR interval: from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS. Most of this time is the delay at the AV node.
    • QT interval: from the start of the QRS to the end of the T wave. It covers ventricular depolarization and repolarization together.

    Reading the paper

    • The paper moves at 25 mm/s. One small box is 0.04 s. One big box (five small boxes) is 0.20 s. Five big boxes make one second.
    • Heart rate when the rhythm is regular: divide 300 by the number of big boxes between two R waves, or divide 1500 by the number of small boxes.
    • Heart rate when the rhythm is irregular: count the QRS complexes in six seconds and multiply by ten. Every strip below is six seconds long.
    • PR interval: count the small boxes from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS, then multiply by 0.04. A normal PR interval is 3 to 5 small boxes.
    • Ask these questions in order: Is it regular? What is the rate? Is there a P before every QRS, and a QRS after every P? Is the PR normal? Is the QRS narrow?

    Three ways to find heart rate

    • 1. The 300 methodCount the big boxes between two R waves in a row. Rate = 300 ÷ big boxes. Example: 4 big boxes is 75 beats/min. Quick, but you have to estimate when an R wave lands between lines.
    • 2. The 1500 methodCount the small boxes between two R waves in a row. Rate = 1500 ÷ small boxes. Example: 20 small boxes is 75 beats/min. The most exact, because small boxes are finer.
    • 3. The six-second methodCount the QRS complexes in the six-second strip and multiply by 10. Example: 7 QRS complexes is 70 beats/min. The roughest number, but it is the one to use when the rhythm is irregular.
    • Compare themTry all three on Strip A. The 300 and 1500 methods agree (75), and the six-second method gives 70, because it counts only whole beats and can only land on tens. When the gaps between beats change, the 300 and 1500 methods give a different answer for every pair of beats, so use the six-second method.
    • Strip EThe P waves and QRS complexes run at their own rates. The ventricular rate comes from R to R. You can find the atrial rate the same way, from P to P.

    You should be able to167. Explain the origin of the first and second heart sounds and relate a murmur to valve stenosis or regurgitation by its timing.   173. Auscultate the heart sounds, locate the valve areas, and correlate the sounds with the pulse and with the cardiac cycle phases.

    Two sounds, four places to listen

    • S1, "lub": the mitral and tricuspid valves close as the ventricles start to contract, just after the QRS.
    • S2, "dub": the aortic and pulmonary valves close as the ventricles start to relax, at the end of the T wave.
    • How to tell them apart: at a normal heart rate, the gap from S1 to S2 (systole) is shorter than the gap from S2 to the next S1 (diastole).
    • S3: a soft sound early in diastole, from blood rushing in fast. It is normal in children, athletes and pregnancy. In an older adult it can mean a failing ventricle that is holding too much blood.
    • S4: a sound late in diastole, when the atrium pushes blood into a stiff ventricle, as in long-standing high blood pressure.
    • Murmur: the sound of turbulent (rough, swirling) blood flow. It happens when blood is forced through a valve that is too narrow (stenosis) or leaks back through a valve that should be shut (regurgitation).
    • The place where the murmur is loudest tells you which valve is involved. The timing of the murmur in the cardiac cycle tells you what is wrong with that valve.

    Where to listen for each valve

    • A, aortic: right 2nd intercostal space (the space between two ribs), beside the sternum.
    • P, pulmonary: left 2nd intercostal space, beside the sternum.
    • T, tricuspid: lower left edge of the sternum, 4th to 5th intercostal space.
    • M, mitral: apex of the heart, left 5th intercostal space, in line with the middle of the collarbone (midclavicular line).

    These are the places where each valve is easiest to hear, because the sound travels with the blood as it leaves the valve. They are not where the valves actually sit in the chest.

    When a heart cell dies, what leaks out, and when

    • Heart muscle cells hold proteins that should not be in the blood.
    • When the cell membrane breaks, those proteins leak out in a set order.
    • Each protein (biomarker) is useful for a different question, because each one rises at a different time and stays high for a different length of time.
    MarkerComes fromRisesPeaksBack to normalBest for
    MyoglobinAny striated muscle, skeletal or cardiac1 to 3 h6 to 12 habout 24 hThe first marker to rise. A normal value early on makes a heart attack (infarct) less likely. A raised value does not prove the heart is the source.
    CK-MBMostly cardiac muscle (MB isoform)3 to 6 h12 to 24 h48 to 72 hFinding a second heart attack in the days after the first, because CK-MB clears from the blood quickly.
    Troponin I or TCardiac muscle only (cardiac isoforms)3 to 6 h12 to 24 h7 to 14 daysThe standard test. It is the most specific marker for heart muscle, and it stays high long enough to detect damage in a patient who waited days to come in.
    BNP or NT-proBNPVentricular muscle under stretchRises when the ventricles are overloaded with volume or pressure, not when cells die. Falls as the overload is treated.Telling heart failure apart from other causes of shortness of breath. Under 100 pg/mL makes heart failure unlikely. Over 400 pg/mL makes it likely.

    These timings are the ranges most often quoted. Labs that use high-sensitivity troponin tests can detect a rise earlier, often within 1 to 3 hours. Always compare a result with the reference range printed on the lab report.

    Case 158 years old, crushing chest pain that started 90 minutes ago

    Labs: Troponin I: normal. Myoglobin: raised. CK-MB: normal. ECG shows ST changes.

    Name the marker that answers the question, say when it rises and falls, and say what tissue it comes from.

    No. Troponin takes about 3 to 6 hours to rise after injury, and this blood was drawn at 90 minutes. Myoglobin is up because it leaks out first (1 to 3 hours), but it does not come only from the heart. The ECG already shows injury. Repeat the troponin at 3 and 6 hours. A rising value confirms the heart attack (infarct). Treat now based on the ECG, and do not wait for the troponin.
    Case 263 years old, three days after a confirmed heart attack, new chest pain this morning

    Labs: Troponin T: still raised, about the same as yesterday. CK-MB: had returned to normal yesterday, raised again today.

    Name the marker that answers the question, say when it rises and falls, and say what tissue it comes from.

    Troponin stays raised for 7 to 14 days, so on day 3 it cannot tell a second heart attack from the first one. CK-MB returns to normal in 48 to 72 hours. So a CK-MB that was normal yesterday and is raised today means new heart muscle death (necrosis). This is a second heart attack (reinfarction). Finding a reinfarction is the one job CK-MB still does better than troponin.
    Case 371 years old, two weeks of worsening shortness of breath, swollen ankles, cannot lie flat

    Labs: Troponin I: normal. BNP: 1,150 pg/mL (reference under 100). Chest X-ray shows fluid in the lungs.

    Name the marker that answers the question, say when it rises and falls, and say what tissue it comes from.

    Ventricular muscle releases BNP when it is stretched by too much volume or pressure. A high BNP means the ventricles are overloaded, which points to heart failure. A BNP under 100 makes heart failure unlikely, and over 400 makes it likely. A normal troponin means the heart muscle is not dying right now. So this heart is failing as a pump, but it is not having a heart attack. BNP also shows how treatment is going, because it falls as the overload is relieved.
    Case 424 years old, finished a marathon six hours ago, aching all over, dark urine

    Labs: Myoglobin: very high. CK (total): very high. Troponin I: normal. Creatinine rising.

    Name the marker that answers the question, say when it rises and falls, and say what tissue it comes from.

    The injured tissue is skeletal muscle, not heart. Myoglobin and total CK come from any striated muscle. Troponin I is the heart form of the protein (cardiac isoform), and it is normal, so the heart is not damaged. This condition is rhabdomyolysis (breakdown of skeletal muscle). When the kidneys filter large amounts of myoglobin, it damages and blocks the renal tubules, so creatinine rises. The dark urine is caused by the myoglobin. Treatment is aggressive fluids (giving large amounts of fluid).
