The problem for today

Where this sits

The first half of this week gave you the cell as a structure: the membrane, the organelles, the four tissue types, and the junctions that hold a sheet of cells together. Nothing in it moved.

This half is about movement. A cell is a bag of concentrated solution sitting in a different solution, and it stays alive by controlling what crosses the boundary between them. Every mechanism below answers the same two questions: which way is this substance going relative to its own gradient, and is the cell paying for the trip?

Hold those two questions. If you can answer them for any substance at any membrane, you can work out the rest, and most of what looks like memorisation in this half turns out to be those two answers in different clothes.

The two questions, and the map they make

The frame for everything below

Put the two questions on two axes and you get every transport mechanism in physiology.

Down its gradient, no energy spent. This is passive transport. Simple diffusion if the substance crosses the lipid on its own, facilitated diffusion if it needs a protein to get through. Osmosis is this same thing for water.

Against its gradient, energy spent. This is active transport. Primary if the protein burns ATP itself, secondary if it spends a gradient that some other pump built with ATP earlier.

Too big for any protein. The cell wraps it in membrane instead, and that is vesicular transport: endocytosis coming in, exocytosis going out.

When you meet something new, put it on that map first. A student who knows the sodium potassium pump is primary active transport and that SGLT1 is secondary has understood the real difference between them, which is where the ATP was spent.

Simple diffusion, and what sets its speed

Competency 5

Diffusion is molecules moving from where there are more of them to where there are fewer, driven by nothing but their own random motion. Nobody pushes them. There are simply more ways to end up spread out than stacked in one corner, so over time they spread out.

The important consequence is that diffusion needs no energy and no protein. It also never stops at equilibrium: molecules keep moving in both directions, but the two rates become equal, so the net movement is zero. Equilibrium is a standoff, not a stillness.

Fick's law names the four things that set the rate. Rate goes up with the concentration gradient, up with the surface area available, down with the thickness of the membrane, and down with the distance the substance has to travel. Membrane permeability rolls into it too, and that depends on how lipid soluble the substance is, how big it is, and the temperature.

Every one of those four shows up again later as a clinical finding. Emphysema destroys alveolar walls and cuts surface area. Pulmonary edema puts fluid between the air and the blood and increases the distance. Fibrosis thickens the barrier. None of those are new physiology; they are Fick's law with one variable moved.

What crosses the lipid unaided, in order: small nonpolar gases like oxygen and carbon dioxide go easily, small uncharged polar molecules like water and urea go slowly, and large polar molecules and every ion do not go at all without help. That order is worth knowing cold, because it decides which mechanism a substance has to use.

Worked problem 1

Oxygen moves from an alveolus into a capillary. A patient develops pulmonary edema, which puts a layer of fluid between the air and the blood and doubles the distance oxygen has to travel. Nothing else changes. What happens to the rate of oxygen transfer, and why does this patient feel short of breath rather than simply tired?

Fick's law: rate rises with gradient and surface area, and falls with thickness and distance.

The work
  1. Name the variable that changed. Distance went up. Gradient, surface area and membrane thickness are all unchanged.
  2. Distance sits in the denominator, so doubling it roughly halves the rate of oxygen transfer across that patch of membrane.
  3. Less oxygen crosses per second, so arterial oxygen falls.
  4. Chemoreceptors respond to that fall by driving ventilation up, which is what the patient experiences as breathlessness.
  5. The picture to draw: two boxes with a barrier between them, an arrow for oxygen, and the barrier drawn twice, once thin and once with fluid added. Label the distance on both.
  6. The point to carry: nothing was wrong with the patient's oxygen, their hemoglobin or their gradient. One geometric variable moved and the whole system followed.

Osmosis: the same idea, seen from the water's side

Competencies 6 and 7

Osmosis is diffusion of water. Water moves down its own concentration gradient, which means it moves toward the side with more solute, because more solute means less water per liter.

Students lose marks here by tracking the solute. Track the water. If one side has more dissolved particles, that side has proportionally less water, and water moves in.

Osmotic pressure is the pressure you would have to apply to stop that movement. It is a way of measuring how badly water wants to move, not a push the solutes are exerting.

Osmolarity is a count: how many osmoles of solute sit in a liter of solution. Body fluids run at about 300 milliosmoles per liter. It counts every particle, and it tells you nothing on its own about what a solution will do to a cell.

Tonicity is the thing that actually matters to a cell, and it counts only the solutes that cannot cross the membrane. A solute that crosses simply follows the water and changes nothing in the end.

That distinction is the single most tested idea in this half of the week. A 300 mOsM solution of urea is isosmotic with the cell, and it will still burst a red blood cell, because urea crosses the membrane, enters the cell, and water follows it in. Isosmotic, hypotonic. The two words are not synonyms and one of them is about the membrane, not the solution.

Worked problem 2

A red blood cell is dropped into each of three solutions. A: 300 mOsM sodium chloride. B: 300 mOsM urea. C: 150 mOsM sodium chloride. Sodium chloride does not cross the red cell membrane; urea does. Predict what happens to the cell in each, and give both the osmolarity word and the tonicity word.

Cell interior is about 300 mOsM, and all of it is nonpenetrating.

The work
  1. Solution A. 300 mOsM, none of it penetrating. Nonpenetrating outside equals nonpenetrating inside, so water has no reason to move. Isosmotic and isotonic. No volume change.
  2. Solution B. 300 mOsM, but urea crosses. Count only the nonpenetrating solute outside: zero. The cell has 300 mOsM of nonpenetrating solute inside and there is none outside, so water moves in.
  3. Urea also moves in, down its own gradient, which raises the inside osmolarity further and pulls in still more water. The cell swells and lyses. Isosmotic but hypotonic.
  4. Solution C. 150 mOsM, nonpenetrating. Half the nonpenetrating solute outside that the cell has inside, so water moves in and the cell swells. Hyposmotic and hypotonic.
  5. The picture to draw: three cells in three beakers, an arrow for water in each, and under each one write the two words separately so you are forced to notice when they disagree.
  6. The point to carry: osmolarity is a property of a solution you can measure in a beaker. Tonicity only exists once you name a membrane, because it depends on what that membrane will let through.

Facilitated diffusion: a protein, but still no bill

Competency 8

Glucose is large and polar. It will not cross a lipid bilayer in any useful amount, and yet every cell in your body takes it up. It goes through a carrier protein, the GLUT family, down its own concentration gradient.

That is facilitated diffusion: protein mediated, still passive, still downhill, still no ATP. The protein is a door, not an engine. Take the gradient away and the movement stops, and if the gradient reverses the protein happily carries glucose the other way.

Two features come with using a protein, and both separate facilitated diffusion from simple diffusion on a graph.

Specificity. A carrier binds a particular shape. GLUT carries glucose and some related sugars and will not carry amino acids.

Saturation. There is a finite number of carriers. Raise the concentration far enough and every carrier is occupied, and the rate stops rising. Simple diffusion has no such ceiling; its graph is a straight line that keeps climbing.

Channels are the other protein route. A channel is an open pore rather than a binding site, so it moves ions far faster than a carrier moves anything, and it is gated: voltage gated, chemically gated, or mechanically gated. The gating is what Week 3 is built on, so notice it now.

Primary active transport: paying with ATP

Competency 9

Everything above moves things downhill. A cell that only did that would come to equilibrium with its surroundings, and a cell at equilibrium with its surroundings is dead. Life is the business of holding gradients that would rather collapse.

Primary active transport is a protein that hydrolyses ATP itself and uses that energy to move a substance against its gradient.

The one to know is the sodium potassium ATPase. Per ATP it moves three sodium ions out and two potassium ions in, both against their gradients. It is in the membrane of every cell you have, and a substantial share of your resting metabolic rate goes to running it.

Two consequences follow, and both matter later.

Because three positive charges leave and only two come in, the pump is electrogenic: it makes the inside of the cell slightly more negative. That is a small direct contribution to the resting membrane potential you meet in Week 3.

Because it holds sodium low inside the cell, it creates a steep inward sodium gradient. That gradient is stored energy, and the whole next section is about cells spending it.

Other primary pumps worth naming: the calcium ATPase that keeps intracellular calcium about ten thousand times lower than outside, and the proton pump in the stomach that acidifies gastric juice.

Secondary active transport: spending somebody else's gradient

Competency 10

A secondary active transporter moves one substance down its gradient and uses the energy released to drag a second substance up its own. No ATP touches this protein. The ATP was spent earlier, by the sodium potassium pump, building the gradient this transporter now spends.

That is why it is called secondary. It is one step removed from the ATP, and if you poison the sodium potassium pump, every secondary transporter in the cell fails shortly afterwards, because the gradient they all live on runs down.

Two shapes, and the names are literal.

Symport, both substances moving in the same direction. SGLT1 in the intestine and the kidney brings sodium in down its gradient and drags glucose in with it, against the glucose gradient.

Antiport, the two moving in opposite directions. The sodium calcium exchanger lets sodium in and throws calcium out.

Digoxin is the clinical case that ties the two sections together. It blocks the sodium potassium pump, so intracellular sodium rises, so the sodium gradient the sodium calcium exchanger depends on gets weaker, so less calcium leaves the cell, so intracellular calcium rises and the heart contracts more forcefully. Four steps, no new physiology, and a drug that has been in use for two hundred years.

Worked problem 3

A patient with cholera has watery diarrhea and is losing salt and water fast. Intravenous fluid is not available. Oral rehydration solution, which is water with salt and glucose in it, saves the patient's life. Plain salt water does not work nearly as well. Explain why the glucose matters, using the transport proteins involved.

Cholera toxin drives chloride and water secretion into the gut lumen. It does not damage SGLT1 on the apical membrane of the intestinal cell.

The work
  1. Name the protein the glucose is for. SGLT1 on the apical membrane is a symport: sodium in, glucose in, the sodium moving down its gradient and paying for the glucose.
  2. For SGLT1 to run, there has to be sodium in the lumen and a sodium gradient across the apical membrane. The sodium potassium pump on the basolateral membrane keeps intracellular sodium low, so the gradient is there.
  3. With glucose present, every glucose molecule absorbed brings a sodium ion in with it. Salt alone gives no such coupling and is absorbed far less efficiently.
  4. Sodium accumulating inside the cell is pumped out across the basolateral membrane into the interstitial fluid, which raises the osmolarity there.
  5. Water follows that solute by osmosis, out of the lumen and into the body. The water is never actively transported; it follows the sodium the whole way.
  6. The picture to draw: one intestinal cell with an apical and a basolateral membrane, SGLT1 on the apical side, the sodium potassium pump on the basolateral side, and a dotted arrow for water following the sodium.
  7. The point to carry: the treatment works because cholera toxin breaks one pathway and leaves another intact. Knowing which proteins sit on which membrane is what lets you see that.

Transport maximum, and why glucose appears in urine

Competency 11

Any transport that uses a protein can saturate, because there is a finite number of proteins. The rate at which every carrier is working flat out is the transport maximum, the Tm.

Below the Tm, raising the concentration raises the rate. At the Tm, raising the concentration does nothing at all, and whatever cannot be carried goes somewhere else.

The kidney is the clean example. Filtered glucose is normally reabsorbed completely, so there is no glucose in urine. Raise plasma glucose far enough and the carriers saturate, and glucose starts appearing in the urine. The plasma concentration at which that begins is the renal threshold, around 180 to 200 mg/dL in most people, and the Tm itself is around 375 mg/min.

This is why uncontrolled diabetes produces sugar in the urine, and why it also produces thirst and high urine volume: glucose left in the tubule holds water there by osmosis, so more water is lost. Saturation of one carrier explains three symptoms.

Simple diffusion never saturates. If you are given a graph that flattens out, a protein is involved somewhere.

Vesicular transport: too big for any protein

Competency 12

Proteins, bacteria and droplets of extracellular fluid are far too large for any channel or carrier. The cell moves them by wrapping them in a piece of its own membrane.

Phagocytosis, cell eating. The cell sends out actin driven pseudopods that surround a large particle, a bacterium or a dead cell, and pinch off a phagosome. The phagosome fuses with a lysosome and the contents are digested. This is not a general cell ability: it belongs to specialized cells, chiefly macrophages and neutrophils, and it is the front line of your immune defense.

Pinocytosis, cell drinking. The membrane dimples inward and pinches off a small vesicle of extracellular fluid with whatever happens to be dissolved in it. It is nonselective and it goes on continuously in most cells.

Receptor mediated endocytosis, the selective one. Receptors on the surface bind a specific ligand, the loaded receptors gather in a region coated on its inner face with clathrin, and that coated pit invaginates and pinches off. This is how cells take up cholesterol carried as LDL, and how they take up iron carried on transferrin. Familial hypercholesterolaemia is a defect in the LDL receptor: the cholesterol is in the blood, the cell cannot bring it in, and plasma cholesterol runs very high from childhood.

Exocytosis, the way out. A vesicle moves to the plasma membrane, fuses with it, and empties its contents outside. It does two jobs at once: it secretes the contents, and it adds the vesicle membrane, with whatever proteins were in it, to the plasma membrane. Regulated exocytosis, the kind that releases neurotransmitter or insulin, is triggered by a rise in intracellular calcium, which is worth remembering when you meet synapses in Week 3.

Transcytosis is endocytosis on one face and exocytosis on the other, carrying something straight across a cell. Capillary endothelium moves large proteins this way.

Worked problem 4

A child is found to have a plasma cholesterol several times the normal value from a young age, and the family history shows the same pattern. Investigation shows the LDL receptor is defective. Explain why plasma cholesterol is high, and say which step of the transport pathway has failed.

LDL carries cholesterol in the blood. Cells take it up by receptor mediated endocytosis.

The work
  1. Name the mechanism. Cholesterol arrives as LDL, which is far too large to cross the membrane by any protein route, so it is taken up by receptor mediated endocytosis.
  2. Name the step. The LDL receptor binds LDL at the cell surface and the loaded receptors gather in a clathrin coated pit, which invaginates and pinches off.
  3. With a defective receptor, LDL is never bound, so no coated pit forms and no vesicle is taken in. The uptake step fails at the very beginning.
  4. The cholesterol is not missing from the body. It is in the plasma and cannot get out of it, so plasma concentration rises and stays high.
  5. Cells sense they are short of cholesterol and make more of their own, which makes the plasma level worse rather than better.
  6. The picture to draw: a cell surface with receptors, LDL particles outside, a coated pit forming in the normal case, and the same drawing with the receptor crossed out and LDL piling up outside.
  7. The point to carry: a transport defect does not always mean too little of a substance somewhere. Here there is far too much of it, in the wrong compartment, because the door into the right one is broken.

Transepithelial transport: two membranes, two sets of proteins

Competency 13

Everything so far moved something across one membrane. Absorbing a nutrient from the gut, or reabsorbing it in the kidney, means moving it across a whole sheet of cells, in one side and out the other.

That only works because an epithelial cell is polarized: the membrane facing the lumen, the apical membrane, carries different proteins from the membrane facing the blood, the basolateral membrane. The tight junctions from the first half of this week are what keep the two sets from mixing, which is where the anatomy and the physiology of this week meet.

Glucose absorption in the small intestine is the pathway to know, and it uses three proteins on two membranes.

On the apical membrane, SGLT1 brings glucose in against its gradient, powered by sodium coming down its gradient. Secondary active transport.

On the basolateral membrane, GLUT2 lets glucose leave down its gradient into the interstitial fluid. Facilitated diffusion.

Also on the basolateral membrane, the sodium potassium pump throws sodium out, keeping intracellular sodium low so the apical gradient never runs down. Primary active transport.

Three mechanisms, one molecule, one trip. That is the pathway to be able to draw from blank paper, and the question to ask yourself at every step is still the same two: which way relative to the gradient, and who is paying.

There is also a route between the cells rather than through them, the paracellular route, and how leaky it is depends on the tight junctions. A leaky epithelium like the proximal tubule lets a good deal through between cells; a tight one like the distal nephron or the bladder lets almost nothing.

Worked problem 5

In an experiment, a drug is applied that blocks only the basolateral sodium potassium pump in an intestinal epithelial cell. Glucose absorption across the epithelium falls to almost nothing within minutes, even though the drug never touched SGLT1. Explain the sequence.

SGLT1 on the apical membrane is a sodium glucose symport. GLUT2 on the basolateral membrane is facilitated diffusion.

The work
  1. Name what the pump was doing. It threw sodium out of the cell across the basolateral membrane, holding intracellular sodium low.
  2. Block it, and sodium stops leaving. Intracellular sodium begins to rise.
  3. SGLT1 runs on the sodium gradient across the apical membrane. As intracellular sodium rises, that gradient shrinks.
  4. With a small gradient there is little energy to spend, so SGLT1 can no longer drag glucose in against the glucose gradient. Apical uptake falls.
  5. Nothing is wrong with SGLT1 itself. It is a secondary active transporter and its fuel supply was cut off two steps upstream.
  6. The picture to draw: the same polarized cell as before, with the pump crossed out and a rising sodium level written inside the cell, and an arrow through SGLT1 getting thinner.
  7. The point to carry: this is what secondary means. Every secondary transporter in the body is one pump failure away from stopping.
