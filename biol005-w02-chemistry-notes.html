The problem this lecture solves

Slides 1 to 2 of the deck

Last week you drew a control loop, and every box in it was a protein doing a job. The sensor was a protein that changes shape when something binds it, the signal was a molecule that fits one receptor and nothing else, and the effector was a protein that turns binding into work. This week is about those proteins, and about where the cell gets the energy to run them.

A patient arrives with these numbers: pH 7.18 against a normal 7.35 to 7.45, bicarbonate 12 mmol/L against 22 to 26, lactate 8.0 mmol/L against 0.5 to 2.0, and a blood pressure of 78 over 40. Three things are wrong at once. The lactate is high, the pH is low, and the bicarbonate is low.

Before reading further, put those three in causal order in your head. Which came first, and which two followed from it? The answer is built one step at a time through this lecture and assembled at the end.

Water, solubility and solutions

Slides 4 to 8 of the deck

Water is a polar molecule. Oxygen pulls the shared electrons toward itself, so the oxygen end carries a slight negative charge and the two hydrogen ends carry a slight positive charge. Those partial charges let water molecules cling to each other through hydrogen bonds, and cling to anything else that carries a charge.

Dissolving is exactly that clinging. Water surrounds a charged or polar particle with a shell of its own molecules and carries it away from its neighbours. A molecule with no partial charge anywhere on it gives water nothing to hold, so water pushes it aside and it stays clumped together with its own kind. That is why oil beads up.

Three words do most of the work here. Hydrophilic molecules are polar or charged and dissolve easily. Hydrophobic molecules are nonpolar, mostly carbon and hydrogen, and do not. Amphipathic molecules carry both in one molecule, a charged head and oily tails, and phospholipids being amphipathic is the entire reason a cell membrane exists.

Polar molecule
A molecule with an uneven spread of charge, so one region is slightly negative and another slightly positive. Water is the one that matters.
Hydrogen bond
A weak attraction between a slightly positive hydrogen and a slightly negative oxygen, nitrogen or fluorine on another molecule. Feeble alone, powerful in bulk.
Hydrophilic
Dissolves readily in water. Polar or charged.
Hydrophobic
Does not dissolve in water. Nonpolar.
Amphipathic
One polar region and one nonpolar region in the same molecule. Phospholipids.
Hydration shell
The layer of water molecules that forms around a dissolved ion or polar molecule. This is what dissolving actually is.
Solute and solvent
The solute is what dissolves; the solvent does the dissolving. In the body the solvent is essentially always water.
Aqueous
Water based. Every fluid in your body.

Work it through 1

Sort six molecules by whether water can dissolve them.

The rule is the only thing you need: does the molecule carry charge or partial charge anywhere on it?

Working
  1. Glucose, covered in hydroxyl groups: dissolves. Every OH group is polar, so water grabs it from all sides.
  2. Cholesterol, four carbon rings: does not. Almost all carbon and hydrogen, so there is nothing for water to hold.
  3. Sodium ion: dissolves. It carries a full charge, which is the strongest thing water can grab.
  4. A triglyceride, three long fatty acid tails: does not. Long carbon chains with no charge anywhere, which is why fat floats on soup.
  5. Insulin, a small protein with charged R groups on its surface: dissolves. The charged groups face outward, so the whole protein dissolves in plasma.
  6. Oxygen gas: does not. Two identical atoms share electrons evenly, so there are no partial charges at all. This is the most useful one to get right, because it is exactly why you need hemoglobin to carry oxygen rather than just dissolving it in plasma.

Alongside solubility you need the units, because you will read all of them on a lab report. Molarity is moles of solute per liter of solution, written M, and body fluids are dilute so values are usually millimoles per liter, mM. Equivalents are moles multiplied by the charge on the ion, reported as mEq/L, and they exist because body fluids have to stay electrically neutral so charge matters as much as particle count. A deciliter is 100 mL, so mg/dL is a mass per volume, and that is how glucose, calcium and cholesterol are usually reported in the United States.

You will not be asked to convert between them. You will be asked to read them, and to notice when a number and its unit do not match the physiology you expect.

Nursing

Whether a drug dissolves is why some of them need a certain fluid, a filter, or a line of their own.

Example: Oily preparations separate out or stick to tubing, so the route matters as much as the dose.

Radiologic technology

MRI is not really imaging tissue. It is imaging hydrogen, and nearly all the hydrogen in a person sits in water and fat.

Example: Two soft tissues look different because they hold different amounts of water and fat, which is why MRI separates soft tissues an X-ray cannot tell apart.

Medicine

Whether a molecule likes water or likes oil tells you where in the body it can reach.

Example: A fat soluble drug crosses into the brain readily and a water soluble one largely does not, which is why two similar drugs can differ completely in how sleepy they make someone.

Respiratory therapy

An inhaled drug has to survive as droplets the whole way down and still reach the small airways.

Example: Particle size and humidity change how much of a nebulized dose gets past the upper airway at all.

pH and buffers

Slides 9 to 12 of the deck

An acid releases hydrogen ions into solution and a base takes them out, either by binding them directly or by releasing hydroxide that combines with them to make water. pH is a way of writing the hydrogen ion concentration so the numbers stay manageable, and pure water sits at pH 7.

The scale is logarithmic, and that is the part people miss. Each step is ten times the hydrogen ion concentration of the step beside it, so going from pH 8 to pH 6 is not twice the acid, it is a hundred times the acid. Different compartments sit at very different values on purpose: the stomach runs near 1 to 3 because its enzyme needs that, and the inside of a cell sits near 7.0 to 7.2.

The body defends pH this hard because of a four-step chain that is worth learning as a unit. A free hydrogen ion is small and charged, so it can join in hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds anywhere it lands. Enough of them change which R groups on a protein carry a charge. Change the charges and you change which weak bonds hold the protein in its shape. Change the shape and you change the job.

A buffer is a pair of molecules that acts as a sponge for hydrogen ions: one half soaks them up when there are too many, the other releases them when there are too few. The one that matters most in blood is the bicarbonate system.

CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3 ⇌ H+ + HCO3−

Read it in both directions. The double arrows mean this reaction runs either way depending on what the body needs.

What it says: add acid and the reaction runs to the left, because the bicarbonate on the right mops up the extra hydrogen ions and turns them into carbon dioxide and water. The pH still falls, but far less than it would have.

What makes this buffer special is that both ends are adjustable, and by different organs on different timescales. The lungs control the carbon dioxide end and can change it within minutes. The kidneys control the bicarbonate end over hours to days. You build the fast lever properly in Week 13 and the slow one in Week 15.

Now look back at the patient. Their bicarbonate is 12 against a normal 22 to 26. That low number is not the problem. It is the evidence that the sponge has been used up.

Nursing

The first sign of an acid problem is often how the person is behaving, not a number on a report.

Example: New confusion or agitation can arrive before anyone has drawn a blood gas.

Radiologic technology

The body holds its pH in a narrow window, so anything injected has to be mixed to match it.

Example: Modern low osmolar contrast agents are tolerated better than the old ones because their chemistry sits closer to what blood already is.

Medicine

A pH change that looks small is not small, because every whole step on the scale is ten times more acid.

Example: A fall from 7.40 to 7.10 doubles the hydrogen ion concentration, which is enough to change protein shape throughout the body.

Respiratory therapy

Reading acid and base status is the central skill of the specialty.

Example: Ventilation is the fast lever on pH, so a change in minute ventilation moves the pH within minutes, not hours.

Protein structure and function

Slides 13 to 16 of the deck

Twenty amino acids build human proteins, and nine of them are essential, meaning the body cannot make them and you have to eat them. Every amino acid has the same three parts, an amino group, a carboxyl group and a hydrogen, all attached to one carbon, plus a fourth attachment called the R group. The R group is the only thing that differs between the twenty, and it is where the personality lives: some R groups carry a charge, some are polar, some are oily, and one contains sulfur.

A peptide bond joins the amino group of one amino acid to the carboxyl group of the next and releases a water molecule. It is covalent and strong, so a protein chain does not come apart on its own.

Proteins have four levels of structure. Primary structure is the order of the amino acids, set by the gene and held by peptide bonds. Secondary structure is local folding into alpha helices and beta sheets, held by hydrogen bonds along the backbone. Tertiary structure is the full three dimensional shape, held by weak bonds between R groups and sometimes by a disulfide bridge between two cysteines; fibrous proteins like collagen are insoluble and globular proteins dissolve. Quaternary structure is several folded chains held together by weak bonds, and hemoglobin is four of them.

Notice which bonds hold which level. Only the primary structure is held by strong covalent bonds. Levels two, three and four are held mostly by weak bonds, and that is deliberate, because it lets a protein flex when something binds to it. It also means heat and pH can undo the fold without touching the chain at all.

Denaturation is losing the shape while keeping the chain. The same amino acids in the same order, folded wrong, with no function. Heat does it by shaking the weak bonds apart; pH does it by changing which R groups carry a charge, so the ionic bonds that held the fold either break or form where they should not. A mild disturbance can be undone and the protein refolds, but a severe one usually cannot, which is why a cooked egg white never goes clear again.

Underneath every protein job is the same three steps. Something binds, the protein changes shape, and the shape change does the work. A ligand is anything that binds: a substrate, a hormone, a drug, an ion. Specificity means a protein binds one ligand or a small family of very similar ones, because the site and the ligand have to match in both shape and charge. Affinity is how tightly it holds on once bound. And binding is reversible, which is the only reason a signal can ever be switched off.

R group
The variable part of an amino acid. It decides how the chain folds and therefore what the protein does.
Peptide bond
The covalent bond joining amino acids, formed with the loss of a water molecule.
Denaturation
Loss of a protein's shape, and therefore its function, without breaking the peptide bonds.
Disulfide bond
A covalent sulfur to sulfur link between two cysteines that helps hold a fold together.
Ligand
Anything that binds to a protein.
Specificity
A protein binding one ligand or a small family of closely related ones.
Affinity
How tightly a protein holds its ligand once bound.
Induced fit
The site and the ligand adjusting to each other as they meet, which is how binding can switch a protein on.

Enzymes: rate, and everything that changes it

Slides 17 to 24 of the deck

An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a reaction without being used up by it, and one enzyme molecule can turn over thousands of substrate molecules a second. It works by holding the substrates in the right position and straining the bonds that need to break, which lowers the activation energy, the hill every reaction has to climb before it can happen.

It is worth being clear about what an enzyme does not do. It does not make a reaction release more energy, it does not make an impossible reaction possible, and it does not decide the direction. Without enzymes most reactions in your body would still happen, just far too slowly to keep you alive. Enzymes are about rate, and rate is what physiology is made of.

Vmax is the fastest the reaction can go with the enzyme that is present. At that point every binding site is occupied and the enzyme is saturated, and adding more substrate does nothing at all. Km is a measure of grip: it is the substrate concentration that fills half the sites, so a small Km means the enzyme grabs substrate even when there is very little around. Two enzymes doing the same reaction with different Km values behave completely differently at low substrate, which is how the body puts the right enzyme in the right organ.

Neither value is something you calculate in this course. What you are asked for is which way the curve moves.

Work it through 3

Why sugar appears in the urine when blood glucose is high.

Transporters saturate exactly the way enzymes do, so figure 3 applies to them too.

Working
  1. At a normal blood glucose of 5 mmol/L, the kidney's glucose transporters are operating on the steep, rising part of the curve. There is spare capacity, so every glucose molecule that arrives finds a free transporter and gets reclaimed.
  2. When blood glucose rises to 20 mmol/L, the transporters saturate. Every site is occupied at all times, so they are moving glucose as fast as they ever can.
  3. The extra glucose arriving has nowhere to bind. It stays in the filtrate and leaves in the urine. Nothing is broken; the system simply hit its ceiling.
  4. Would more time help? No. The transporters are already running at Vmax every second, so slowing the flow gives each molecule more chances but does not move the ceiling.
  5. Would more glucose help? No, and that is the whole point of the flat part of the curve.
  6. What would work is changing the protein side: fewer glucose molecules arriving, or more transporters in the membrane. The rule to reuse all term is that when a response stops rising even though the input keeps rising, look for a saturated protein.

There are three ways to switch an enzyme on. Cut it: proteolytic activation makes the protein in an inactive form and removes a piece to switch it on, which is how digestive enzymes and clotting factors work, and it is why a digestive enzyme does not digest the cell that made it. Give it a cofactor: the binding site does not work until an ion or small organic molecule sits in it, and many trace minerals and B vitamins are cofactors. Or bind a modulator at a second site: allosteric means other site, and an allosteric activator binds away from the working site, shifts the fold, and makes the site usable.

There are two ways to switch one off, and telling them apart is worth real points. A competitive inhibitor is shaped enough like the substrate to sit in the binding site itself, so the two compete for the same spot and flooding the system with substrate wins the site back. An allosteric inhibitor binds somewhere else and deforms the site, so more substrate does not help. Reversible inhibitors use weak bonds and let go; irreversible ones bind covalently and the protein is finished until the cell builds a new one.

Work it through 4

Two poisons, two antidotes. Which kind of inhibitor is each, and what does the antidote have to do?

Working
  1. Carbon monoxide is competitive. It occupies the exact site oxygen uses on hemoglobin, and holds it roughly 200 times more tightly, so the two are in direct competition for one spot.
  2. Because it is competitive, mass action can win it back. Flood the patient with 100 percent oxygen, and in severe cases oxygen at high pressure in a chamber, so oxygen molecules vastly outnumber the carbon monoxide at the site.
  3. That is the left panel of figure 5 in real life. Vmax was never lost; you just have to push much further right along the substrate axis to reach it.
  4. Cyanide is allosteric. It binds an enzyme in the electron transport chain away from the substrate site and deforms it, so no amount of extra substrate recovers the rate.
  5. The antidote therefore cannot work by competition. It works by removal instead, giving the body something that cyanide binds even more tightly than the enzyme does, which pulls it off.
  6. The transferable lesson: identify where the inhibitor binds and you have already narrowed down what the antidote has to do. Competitive, out-compete it. Allosteric, get rid of it.

One last lever, and it works on a different timescale from all the others. Up-regulation is the cell making more copies of a protein, or breaking down fewer; down-regulation is the reverse. Everything else in this section happens in milliseconds to seconds, but this takes hours to days, because it means transcribing a gene and building protein. So the body has a fast set of controls and a slow set working on the same enzymes. Someone who trains regularly builds more mitochondria and more of the enzymes inside them; nothing about the individual enzyme changed, there is just more of it.

Nursing

A fever is not just a number. Above about 40 °C (104 °F) you are on the falling side of the temperature curve for a lot of enzymes.

Example: That is part of why a very high fever causes confusion, and why cooling is treatment rather than comfort.

Radiologic technology

Enzyme levels in the blood are used as a map of which organ is damaged, because each tissue leaks its own set.

Example: That is the same reasoning behind reading a contrast study: inferring what is happening inside from what shows up outside.

Medicine

Body temperature and pH are held where the enzymes work, not the other way around.

Example: Therapeutic cooling after a cardiac arrest deliberately slows enzyme activity to lower the brain's demand for ATP.

Respiratory therapy

The pH an enzyme sees changes within minutes of a change in breathing.

Example: That is why correcting ventilation can change a patient's chemistry faster than any drug you could give.

ATP and energy coupling

Slides 26 to 27 of the deck

A reaction that releases energy is exergonic. One that needs energy put into it is endergonic, and on its own an endergonic reaction does not happen. Coupling is the trick the cell uses: run the two together so the energy released by one pays for the other. ATP is the intermediate that makes coupling possible, because breaking the bond to its last phosphate group releases energy the cell can immediately spend.

ATP pays for three kinds of work, and all three are happening in you right now. Chemical work is building molecules, such as making a protein from amino acids or storing glucose as glycogen. Transport work is moving things uphill, such as the sodium potassium pump pushing ions against their gradient. Mechanical work is moving parts, such as myosin pulling on actin, cilia beating, or a vesicle travelling. Stop making ATP and all three stop within minutes.

The single most useful thing to understand about ATP is that it is a rechargeable battery, not a fuel tank, and this is the part students most often have backwards. An adult holds only about 50 grams of ATP at any moment, and yet turns over something on the order of a whole body weight of it across a day. That only works because ATP is recycled continuously: spent to ADP, recharged back to ATP, thousands of times a day per molecule. So the pathways in the next section are not making ATP out of nothing. They are recharging ADP back to ATP, using energy pulled out of food.

ATP + H2O → ADP + Pᵢ + energy

Abbreviations: ATP adenosine triphosphate, ADP adenosine diphosphate, Pᵢ inorganic phosphate, H2O water.

What it says: this is the spending half. The energy released here pays for chemical, transport and mechanical work.

ADP + Pᵢ + energy → ATP + H2O

The same reaction run backwards. The energy now has to come from somewhere, and that somewhere is food.

What it says: this is the recharging half, and it is what the whole of the next section exists to do.

Where ATP comes from

Slides 28 to 32 of the deck

Stage 1, glycolysis. Splits one six-carbon glucose into two three-carbon pyruvate molecules, in the cytosol, without needing oxygen. It costs 2 ATP to get started and makes 4, so the net gain is 2 ATP, and the investment at the front is why a cell that is already out of ATP is in real trouble. It also makes 2 NADH, which are electron carriers worth far more later. Being the only stage that runs without oxygen is what makes glycolysis the emergency route, and it is why a mature red blood cell, which has no mitochondria at all, uses it for its entire life.

Glycolysis has a catch worth knowing. It needs NAD+ to accept electrons at one of its steps, and every time it runs it turns NAD+ into NADH, using up the supply. If the NADH is not emptied out again, glycolysis stops within seconds. With oxygen, the mitochondrion empties it. Without oxygen, the cell has to find another way, and that is what lactate is for.

Stage 2, pyruvate to acetyl CoA. Pyruvate crosses into the mitochondrial matrix and loses a carbon as carbon dioxide. It makes 2 NADH per glucose and no ATP at all. This is a one-way link reaction rather than a cycle, and once carbon has left as carbon dioxide you cannot rebuild glucose from what is left, which is why fat cannot be converted back into glucose in any useful amount.

Stage 3, the citric acid cycle. Also in the matrix, and it turns twice per glucose, once for each pyruvate. Per glucose it makes 2 ATP, 6 NADH, 2 FADH2 and 4 carbon dioxide. Look at what it is really producing: two ATP is almost nothing, so the cycle's actual job is to strip electrons off the carbons and load them onto carriers.

Stage 4, oxidative phosphorylation. On the inner mitochondrial membrane, and this is where almost all the ATP is actually made. NADH and FADH2 hand their electrons to a chain of proteins in the membrane. As the electrons pass down the chain, the proteins use the energy to pump hydrogen ions out of the matrix, building a gradient. Those hydrogen ions then flow back in through an enzyme called ATP synthase, and that flow is what spins it and makes ATP. At the end of the chain, oxygen accepts the spent electrons and combines with hydrogen to make water.

Why losing oxygen stops stages 2 and 3, which do not use oxygen directly

Oxygen's entire job is to sit at the end of the chain and accept electrons. With no oxygen there, the electrons have nowhere to go, so the chain fills up and stops. The chain is the only thing that empties NADH and FADH2. Once those carriers are all full, stages 2 and 3 have nowhere to put the electrons they strip off, so they stop as well. Only glycolysis, which can dump its electrons onto pyruvate instead, keeps running.

For this course, use 30 to 32 ATP per glucose and be able to say why the number is not exact. If you are holding an older textbook that says 36 or 38, you have not read it wrong; the estimate changed as the measurements improved. The old numbers assumed each NADH bought exactly 3 ATP and each FADH2 exactly 2, and the measured values turn out to be lower and not whole numbers, because the inner mitochondrial membrane leaks some protons. Being able to explain a disagreement between two sources is worth more here than memorizing either number.

Work it through 5

What the shortfall actually costs, when oxygen goes.

Working
  1. With oxygen, a cell gets about 32 ATP from each glucose. Without it, glycolysis alone gives 2.
  2. So to hold its ATP supply steady, the cell needs far more glucose, because each one is now worth about a sixteenth of what it was worth.
  3. About 16 times as much, since 32 divided by 2 is 16. That single ratio is the only number from this section worth carrying, and it is the whole reason oxygen matters so much.
  4. Now add the patient's real situation: the blood supply to that tissue is reduced, so less glucose is arriving, not more. Demand went up sixteenfold at exactly the moment supply went down.
  5. The cell cannot meet it, so ATP falls and the jobs ATP pays for start failing in order of how much they cost.
  6. Meanwhile every one of those glucose molecules is producing lactate, which is why the lactate climbed to 8.0. Say it as a principle: lactate is not measuring the lactate. It is measuring the gap between the ATP a tissue needs and the ATP oxygen is letting it make.

Nursing

Oxygen is a drug with a purpose, and the purpose is the last step of the electron transport chain.

Example: A patient can have a normal oxygen saturation on the monitor and still be starved at the tissue if blood pressure is too low to deliver it, which is why lactate is measured alongside saturation.

Radiologic technology

Nuclear medicine scans that measure metabolism are reading this pathway directly.

Example: A PET scan uses a glucose analogue, so tissue lighting up brightly is tissue consuming glucose fast, which is why tumours show up.

Medicine

Cyanide kills by blocking the very end of the chain while everything upstream is intact.

Example: The patient has plenty of oxygen in the blood and cannot use any of it, which is why their venous blood stays bright red.

Respiratory therapy

This is why a patient who cannot ventilate deteriorates in minutes rather than hours.

Example: There is no meaningful store of ATP to fall back on, so the deficit starts almost immediately.

The patient, start to finish

Slides 33 to 34 of the deck

The causal order, which is the answer to the question on slide 2. Oxygen delivery fell first, because blood pressure was too low to push blood through the tissue. The lactate rose second, because the cell fell back on glycolysis and had to convert pyruvate to lactate to keep it running. The bicarbonate fell third, because it was spent buffering that acid; the low bicarbonate is the receipt, not the cause. And the pH fell because the buffer ran out before the acid did.

Now notice the loop at the bottom of the figure. Falling pH slows every enzyme in the cell, including the enzymes of glycolysis, which is the one pathway still making any ATP at all. So the cell gets worse at the only thing keeping it alive. That is positive feedback, and positive feedback does not correct itself.

This is also why the treatment is not simply giving bicarbonate. Giving a buffer treats the number on the report. Restoring blood pressure and oxygen delivery treats the first box in the chain, and everything downstream follows from it.

Change one thing, twice

A different patient has normal blood pressure and normal lungs but has taken cyanide, so their tissue oxygen delivery is fine. Box 1 of the chain does not happen. Everything from box 2 onward happens exactly as drawn, because cyanide blocks the end of the electron transport chain, which is the same point of failure that missing oxygen would have caused. Same chain, different entry point, and it is why cyanide poisoning produces a severe lactic acidosis in a patient whose oxygen saturation reads normal.

Our original patient also develops a fever of 40 °C (104 °F). That makes the enzyme problem worse, through a second and independent route. The pH is already pulling folds apart by changing R group charges, and the fever is now pushing on the same weak bonds from the other direction, since 40 °C sits just past the peak on the temperature curve. Two different perturbations, one shared mechanism: both are attacking the weak bonds that hold protein shape.

Close the page and produce it

These are the six competencies this lecture covers, straight off the study guide, with the two prompts that go with each one. The seventh Week 2 competency, the enzyme assay, is measured in the lab rather than here. Pick one prompt per competency. Blank paper, nothing open.

Read the full instructions first, including how the first pass before the lecture differs from the second pass after it.

01

Water and solution properties

Explain how the polarity and hydrogen bonding of water determine solubility and identify whether a given solute is hydrophilic or hydrophobic.

Do one of the two. Head your paper with the number.

1-1

Draw a water molecule and label the partial charges on it. Beside it draw one thing that dissolves in body water and one thing that does not, and write one sentence under each saying why. Then write what the body has to do for the one that does not dissolve.

1-2

Draw a blood vessel in cross section. Show one substance travelling free in the plasma and one travelling bound to a carrier protein. Label both, and write what decides which way a substance has to travel.

02

pH and buffers

Define pH, state the normal pH range of arterial blood, and explain how a buffer pair resists a change in pH when acid or base is added.

Do one of the two. Head your paper with the number.

2-1

Write the bicarbonate buffer equation from memory. Draw an arrow showing which way it shifts when acid is added and mark what happens to bicarbonate. Then write what the lung does to this equation and what the kidney does, and how fast each one works.

2-2

Draw the pH scale from 6.8 to 7.8. Mark the normal arterial range, mark which end is acidemia and which is alkalemia, and mark the survival limits. Underneath, write the four-step chain from a fall in pH to a protein losing its job.

03

Protein structure and function

Relate the levels of protein structure to binding site shape and explain how denaturation by heat or pH change destroys function.

Do one of the two. Head your paper with the number.

3-1

Draw the four levels of protein structure as four small sketches in order and label each one. On the tertiary sketch mark where the binding site is, and write one sentence saying why the shape is the job rather than a consequence of the job.

3-2

Draw a folded protein with its binding site. Then draw it again after heat, and again after a pH change. Label what happened to the fold each time, and write what the protein can no longer do and whether it can recover.

04

Enzyme activity and regulation

Describe how enzymes lower activation energy and predict the effect of substrate concentration, temperature, pH, and competitive or allosteric inhibition on reaction rate.

Do one of the two. Head your paper with the number.

4-1

Draw the activation energy diagram with and without an enzyme, and label what the enzyme changed and what it did not. Then draw rate against substrate concentration, mark where the enzyme is saturated, and write in your own words what happens past that point and why.

4-2

Draw rate against substrate three times: normal, with a competitive inhibitor, and with an allosteric one. Beside each, write which way the ceiling moved and whether adding more substrate helps. Then name one real poison of each kind and say what its antidote has to do.

05

ATP and energy coupling

Explain how ATP hydrolysis is coupled to endergonic cellular work and name three categories of work that require it.

Do one of the two. Head your paper with the number.

5-1

Draw ATP losing its last phosphate, and beside it draw one job that energy pays for. Label which side releases energy and which side needs it. Then write the three categories of work and one real example of each from a body system you have already met.

5-2

Draw ATP and ADP as a cycle rather than a line, and mark which half is spending and which half is recharging. Underneath, write why holding only about 50 grams of ATP is enough to live on, and what happens in the first minutes if the recharging half stops.

07

ATP production pathways

Compare glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation by location, oxygen requirement, and ATP yield.

Do one of the two. Head your paper with the number.

7-1

Draw the four stages from glucose to water as a flow chart. For each stage label where in the cell it happens, whether it needs oxygen, what it makes, and how much ATP. Then circle the stage that makes almost all of the ATP and the stage that can run without oxygen.

7-2

Draw the fork at pyruvate, with oxygen on one side and no oxygen on the other. Put the ATP yield on each branch. Then write what lactate is actually for, and trace what happens to pH when a tissue takes the no-oxygen branch for a long time.

Periodic table and molecular mass

Capital letter starts each element: NaCl, CaCl2, Ca(OH)2, C6H12O6. Tapping an element adds one atom of it. Masses are in daltons, and the same number is grams per mole. The slides round to whole numbers (carbon 12, oxygen 16); this table keeps two decimals, so glucose reads 180.18 here and 180 on the slide.
