What it is

An ESR measures how far red blood cells fall in one hour.

ESR stands for erythrocyte sedimentation rate. It is also called a sed rate.

How it is read

The result is the height of the clear plasma, in millimeters.

The red cells settle and leave plasma at the top.

Why it changes

Inflammation makes red cells stack, and stacks fall faster.

The stacks are called rouleaux.

The test

The Westergren method

This is the standard way to measure an ESR.

How it is done

  1. Blood is collected with an so it cannot clot.
  2. The blood is drawn up to the 0 mark of a 200 mm tube.
  3. The tube stands perfectly upright and undisturbed at room temperature.
  4. At 60 minutes, read the line where the clear plasma meets the red cells.

This tube

  • The plasma reaches from 0 down to 34.
  • The ESR is 34 mm/hr.

Other causes

What else changes the ESR

Check for these before reading a result as inflammation, or as no inflammation.

Raises the ESR

Without inflammation

  • Anemia: fewer red cells settle faster.
  • Pregnancy: fibrinogen rises and the blood is more dilute.
  • Older age and female sex, which is why the range changes.
Lowers the ESR

Even with inflammation

  • Sickle cell disease: sickled cells cannot stack.
  • : crowded red cells fall slowly.
  • : round cells do not stack well.
  • Low fibrinogen.
Technique

How the test was run

  • Tilted tube: falsely high.
  • Read after 60 minutes: too high.
  • Read before 60 minutes: too low.
  • Clotted sample: falsely low.

Interpretation

What the size of the number means

The ESR supports a clinical picture. It never makes a diagnosis alone.

Within the range

  • Lowers the suspicion of inflammation.
  • Does not rule out disease. Some inflammatory diseases have a normal ESR.

Above the range

  • Supports inflammation.
  • Common causes are infection, autoimmune disease and cancer.
  • The history, exam and other tests find the cause.

Above 100 mm/hr

  • A serious cause is usually found.
