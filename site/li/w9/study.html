What you are looking at
  • One smooth muscle cell, cut open. The membrane is the outline, the small Ca²⁺ store (SR) sits inside, and the contractile proteins are at the lower right. There is no troponin and no sarcomere, so the switch is different: it is on myosin, and it runs through an enzyme.
  • First we name the parts. Then, on the next slide, we follow what happens, step by step.
1Voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel
  • What it is: A membrane channel that opens when the smooth muscle cell depolarizes
  • What it does: Lets Ca²⁺ in from the extracellular fluid, the main Ca²⁺ source for smooth muscle
  • Why it matters: Calcium channel blockers relax vascular smooth muscle here, lowering blood pressure
3Sarcoplasmic reticulum (smooth muscle)
  • What it is: A small internal Ca²⁺ store, much less developed than in skeletal muscle
  • What it does: Releases Ca²⁺ through IP₃ receptors and ryanodine receptors when signaled
  • Why it matters: Smooth muscle leans on extracellular Ca²⁺ because this store is small
4Calmodulin
  • What it is: The Ca²⁺-binding protein of smooth muscle; it takes the place troponin has in striated muscle
  • What it does: Binds four Ca²⁺, then activates myosin light chain kinase
  • Why it matters: Regulation is on the thick filament (myosin), not on actin
8Myosin light chain phosphatase
  • What it is: The enzyme that removes the phosphate from the myosin light chain
  • What it does: Ends crossbridge cycling as Ca²⁺ falls and calmodulin releases MLCK
  • Why it matters: Signals that change this enzyme's activity change tone without changing Ca²⁺ (Ca²⁺ sensitization)
12345678
2Receptor-operated Ca²⁺ channel
  • What it is: A channel opened by a chemical signal (neurotransmitter or hormone) binding its receptor, not by voltage
  • What it does: Lets Ca²⁺ in without an action potential, so smooth muscle can contract with no electrical spike
  • Why it matters: This is how many hormones and local chemicals set smooth muscle tone
5Myosin light chain kinase (MLCK)
  • What it is: The enzyme switched on by Ca²⁺-calmodulin
  • What it does: Puts a phosphate on the myosin light chain, which turns on myosin ATPase so crossbridges can cycle
  • Why it matters: This extra enzyme step is one reason smooth muscle contracts slowly
6Phosphorylated myosin light chain
  • What it is: Myosin with a phosphate on its regulatory light chain
  • What it does: Only phosphorylated myosin can bind actin and cycle
  • Why it matters: Dephosphorylated myosin can stay attached in the latch state: tension held with almost no ATP
7Dense bodies
  • What it is: Protein anchors in the cytoplasm and on the membrane, the smooth muscle version of Z discs
  • What it does: Actin filaments attach here, so shortening pulls the whole cell in like a drawstring
  • Why it matters: No sarcomeres, no striations, and the cell can shorten far more than skeletal muscle

A. Smooth muscle cell: the calcium switch how it happens

Competency Smooth muscle contraction mechanism · Smooth muscle types and regulation
    You should be able to
  • Smooth muscle contraction mechanism. Trace smooth muscle contraction through calcium entry and release to calmodulin and myosin light chain kinase and explain why the mechanism is slower and more economical than in skeletal muscle.
What you are looking at
  • One smooth muscle cell, cut open. The membrane is the outline, the small Ca²⁺ store (SR) sits inside, and the contractile proteins are at the lower right. There is no troponin and no sarcomere, so the switch is different: it is on myosin, and it runs through an enzyme.
  • Now the sequence: each step lights up where it happens, with a close-up of that moment.
1Ca²⁺ rises, by one of two doors
  • What happens: Electrical door: the cell depolarizes (stretch, a pacemaker cell, or a neurotransmitter opening a cation channel) and voltage-gated L-type Ca²⁺ channels open; Ca²⁺ enters from the ECF. Chemical door: a neurotransmitter or hormone binds a G protein-coupled receptor (norepinephrine on α₁, acetylcholine on M₃, angiotensin II, oxytocin). The receptor activates phospholipase C, IP₃ is made, IP₃ opens Ca²⁺ channels on the SR, and ligand-gated (receptor-operated) channels in the membrane let more Ca²⁺ in. No action potential is needed
2Ca²⁺ binds calmodulin
  • What happens: Four Ca²⁺ per calmodulin, in the cytoplasm. This is the smooth muscle version of Ca²⁺ meeting troponin; there is no troponin here
6Ca²⁺ falls and myosin phosphatase turns it off
  • What happens: Ca²⁺ is pumped out of the cell and back into the SR; calmodulin lets go of MLCK. Myosin light chain phosphatase removes the phosphate, so cycling stops. If a crossbridge is still attached when the phosphate comes off it can stay attached (latch state), holding tension for almost no ATP. Signals that inhibit the phosphatase (through Rho kinase) raise tension without raising Ca²⁺
123456
3Ca²⁺-calmodulin activates myosin light chain kinase
  • What happens: MLCK is an enzyme that was sitting idle; Ca²⁺-calmodulin binds it and turns it on. Because this is an enzyme step, it takes time, which is one reason smooth muscle is slow to start
4MLCK phosphorylates the myosin light chain
  • What happens: MLCK moves a phosphate from ATP onto the regulatory light chain of myosin. That phosphate switches on myosin ATPase; unphosphorylated myosin cannot bind actin
5Crossbridges cycle and the cell shortens
  • What happens: Phosphorylated myosin binds actin and pulls it toward the dense bodies, so the whole cell draws in like a drawstring. Cycling is 10 to 100 times slower than skeletal muscle
The effect
  • Slow, graded contraction that can be held for hours as tone. Tone changes whenever Ca²⁺ entry changes (depolarization, stretch, a ligand opening channels or releasing the SR) or whenever the phosphatase is turned up or down. That is how norepinephrine constricts an arteriole (α₁, Ca²⁺ up), epinephrine relaxes a bronchiole (β₂, cAMP inhibits MLCK), nitric oxide relaxes a vessel (cGMP lowers Ca²⁺ and activates the phosphatase), and calcium channel blockers lower blood pressure.

B. Two cardiac cells and the intercalated disc label the drawing

Competency Cardiac muscle properties · Comparison of the three muscle types
    You should be able to
  • Cardiac muscle properties. Explain how intercalated discs and gap junctions allow the myocardium to act as a functional syncytium and state why cardiac muscle cannot be tetanized.
What you are looking at
  • Two cardiac muscle cells end to end. The stepped dark line between them is the intercalated disc, with its gap junction channels and desmosome anchors. Inside the left cell, a T-tubule drops in from the membrane and meets the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
  • First we name the parts. Then, on the next slide, we follow what happens, step by step.
1Intercalated disc
  • What it is: The stepped junction where one cardiac cell ends and the next begins
  • What it does: Holds the cells together and carries the electrical signal across
  • Why it matters: Because of it the myocardium works as one unit, a functional syncytium
2Gap junction
  • What it is: Channels (connexons) in the intercalated disc that join the cytoplasm of two cells
  • What it does: Let ions and the action potential pass straight to the next cell
  • Why it matters: This is why one SA node signal spreads to every atrial cell; the ventricles form a second syncytium
4T-tubule (cardiac)
  • What it is: An inward fold of the cell membrane, larger than in skeletal muscle
  • What it does: Carries the action potential deep into the cell to the L-type Ca²⁺ channels
  • Why it matters: This is where the electrical signal first lets Ca²⁺ in
5L-type Ca²⁺ channel
  • What it is: A voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel in the T-tubule membrane (the dihydropyridine receptor)
  • What it does: Opens during the action potential plateau and lets a little Ca²⁺ in from the ECF
  • Why it matters: That trigger Ca²⁺ opens the ryanodine receptor: calcium-induced calcium release
6Ryanodine receptor (cardiac SR)
  • What it is: The Ca²⁺ release channel on the sarcoplasmic reticulum
  • What it does: Opens when trigger Ca²⁺ binds it, releasing the SR store, about 90% of the Ca²⁺ for contraction
  • Why it matters: Cardiac EC coupling needs both ECF and SR Ca²⁺; skeletal muscle needs SR only
7Mitochondrion (cardiac)
  • What it is: Up to a third of the cardiac cell's volume is mitochondria
  • What it does: Makes ATP aerobically, all day, from fatty acids, glucose and lactate
  • Why it matters: The heart cannot run an oxygen debt; cut its oxygen and the muscle dies (infarction)
12345678
3Desmosome
  • What it is: A strong protein anchor in the intercalated disc
  • What it does: Holds cells together through the pull of every beat
  • Why it matters: Without it the cells would tear apart under cardiac force
8Branching, single nucleus
  • What it is: Cardiac cells are short, branched and usually have one central nucleus
  • What it does: Branches let each cell touch several neighbors
  • Why it matters: Tells cardiac apart from skeletal (long, many nuclei at the edge) and smooth (spindle, one nucleus, no striations)

What you are looking at
  • Two cardiac muscle cells end to end. The stepped dark line between them is the intercalated disc, with its gap junction channels and desmosome anchors. Inside the left cell, a T-tubule drops in from the membrane and meets the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
  • Now the sequence: each step lights up where it happens, with a close-up of that moment.
1An action potential arrives from the neighbor
  • What happens: Positive current flows through the gap junction channels (connexons) in the intercalated disc and depolarizes this cell to threshold. No nerve and no neurotransmitter are involved between cardiac cells
2The signal runs down the T-tubule
  • What happens: Deep into the cell, to the L-type Ca²⁺ channels
3Trigger Ca²⁺ enters
  • What happens: The L-type channel opens during the plateau and a little Ca²⁺ comes in from the ECF
4The SR releases the rest: calcium-induced calcium release
  • What happens: Trigger Ca²⁺ opens the ryanodine receptors and the store empties into the cytoplasm
6Ca²⁺ is pumped away and the cells relax
  • What happens: SERCA refills the SR and the Na⁺-Ca²⁺ exchanger pushes Ca²⁺ out; mitochondria pay for all of it
123456
5Both cells contract together, held by the desmosomes
  • What happens: Ca²⁺ binds troponin C, tropomyosin moves off the myosin-binding sites on actin, crossbridges cycle; force is graded by how much Ca²⁺ was released. The desmosomes keep the cells from tearing apart under that pull
The effect
  • The whole chamber contracts as one unit, with force graded by how much Ca²⁺ was released. Lose the gap junctions and the heart cannot act as one; lose the oxygen supply and the mitochondria stop and the cells die, which is a heart attack.

What you are looking at
  • Membrane potential of one SA node cell over two heartbeats. Notice there is no flat resting line: as soon as one beat ends the cell starts drifting toward the next.
  • First we name the parts. Then, on the next slide, we follow what happens, step by step.
1Pacemaker potential (slow depolarization)
  • What it is: The slow upward drift from about −60 mV to threshold between beats
  • What it does: Makes the SA node fire on its own, with no nerve input
  • Why it matters: Its slope sets heart rate: steeper slope, faster heart
2Funny current, I_f
  • What it is: A current through HCN channels that open when the cell hyperpolarizes (the opposite of most channels)
  • What it does: Lets Na⁺ in, starting the pacemaker potential
  • Why it matters: Sympathetic cAMP opens more of these channels; the drug ivabradine blocks them to slow the heart
3T-type Ca²⁺ channels (SA node)
  • What it is: Transient Ca²⁺ channels that open as the cell nears threshold
  • What it does: Add Ca²⁺ entry that finishes the climb to threshold
  • Why it matters: Pacemaker depolarization is driven by Na⁺ and Ca²⁺, not by Na⁺ alone
4Threshold in the SA node
  • What it is: About −40 mV in a pacemaker cell
  • What it does: Reaching it opens the L-type Ca²⁺ channels
  • Why it matters: The SA node reaches threshold first (about 70 times a minute with vagal tone), so it sets the rhythm
5Upstroke of the SA node action potential
  • What it is: The rapid rise to about 0 mV
  • What it does: Carried by Ca²⁺ entering through L-type channels, not Na⁺
  • Why it matters: Pacemaker cells have no fast Na⁺ channels, so the upstroke is slower than in a ventricular cell
6Repolarization of the SA node cell
  • What it is: The fall back toward −60 mV
  • What it does: K⁺ leaves through voltage-gated K⁺ channels as the Ca²⁺ channels close
  • Why it matters: As soon as the K⁺ channels close, I_f starts the next pacemaker potential
7Minimum potential (about −60 mV)
  • What it is: The lowest point of the cycle; there is no true resting potential
  • What it does: Hyperpolarization here is what opens the HCN channels
  • Why it matters: Parasympathetic K⁺ channels push this lower, so the climb to threshold takes longer: slower heart

D. SA node cell: pacemaker potential how it happens

Competency Pacemaker potential · Contractile cell action potential · Cardiac refractory period
    You should be able to
  • Pacemaker potential. Explain the unstable pacemaker potential of the sinoatrial node by the funny current, calcium entry, and potassium exit and state what sets intrinsic heart rate.
What you are looking at
  • Membrane potential of one SA node cell over two heartbeats. Notice there is no flat resting line: as soon as one beat ends the cell starts drifting toward the next.
  • Now the sequence: each step lights up where it happens, with a close-up of that moment.
1At −60 mV the funny channels open
  • What happens: HCN channels are unusual: they open when the membrane is hyperpolarized, not depolarized, and cAMP makes them open more. They pass mostly Na⁺ in (a little K⁺ out), so the cell drifts upward: the funny current, I_f
2T-type Ca²⁺ channels add to the climb
  • What happens: As the cell nears threshold the small, transient (T-type) voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open briefly and Ca²⁺ entry finishes the pacemaker potential
3Threshold, about −40 mV
  • What happens: The long-lasting (L-type) voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels open; these are the same channel type a calcium channel blocker acts on
4Upstroke: Ca²⁺ in
  • What happens: Depolarization to about 0 mV carried by Ca²⁺, not Na⁺; there are no fast Na⁺ channels here
5Voltage-gated K⁺ channels open: repolarization
  • What happens: The L-type Ca²⁺ channels inactivate, delayed-rectifier K⁺ channels open, K⁺ leaves down its gradient and the potential falls back to −60 mV
6Back at −60 mV, it starts again
  • What happens: K⁺ channels close, the funny channels reopen; this is the next beat starting
123456
The effect
  • The slope of the pacemaker potential sets heart rate. Sympathetic cAMP steepens it (faster); parasympathetic ACh flattens it and drops the floor (slower). That is the whole mechanism of heart rate control, and the SA node wins because its slope is steepest.

D. Ventricular contractile cell: action potential and twitch label the drawing

Competency Pacemaker potential · Contractile cell action potential · Cardiac refractory period
    You should be able to
  • Contractile cell action potential. Diagram the ventricular action potential and identify the ion movement responsible for the rapid upstroke, the plateau, and repolarization.
  • Cardiac refractory period. Explain why the long refractory period of cardiac muscle prevents tetanus and why that matters for pump function.
What you are looking at
  • One ventricular contractile cell: membrane potential in solid color, the twitch tension dashed, and the refractory period as the bar underneath. Numbers on the left are millivolts.
  • First we name the parts. Then, on the next slide, we follow what happens, step by step.
1Phase 4: resting membrane potential (ventricle)
  • What it is: A stable −90 mV between beats
  • What it does: Held by K⁺ leak; contractile cells wait for a signal from a neighbor
  • Why it matters: Unlike the SA node, contractile cells do not drift to threshold on their own
2Phase 0: rapid upstroke
  • What it is: Depolarization to about +20 mV in 1 to 2 ms
  • What it does: Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open; Na⁺ rushes in
  • Why it matters: Fast upstroke means fast conduction from cell to cell
3Phase 1: brief early repolarization
  • What it is: A small dip just after the peak
  • What it does: Na⁺ channels inactivate and a few K⁺ channels open briefly
  • Why it matters: Sets up the plateau that follows
4Phase 2: plateau
  • What it is: The long flat part, about 200 ms, near 0 mV
  • What it does: Ca²⁺ in through L-type channels balances K⁺ out; this Ca²⁺ triggers contraction
  • Why it matters: The plateau makes the cardiac action potential as long as the twitch, which prevents tetanus
6Absolute refractory period
  • What it is: About 250 ms during which no second action potential can start, because the Na⁺ channels are inactivated
  • What it does: Lasts almost as long as the contraction itself
  • Why it matters: No summation, no tetanus: the ventricle relaxes and fills between beats, which is the whole point of a pump
7Tension curve (cardiac twitch)
  • What it is: The contraction that follows the action potential
  • What it does: Rises and falls while the cell is still refractory
  • Why it matters: In skeletal muscle the refractory period is over before tension peaks, so skeletal muscle can be tetanized and cardiac cannot
1234567
5Phase 3: repolarization (ventricle)
  • What it is: The return to −90 mV
  • What it does: Ca²⁺ channels close, slow K⁺ channels open, K⁺ leaves
  • Why it matters: On the ECG this is the T wave

    You should be able to

What you are looking at
  • One ventricular contractile cell: membrane potential in solid color, the twitch tension dashed, and the refractory period as the bar underneath. Numbers on the left are millivolts.
  • Now the sequence: each step lights up where it happens, with a close-up of that moment.
1Phase 4: resting at −90 mV
  • What happens: K⁺ leak holds it; this cell waits for a neighbor, it does not drift
2Phase 0: Na⁺ rushes in
  • What happens: A neighbor depolarizes this cell to threshold through gap junctions; fast voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open and Na⁺ pours in; the upstroke takes 1 to 2 ms
3Phase 1: a brief dip
  • What happens: Na⁺ channels inactivate and a few K⁺ channels open
4Phase 2: the plateau, Ca²⁺ in
  • What happens: Voltage-gated L-type Ca²⁺ channels (opened by the depolarization) let Ca²⁺ in while K⁺ permeability is low; the two currents balance for about 200 ms. This Ca²⁺ is the trigger for calcium-induced calcium release and the contraction
6Refractory through most of the twitch
  • What happens: Na⁺ channels stay inactivated about 250 ms, so no second action potential can start until the contraction is nearly over
123456
5Phase 3: K⁺ out, repolarization
  • What happens: The Ca²⁺ channels inactivate and the slow delayed-rectifier K⁺ channels open; K⁺ leaves and the cell returns to −90 mV. On the ECG this is the T wave
The effect
  • The plateau does two jobs: it brings in the Ca²⁺ that starts the contraction, and it keeps the cell refractory until the twitch is almost finished. So cardiac muscle cannot be tetanized, the ventricle always relaxes and refills, and the pump keeps pumping.

  • Why it matters: A rhythm set by the SA node is called sinus rhythm
2Internodal pathways
  • What it is: Conducting fibers running through the atria from SA node to AV node
  • What it does: Spread the signal across both atria (P wave) and deliver it to the AV node
  • Why it matters: The fibrous skeleton stops the signal there: the AV node is the only electrical road to the ventricles
3Atrioventricular (AV) node
  • What it is: Pacemaker and conducting tissue at the floor of the right atrium
  • What it does: Slows the signal by about 0.1 s so the atria finish contracting before the ventricles start
  • Why it matters: The AV delay is the PR segment; its backup rate is 40 to 60 a minute
5Bundle branches
  • What it is: Left and right branches running down the interventricular septum
  • What it does: Carry the signal fast to the apex
  • Why it matters: Depolarization starts at the apex, so the ventricles squeeze from the bottom up toward the outflow valves
123456
4Bundle of His (AV bundle)
  • What it is: The conducting bundle that pierces the fibrous skeleton into the septum
  • What it does: Carries the signal from the AV node to the bundle branches
  • Why it matters: The one route through the insulating fibrous skeleton
6Purkinje fibers
  • What it is: Large, fast-conducting fibers that spread from the apex through the ventricular walls
  • What it does: Deliver the signal to the contractile cells almost at once (QRS lasts under 0.12 s)
  • Why it matters: Backup rate of 25 to 40 a minute if everything above fails
