1Elastic artery
  • What it is: Aorta and its big branches: thick wall with layers of elastin
  • What it does: Stretches during ejection and recoils during diastole, so flow continues between beats
  • Why it matters: Pressure reservoir: stiff arteries (age, atherosclerosis) raise systolic pressure and pulse pressure
2Arteriole
  • What it is: Small artery with a thick ring of smooth muscle and little elastin
  • What it does: The main site of resistance; its radius sets how much flow a tissue gets and how much pressure is left for the capillaries
  • Why it matters: Total peripheral resistance lives here; this is where blood pressure drugs act
3Precapillary sphincter
  • What it is: A ring of smooth muscle where a capillary leaves a metarteriole
  • What it does: Opens or closes individual capillary beds
  • Why it matters: Lets a tissue route blood to the cells that need it
9Pulse pressure in the artery
  • What it is: The swing between systolic and diastolic in the arteries
  • What it does: Shows the stroke volume hitting an elastic wall
  • Why it matters: The pulse you feel at the wrist is this pressure wave, not the blood itself
10Pressure drop across the arterioles
  • What it is: The steepest fall on the profile, from about 93 to about 35 mm Hg
  • What it does: Resistance converts pressure into a controlled trickle for the capillaries
  • Why it matters: Dilate the arterioles and pressure downstream rises; constrict them and it falls
123456789101112
4Capillary
  • What it is: One layer of endothelium, 5 to 10 μm across, with a basement membrane
  • What it does: The only vessel that exchanges: gases, nutrients, waste and fluid cross here
  • Why it matters: Huge total cross-section makes flow slowest here, which gives time for exchange
5Venule
  • What it is: Small vein that collects capillaries
  • What it does: Some exchange continues; white cells leave the blood here in inflammation
  • Why it matters: First place pressure is low enough for cells to crawl out
6Vein
  • What it is: Thin wall, little muscle, large lumen, very compliant
  • What it does: Capacitance vessel: holds about 60% of the blood at rest and gives it up when the sympathetic system tightens it
  • Why it matters: The venous reservoir is what venous return and preload are drawn from
7Venous valve
  • What it is: Flaps of endothelium in limb veins
  • What it does: Let blood flow toward the heart only
  • Why it matters: Make the skeletal muscle pump work; failed valves give varicose veins
8Endothelium
  • Step by step, the numbers on the drawing:
    1. A dilating signal reaches the lining: blood dragging across it (shear stress), or acetylcholine, histamine or bradykinin binding receptors on the endothelial cell.
    2. The endothelial cell switches on its nitric oxide synthase and makes nitric oxide, a gas that diffuses straight through membranes.
    3. Nitric oxide enters the smooth muscle cell underneath, raises cGMP, and the muscle relaxes: the vessel dilates.
    4. A constricting signal reaches the lining instead: injury, low flow, or angiotensin II.
    5. The endothelial cell makes endothelin, which binds the smooth muscle and works through IP₃ and Ca²⁺: the vessel constricts.
    6. The lining makes its own dilator and its own constrictor. Nitroglycerin and sildenafil both act on the nitric oxide side.
  • What it is: The single cell layer lining every vessel
  • What it does: Makes nitric oxide and endothelin, controls what crosses, keeps blood from clotting on it
  • Why it matters: Endothelial damage is the start of atherosclerosis
11Capillary pressure
  • What it is: About 32 mm Hg at the arterial end falling to about 15 at the venous end
  • What it does: Pushes fluid out through the pores (filtration)
  • Why it matters: This is the pushing force in the Starling balance
12Venous pressure
  • What it is: Low and steady, a few mm Hg, nearly zero at the right atrium
  • What it does: The small gradient that still drives blood back; it needs help from the pumps
  • Why it matters: Central venous pressure is a bedside measure of volume status

1The ventricle ejects into an elastic artery
  • What happens: During systole the aorta's elastic wall stretches and stores some of the pulse's energy; pressure peaks near 120 mm Hg
2Diastole: the artery recoils
  • What happens: The stretched wall springs back and keeps pushing blood forward between beats, so flow never stops and pressure only falls to about 80; the pulses on the graph are this stretch and recoil
3The arteriole resists
  • What happens: A thick ring of smooth muscle makes the arteriole the narrowest adjustable point; most of the pressure is spent getting through it (about 93 down to 35) and the pulse disappears
123456
4The capillary exchanges
  • What happens: Total cross-sectional area is enormous, so velocity is slowest here; with one cell of wall, gases, nutrients, waste and fluid cross
5Venules and veins collect and store
  • What happens: Pressure is now a few mm Hg; the thin, compliant veins hold about 60% of the blood, and valves keep it moving one way
6Back to the right atrium at about 0 mm Hg
  • What happens: The whole loop runs on one rule: flow = pressure gradient ÷ resistance, so cardiac output = MAP ÷ total peripheral resistance
The effect
  • Pressure falls all the way around, and most of it is spent at the arterioles. Resistance goes with 1 ÷ radius⁴, so halving an arteriole's radius raises its resistance 16 times: small changes in arteriolar tone are the body's main way to steer flow and set blood pressure.

A. The vessels, and the rules of flow fill in

Competency Vessel structure and function · Pressure flow and resistance
    You should be able to
  • Pressure flow and resistance. Apply the relationship among flow, pressure gradient, and resistance and predict the effect of a change in vessel radius, blood viscosity, or vessel length on flow.

The rules of flow

Flow =pressure gradient ÷ resistance (F = ΔP ÷ R); for the whole circuit, CO = MAP ÷ TPR
Resistance depends onvessel length, blood viscosity, and radius; R is proportional to length × viscosity ÷ radius⁴
Halve the radius andresistance rises 16 times, so flow falls to one sixteenth at the same pressure
Double the viscosity (polycythemia) andresistance doubles, flow halves
Length matters whennew vessels grow (obesity, a tumor); otherwise it is fixed
Velocity of flow isflow ÷ total cross-sectional area: fastest in the aorta, slowest in the capillaries, faster again in the veins

  • What it is: The peak, about 120 mm Hg, during ventricular ejection
  • What it does: Set by stroke volume and how stiff the arteries are
  • Why it matters: This is the top number of a blood pressure reading
2Diastolic pressure
  • What it is: The trough, about 80 mm Hg, just before the next ejection
  • What it does: Set mainly by total peripheral resistance and how long diastole lasts
  • Why it matters: This is the bottom number of a blood pressure reading, and it tells you how tight the arterioles are

  • What it does: Marks the aortic valve closing (S2) and the brief backflow against it

4Pulse pressure
  • What it is: Systolic minus diastolic, about 40 mm Hg
  • What it does: Rises with a bigger stroke volume or stiffer arteries
  • Why it matters: A wide pulse pressure in an older adult means stiff arteries
5Mean arterial pressure
  • What it is: The average pressure over the cycle, about 93 mm Hg; diastolic + one third of pulse pressure
  • What it does: The pressure that actually drives flow to the organs
  • Why it matters: MAP = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance; the number the baroreflex defends
6Cuff pressure
  • What it is: The pressure in the inflated cuff, let down slowly
  • What it does: Above systolic it flattens the artery; below diastolic it does nothing
  • Why it matters: The sounds you hear only exist while cuff pressure is between the two
12345678
7First Korotkoff sound
  • What it is: The first tapping heard as the cuff is let down
  • What it does: Cuff pressure has just dropped below systolic: blood spurts through the squeezed artery in turbulent bursts
  • Why it matters: The cuff reading at that moment is the systolic pressure
8Last Korotkoff sound
  • What it is: The sounds muffle and then stop
  • What it does: Cuff pressure has dropped below diastolic: the artery stays open and flow is smooth and silent
  • Why it matters: The cuff reading at that moment is the diastolic pressure

What you are looking at
  • Left: the pressure in a large artery, beat by beat. Right: the same wave with a cuff pressure (gold) being let down across it. The sounds you hear through the stethoscope are drawn below the wave.
  • Now the sequence: each step lights up where it happens, with a close-up of that moment.
1Cuff above systolic: the artery is shut
  • What happens: At 140 mm Hg the cuff squashes the brachial artery flat; no blood passes, no sound
5Pulse pressure and mean pressure
  • What happens: Pulse pressure = systolic − diastolic (120 − 80 = 40). Mean arterial pressure = diastolic + ⅓ pulse pressure (80 + 13 = 93), because diastole lasts longer than systole
6What sets each number
  • What happens: Systolic rises with stroke volume and stiff arteries; diastolic rises with resistance (arteriolar tone) and heart rate; MAP = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance
123456
2Cuff drops below systolic: the first tap
  • What happens: The first time the pulse peak exceeds cuff pressure, blood squirts through the narrowed artery in a turbulent burst; that tap is the first Korotkoff sound and the cuff reading is systolic
3Sounds continue while the cuff is between the two
  • What happens: Each beat the artery opens briefly and snaps shut: tapping with every pulse
4Cuff drops below diastolic: silence
  • What happens: The artery stays open all the time, flow is smooth (laminar) and makes no noise; the cuff reading when the sounds vanish is diastolic
The effect
  • Two numbers from a cuff tell you about the pump and the pipes: a high diastolic means the arterioles are tight, a wide pulse pressure means stiff arteries or a big stroke volume. MAP is the number the body defends, and it has only two levers: cardiac output and resistance.

1Capillary hydrostatic pressure, arterial end
  • What it is: Blood pressure inside the capillary at its start, about 32 mm Hg
  • What it does: Pushes fluid out through the pores
  • Why it matters: The force that wins at the arterial end
5Net filtration (arterial end)
  • What it is: Out minus in: about 32 − 25 = 7 mm Hg outward
  • What it does: Fluid leaves the capillary carrying dissolved nutrients
  • Why it matters: Filtration is how plasma, with its dissolved nutrients, reaches the cells
8Capillary wall (endothelium with pores)
  • What it is: One cell thick, with pores between the cells (and fenestrations in kidney and gut)
  • What it does: Water and small solutes pass; proteins mostly do not
  • Why it matters: Inflammation widens the pores, proteins leak, and the osmotic pull collapses
12345678
2Capillary hydrostatic pressure, venous end
  • What it is: Blood pressure at the capillary's end, about 15 mm Hg
  • What it does: Still pushes out, but weakly
  • Why it matters: At this end the osmotic pull is stronger than the push, so fluid comes back in
3Plasma colloid osmotic pressure
  • What it is: About 25 mm Hg of pull from plasma proteins, mostly albumin, that cannot leave the capillary
  • What it does: Draws water back in along the whole length
  • Why it matters: Low albumin (liver failure, kidney loss, starvation) means fluid stays out: edema
4Interstitial fluid
  • What it is: The fluid between the cells, about a quarter of the extracellular fluid
  • What it does: Its own hydrostatic pressure is near zero and its protein is low, so its forces are small
  • Why it matters: Edema is simply too much interstitial fluid
6Net absorption (venous end)
  • What it is: In minus out: about 25 − 15 = 10 mm Hg inward
  • What it does: Most of the filtered fluid returns here
  • Why it matters: Across the body about 3 L a day is left over, not returned
7Lymphatic capillary
  • What it is: Blind-ended vessel with overlapping flaps that open inward
  • What it does: Picks up the leftover 3 L a day and any protein that leaked, and returns it to the veins
  • Why it matters: Block it and the limb swells (lymphedema)

D. Starling forces along one capillary how it happens

Competency Capillary exchange and Starling forces · Edema · Lymphatic return
    You should be able to
  • Capillary exchange and Starling forces. Identify the four Starling forces at a capillary and determine the net direction of fluid movement at the arterial and venous ends.
What you are looking at
  • One capillary opened out, blood flowing left to right. Red arrows: pressure pushing fluid out through the pores. Blue arrows: the pull of plasma proteins drawing fluid back in. Green: the lymphatic that collects what is left over.
  • Now the sequence: each step lights up where it happens, with a close-up of that moment.
1At the arterial end, hydrostatic pressure wins
  • What happens: Capillary hydrostatic pressure is about 32 mm Hg pushing out; plasma colloid osmotic pressure is about 25 pulling in; net 7 mm Hg outward: filtration
2Fluid leaves, proteins stay
  • What happens: Water and small solutes pass through the pores between endothelial cells; albumin is too big, so the osmotic pull inside stays at 25
123456
3Pressure falls along the capillary
  • What happens: Friction drops hydrostatic pressure from 32 to about 15 by the venous end; the osmotic pull does not change
4At the venous end, the osmotic pull wins
  • What happens: 25 in against 15 out: net 10 mm Hg inward: absorption. Most of what left comes back
5The lymphatic takes the remainder
  • What happens: About 3 L a day across the body is not reabsorbed; lymphatic capillaries pick it up, with any protein that leaked, and return it to the veins near the heart
6The interstitial forces are small
  • What happens: Interstitial hydrostatic pressure is near zero and interstitial protein is low, so in health the balance is set by the two capillary forces
The effect
  • Filtration at the start, absorption at the end, lymph for the difference. Raise capillary pressure, lower plasma protein, open the pores, or block the lymphatics and fluid piles up in the tissue: that is edema, and each cause maps to a disease.

  • First we name the parts. Then, on the next slide, we follow what happens, step by step.
1Carotid sinus baroreceptor
  • Step by step, the numbers on the drawing:
    1. At normal pressure the carotid sinus wall is stretched a little.
    2. The baroreceptor nerve endings in the wall fire a steady, modest train of action potentials up the glossopharyngeal nerve (from the aortic arch, the vagus).
    3. Pressure rises: the wall stretches more.
    4. The endings fire faster. More spikes per second is the message that pressure is up; fewer spikes is the message that it is down.
    5. The medulla reads the firing rate and adjusts sympathetic and parasympathetic output within a few seconds.
    6. More stretch, more spikes: that is the whole sensor.
  • What it is: Stretch receptors in the wall of the carotid sinus, where the common carotid splits
  • What it does: Fire faster when stretched by higher pressure, slower when pressure falls
  • Why it matters: Guards pressure to the brain; pressing on the neck here can slow the heart
2Aortic arch baroreceptor
  • What it is: Stretch receptors in the wall of the aortic arch
  • What it does: Same signal as the carotid sinus, for the whole body
  • Why it matters: Both sets report continuously; the reflex is tonic, not just emergency
3Glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX), afferent
  • What it is: The nerve carrying carotid sinus signals to the medulla
  • What it does: It carries sensory signals in; it is the afferent arm of the reflex for the carotid sinus
  • Why it matters: Cut it and the medulla no longer hears from the carotid sinus
4Vagus nerve (CN X), afferent
  • What it is: Carries aortic arch signals to the medulla
  • What it does: Sensory input (the vagus also carries the parasympathetic output back out)
  • Why it matters: The same nerve carries the sensory signal in and the parasympathetic signal back out
123456789
5Medullary cardiovascular control center
  • What it is: The nucleus of the solitary tract and the neighboring centers in the medulla
  • What it does: Compares baroreceptor input with the set point and adjusts the two autonomic outputs
  • Why it matters: This is the integrating center of the reflex, where the comparison with the set point happens
6Parasympathetic output to the SA node
  • Step by step, the numbers on the drawing:
    1. Acetylcholine from the vagus nerve arrives at the SA node cell.
    2. It binds the M₂ receptor, a G protein-coupled receptor on the membrane.
    3. M₂ works through Gi: adenylyl cyclase is inhibited and cAMP falls, so the funny channels open less and the drift is flatter.
    4. Gi also opens K⁺ channels directly; K⁺ leaves and the membrane sits lower (hyperpolarized), so the floor drops.
    5. Flatter slope from a lower floor: threshold takes longer to reach.
    6. Heart rate goes down. The vagus fires all the time at rest, which is why the resting rate sits near 70 instead of the node's own 100.
  • What it is: Vagal fibers releasing acetylcholine on M₂ receptors
  • What it does: Slows the pacemaker
  • Why it matters: This is the fast brake on heart rate, and resting vagal tone is why the resting rate is near 70 rather than 100
7Sympathetic output to the heart
  • Step by step, the numbers on the drawing:
    1. Norepinephrine from the sympathetic nerve, or epinephrine from the adrenal medulla, arrives at the SA node cell.
    2. It binds the β₁ receptor, a G protein-coupled receptor on the membrane.
    3. β₁ works through Gs: adenylyl cyclase is switched on and cAMP rises inside the cell.
    4. cAMP binds the funny (HCN) channel directly and opens it more; I_f is larger. cAMP also activates PKA, which opens more L-type Ca²⁺ channels (the red channel).
    5. The pacemaker potential climbs more steeply and reaches threshold sooner.
    6. Heart rate goes up. In ventricular muscle the same cAMP raises contractility, so each beat is stronger too.
  • What it is: Sympathetic fibers releasing norepinephrine on β₁ receptors in the SA node and ventricles
  • What it does: Raises heart rate and contractility
  • Why it matters: Raises cardiac output within seconds
8Sympathetic output to arterioles
  • Step by step, the numbers on the drawing:
    1. Norepinephrine lands on the α₁ receptor on the outside of the smooth muscle cell. The receptor is a G protein-coupled receptor: nothing passes through it.
    2. The receptor switches on its G protein, Gq, on the inside of the membrane.
    3. Gq switches on phospholipase C, which cuts a membrane lipid (PIP₂) and releases IP₃ into the cytoplasm.
    4. IP₃ floats to the sarcoplasmic reticulum and opens its Ca²⁺ release channel, the IP₃ receptor.
    5. Ca²⁺ pours out of the store into the cytoplasm.
    6. At the same time, the same signal opens the receptor-operated Ca²⁺ channel in the membrane, and more Ca²⁺ comes in from outside. No action potential anywhere in this chain.
    7. Ca²⁺ binds calmodulin, calmodulin switches on MLCK, MLCK phosphorylates myosin, and the cell contracts. Acetylcholine on M₃, angiotensin II on AT₁, oxytocin and endothelin run the same chain from step 1 with their own receptor.
  • What it is: Norepinephrine on α₁ receptors in arteriolar smooth muscle
  • What it does: Constricts arterioles, raising total peripheral resistance
  • Why it matters: Resistance is one of the two terms in MAP = CO × TPR, so this output moves blood pressure quickly
9Sympathetic output to veins
  • What it is: Norepinephrine on α₁ receptors in venous smooth muscle
  • What it does: Venoconstriction raises venous return and stroke volume
  • Why it matters: Pulls blood out of the reservoir when pressure falls

1Pressure falls, the baroreceptors stretch less
  • What happens: Carotid sinus and aortic arch receptors fire slower

F. The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, with ADH and ANP label the drawing

Competency Baroreceptor reflex · Hormonal control of blood pressure · Adrenal cortex
    You should be able to
  • Hormonal control of blood pressure. Describe the roles of angiotensin II, aldosterone, antidiuretic hormone, and atrial natriuretic peptide in long term blood pressure control.
What you are looking at
  • The long-term lever on blood pressure: a chain of hormones that ends in the kidney keeping salt and water. Follow the arrows from the kidney at the left, through the blood, to the targets on the right. ANP, in blue, pulls the other way.
  • First we name the parts. Then, on the next slide, we follow what happens, step by step.
1Juxtaglomerular cells (renin)
  • Step by step, the numbers on the drawing:
    1. Pressure in the afferent arteriole falls. The juxtaglomerular cells sit in its wall and are stretch sensors, so less stretch is their first signal.
    2. Sympathetic nerves release norepinephrine onto β₁ receptors on the JG cells.
    3. The macula densa, next door in the distal tubule, senses less NaCl going past (less filtrate) and signals the JG cells.
    4. All three signals mean the same thing to the kidney: pressure or volume is low.
    5. The JG cells release renin into the blood.
    6. Renin starts the cascade: angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, ACE turns that into angiotensin II, and pressure comes up.
  • What it is: Modified smooth muscle cells in the wall of the afferent arteriole of the nephron
  • What it does: Release renin when renal pressure falls, when the macula densa sees less NaCl, or when sympathetic β₁ receptors are stimulated
  • Why it matters: The kidney's own pressure sensor starts the cascade
2Angiotensinogen (from the liver)
  • What it is: A plasma protein always present in blood
  • What it does: Renin cuts it to angiotensin I
  • Why it matters: Nothing happens without the enzyme; the substrate is just waiting
3Angiotensin I
  • What it is: A ten amino acid peptide, inactive
  • What it does: Nothing yet; it circulates until an enzyme converts it
  • Why it matters: It is a step with no action of its own, which is why the next enzyme matters so much
4Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)
  • What it is: An enzyme on the endothelium, especially in the lungs
  • What it does: Converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II
  • Why it matters: ACE inhibitors (the -pril drugs) block this step to lower blood pressure
9Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)
  • Step by step, the numbers on the drawing:
    1. Too much blood volume stretches the walls of the atria.
    2. The stretched atrial muscle cells release atrial natriuretic peptide into the blood.
    3. At the kidney, ANP makes the nephron excrete more Na⁺ and water.
    4. On arterioles, ANP relaxes the smooth muscle: they dilate and resistance falls.
    5. ANP inhibits renin, aldosterone and ADH, so the whole volume-raising cascade is switched off.
    6. Volume and pressure come down. This is the only one of the hormones that pushes pressure down, and it is released only when there is already too much.
  • What it is: A peptide released by the atria when they are stretched by too much volume
  • What it does: Makes the kidney excrete Na⁺ and water, dilates arterioles, and inhibits renin, aldosterone and ADH
  • Why it matters: It is the brake on the whole system, released when there is already too much volume
123456789
5Angiotensin II
  • Step by step, the numbers on the drawing:
    1. Angiotensin II arrives in the blood, made from angiotensin I by ACE in the lungs after renin started the cascade.
    2. It binds the AT₁ receptor, a Gq-coupled receptor, on each of its targets.
    3. On arterioles: IP₃ and Ca²⁺ rise and the smooth muscle constricts. Resistance, and so pressure, rise within seconds.
    4. On the adrenal cortex: aldosterone is released, which will raise blood volume over days.
    5. On the posterior pituitary: ADH is released; in the hypothalamus: thirst. Both add water.
    6. On sympathetic nerves: activity goes up, adding to the constriction.
    7. One hormone, one direction: pressure up now by resistance, and later by volume.
  • What it is: The active hormone of the whole system
  • What it does: Constricts arterioles, triggers aldosterone and ADH, drives thirst, and raises sympathetic activity
  • Why it matters: It raises pressure right away by raising resistance, and over the following days by raising blood volume
6Aldosterone (adrenal cortex)
  • Step by step, the numbers on the drawing:
    1. Aldosterone, a steroid from the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex, passes straight through the membrane of a distal nephron cell.
    2. Inside, it binds a receptor that acts on genes: the cell makes more Na⁺ channels and more Na⁺-K⁺ pumps. Because it works through new proteins it is slow, hours to days.
    3. More Na⁺ is reabsorbed from the tubule back into the blood.
    4. Water follows the sodium, so blood volume rises.
    5. K⁺ is secreted into the tubule in exchange, which is why aldosterone excess lowers blood potassium.
    6. Blood volume, and with it pressure, rise over hours to days: the slow lever.
  • What it is: A steroid from the zona glomerulosa
  • What it does: Makes the distal nephron reabsorb Na⁺ (and water follows) and secrete K⁺
  • Why it matters: The slow lever: more volume, more pressure, over hours to days
7Antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin)
  • Step by step, the numbers on the drawing:
    1. ADH (vasopressin) from the posterior pituitary binds the V₂ receptor on a collecting duct cell.
    2. V₂ raises cAMP inside the cell, and the cell inserts aquaporin-2 water channels into its tubule-facing membrane.
    3. Water leaves the tubule through the channels and returns to the blood.
    4. The urine becomes concentrated and blood volume is protected.
    5. At high levels ADH also binds V₁ receptors on arterioles and constricts them, which is why it has the second name vasopressin.
    6. It is released by angiotensin II, by a rise in plasma osmolarity, and by a large fall in pressure.
  • What it is: A peptide from the hypothalamus released by the posterior pituitary
  • What it does: Makes the collecting duct reabsorb water; at high levels constricts arterioles
  • Why it matters: Released by angiotensin II, by high osmolarity, and by a large fall in pressure
8Thirst center
  • What it is: Hypothalamic neurons that angiotensin II and high osmolarity stimulate
  • What it does: They make you feel thirsty and drink
  • Why it matters: Volume cannot be replaced by the kidney alone; intake has to rise
