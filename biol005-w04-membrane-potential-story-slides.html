01

The baseline story

The resting membrane potential, told as a story

We are going to build a resting cell from nothing, one small piece at a time. We start with a balloon full of little balls. There is no vocabulary to learn first.

  • Each part adds one new piece of information and then asks you a question you can already answer.
  • You draw along on your own sheet: the arrows, the plus and minus signs and the labels.
  • A scientific name arrives only after you understand the idea it belongs to. There are no numbers today.

What we are building

A balloon becomes a cell. The cell builds doors and one machine. By the end it holds a steady electrical difference across its membrane, and you can explain where that difference came from.

02

Part 1

The balloon

Start with a balloon. Inside the balloon there are lots of little balls. Outside the balloon there are only a few.

Ask

If these balls could move through the balloon, which way would they naturally move?

Answer

Out. There are lots of them inside and fewer outside, so they naturally tend to spread out.

Each ball moves at random. Because there are more balls inside, more of them happen to cross outward than inward, so the overall movement is toward the side with fewer.

Lots here → fewer there → movement toward fewer.

03

Part 2

Turn the balloon into a cell

Let's turn our balloon into a cell. It has a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus. There is an important difference between our imaginary balloon and a real cell: the particles cannot simply move through the membrane whenever they want.

Ask

If the membrane stops them, what would they need in order to cross?

Answer

They need doors. A door is a way through the membrane that is made for them.

Nobody hands the cell its doors. Cells have to build those doors.

No door → no crossing.

04

Part 3

Go to the nucleus

Inside the nucleus is DNA. DNA contains genes, and a gene is the instructions for making a protein. Some of those proteins will become doors in the cell membrane.

Ask

A door is a protein. Where are the instructions for building it, and where does the finished door have to end up?

Answer

The instructions are a gene, in the DNA, inside the nucleus. The cell reads the gene and builds the protein, and the protein is placed in the membrane.

We are going to start by building one particular door.

The full lecture shows the stops along the way: mRNA, rough ER, Golgi and a vesicle.

Gene → protein → membrane.

05

Part 4

Build a potassium leak channel

Our little balls have an identity. They are potassium ions, K+. We have lots of K+ inside and less K+ outside. The cell uses a gene to build a potassium leak channel. It is a selective door: this door lets potassium through, and potassium does not need a special signal to use it.

Ask

Which way will potassium go?

Answer

Out. You already know this from the balloon: lots inside, less outside.

Now you can give that old idea its grown-up name. The concentration gradient is pushing K+ out of the cell.

Lots of K+ inside → less outside → K+ moves OUT.

Now name it: concentration gradient

06

Part 5

But potassium has a charge

Now one new piece of information. These are not ordinary balls. Each K+ carries a positive charge. So every time a K+ leaves, a positive charge leaves with it.

Ask

What is going to happen to the inside if positive charges keep leaving?

Answer

It becomes more negative compared with the outside.

The cell started out balanced, with a negative charge inside for every positive one. Each K+ that leaves carries its positive charge out and leaves its negative partner behind. The unmatched charges sit right at the membrane: minus signs on the inner face, plus signs on the outer face.

K+ leaves → positive leaves → inside becomes more negative.

07

Part 6

Uh-oh. Now potassium wants two different things

Potassium still has its original problem: there is lots of K+ inside, so the concentration difference keeps pushing K+ out. But now the inside of our cell has become negative, and K+ is positive.

Ask

Opposite charges attract. So which way does the negative inside pull a positive K+?

Answer

Back in. Now potassium is caught in a tug of war.

Concentration says: OUT. Charge says: IN.

Concentration pushes K+ OUT. The negative inside pulls K+ back IN.

08

Part 7

Eventually the tug of war ties

At first the concentration push is the stronger of the two, and more K+ leaves. But every positive K+ that leaves makes the inside a little more negative, and that makes the electrical pull inward stronger. Eventually the push OUT equals the pull IN.

Ask

Does potassium stop moving?

Answer

No. Individual K+ ions can still move in and out through the leak channels. But now K+ going OUT is about equal to K+ coming IN, so there is no NET movement of potassium. The potassium tug of war is tied.

It takes only a very small number of K+ leaving to get here. The inside still has lots of K+.

Now it gets its name. You do not need a number yet. It is the point where potassium's concentration force pushing it out is balanced by the electrical force pulling it in.

Push OUT = pull IN → no net movement of K+.

Now name it: potassium equilibrium potential

09

Part 8

But our cell is not really a potassium-only cell

We cheated a little. Real cells do not only have potassium. Let's add sodium, Na+. There is lots of Na+ OUTSIDE and less Na+ INSIDE.

Ask

Sodium cannot cross our membrane either, and the potassium door only lets potassium through. So what do we need?

Answer

Another door, one that lets sodium through. And the cell has to build it.

New ion → it needs its own door.

10

Part 9

Back to the nucleus: a sodium leak channel

Back to the nucleus. The cell uses another gene, builds a sodium leak channel and puts it in the membrane.

Ask

Which direction does sodium want to move?

What charge is sodium? And what charge have we created inside our cell?

Answer

In. There is more sodium outside than inside.

Sodium is positive, and the inside of our cell is negative, so the inside attracts it. Sodium has TWO reasons to enter.

For potassium the two forces were fighting each other. For sodium the two forces are currently working together.

Concentration → Na+ IN. Electrical attraction → Na+ IN.

11

Part 10

Now we have a real resting cell

Stand back and look at the whole membrane. K+ is leaking OUT and Na+ is leaking IN. But our cell has a lot more opportunity for potassium to leak than sodium. The scientific way to say it: the membrane at rest is much more permeable to K+ than it is to Na+.

Ask

Which ion has the bigger influence on the electrical difference across the membrane? So is the inside negative or positive?

Answer

Potassium has the bigger influence, so the inside stays negative.

But the sodium leaking in brings some positive charge back in. So the inside does not become quite as negative as it would if potassium were the only ion that could cross.

No numbers yet. You understand what produced it.

Many K+ doors, few Na+ doors → K+ has the bigger influence → the inside stays negative.

Now name it: resting membrane potential

12

Part 11

There is a problem

Look at the two leaks. Neither one ever stops.

Ask

If K+ keeps leaking out and Na+ keeps leaking in, what eventually happens to our nice differences between inside and outside?

Answer

Over time, we would start losing them. K+ inside would slowly fall and Na+ inside would slowly rise.

Our cell needs a way to maintain those concentration differences. So we are going to build another protein. But this one is not a channel.

The leaks keep running → the differences would gradually run down.

13

Part 12

Back to the nucleus: build a machine, not a door

Back to the nucleus: gene, protein, membrane. A CHANNEL is a door. Ions move through it according to the forces acting on them. This new protein is a MACHINE. It uses energy, from ATP, to move ions.

Ask

Na+ has been leaking in and K+ has been leaking out. To keep our differences, which way does the machine have to move each ion?

Answer

Na+ back OUT and K+ back IN. Each cycle uses ATP to move 3 Na+ OUT and 2 K+ IN. It costs energy because both ions are moved toward the side that already has more.

So the pump maintains lots of Na+ outside and lots of K+ inside, and the leak process can continue over time.

This same pump is also what set up those differences in the first place. It was running long before our story began, and that is why our cell started with lots of K+ inside and lots of Na+ outside.

The pump maintains the gradients. The leak channels allow ions to move down those gradients.

Now name it: sodium potassium pump (Na+/K+ ATPase)

14

Part 13

The different kinds of doors

You have earned the channel terminology. You already know a leak channel. Cells can make different kinds of ion channels. What makes them different is what controls whether the door is open.

Ask

Doors 2 and 3 each have a part that door 1 does not have. What do you think controls each of these doors?

Answer

Leak channel: usually available without a special signal. No key required. That is what we have been using.

Ligand gated channel: needs a chemical key. Something binds to the protein. Chemical binds → door changes.

Voltage gated channel: responds to a change in the electrical conditions across the membrane. Electrical change → door changes.

That is all you need right now, and this is where we stop.

What controls the door is what gives the channel its name.

Now name it: leak channel, ligand gated channel, voltage gated channel
