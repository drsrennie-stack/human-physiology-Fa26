What "draw this week's control loop by hand" actually means

This is the one that stops people, and it is a fair question. Here is exactly what goes on the paper.

A control loop is the circle your body uses to hold one number steady. Week 1 is entirely about these, so drawing one is the main thing you practice this week. You are not writing a paragraph. You are drawing boxes and arrows.

Draw six labelled boxes in a circle

  1. The number being held steady. Pick one from her chart. Body temperature is the easiest one to start with.
  2. What pushed it off. She runs in the heat and her core temperature starts to climb.
  3. The sensor, what notices. Temperature receptors in her skin, and others in the hypothalamus reading the temperature of her blood.
  4. The integrating center, what decides. The hypothalamus compares what it is reading against about 37 degrees Celsius (98.6 degrees Fahrenheit).
  5. The output and the effector, what carries the order and what does the work. Nerves to the sweat glands and to the blood vessels in her skin. The glands sweat, the vessels widen.
  6. The response, and the arrow back. Heat leaves, core temperature comes back down. Now draw an arrow from the response back to the sensor, because the response changes what the sensor reads. That arrow is the loop. Without it you have drawn a list, not a loop.

Then do two more things

  • Label it negative or positive. Negative means the response pushes the number back toward where it started, and that is almost everything in the body. Positive means the response pushes it further in the same direction.
  • Say how you know. One line. Sweating lowered the temperature that started it, so the loop shut off its own trigger. That is negative feedback.

The test of whether you have it: hand your page to somebody who is not in this class and they should be able to follow the arrows and tell you which number your body was defending. If you cannot name the sensor, you do not have the loop yet, and the sensor is almost always the part people leave out.

Where the AI log goes

Week 1, step by step

  1. Print the chart once. About twenty pages, all portrait, so any printer will do. Put it in a folder you will not lose.
  2. Pick your entry point. Five to choose from: Nursing, Medicine, Radiology, Respiratory therapy, and Exercise and allied health. Pick the room you are heading for, or the one you know least about. They are graded the same and none of them is the easier one.
  3. Open the Week 1 case and read it. The encounter is her preseason physical, so she is healthy. This week is your baseline.
  4. Write Week 1's numbers into the first column of the flowsheets in your chart. Everything later gets compared against this column.
  5. Draw the control loop on the Week 1 chart page, the way the section above describes.
  6. Answer the five questions everyone answers, then the one extra question for your entry point.
  7. Write your AI line at the bottom, even if it says None.
  8. Turn in the Week 1 case in Canvas by Sunday, 10:00 pm Pacific. The case only. The chart stays in your folder.

Week 2, step by step

  1. Do not print anything again. You are adding to the chart you already have.
  2. Stay in the same entry point. Your choice is remembered in one browser on one device, so a different computer will ask again. Pick the same one.
  3. Open the Week 2 case.
  4. Write Week 2's numbers into the Week 2 column, right beside Week 1's. Do not start a fresh page. The two columns sitting side by side is the whole point.
  5. Add anything new to the problem list.
  6. Draw this week's control loop and answer the five questions plus your entry point question.
  7. Write your AI line.
  8. Turn in the Week 2 case in Canvas by Sunday, September 27, 10:00 pm Pacific. The chart still stays with you.

Week 3 is a catch up week on the same material, so nothing new is due in between. If Week 2 got away from you, that is the week to finish it.

Why it is built this way

  • The trend is the finding. Nobody on a ward reads one day of a chart. They read across. Fifteen columns side by side is what makes that possible, and you cannot see a trend in fifteen files uploaded separately.
  • Writing by hand makes you compare. Putting a number in the column next to last week's number forces you to look at last week's number. Typing it into a box does not.
  • Drawing is how this gets tested. Both midterms ask you to draw and teach, not to recognize an answer. The loop you draw this week is practice for that.
  • One grade at the end means you can fix things. An entry you would write better in November is not locked in a gradebook from September.
  • You will need September in December. In Week 15 you re-read her first blood gas using things you will not learn until November. That only works if what you wrote in September is still in your folder.

Quick answers

Do I upload my chart entry this week?

No. Not this week and not any week. Camila’s chart comes in on November 1 with Midterm 1, and Dale’s on December 16.

Which one is actually graded on Sunday?

The week's case. That is the one with a Canvas submission and a Sunday deadline.

Can I change my entry point later?

Pick in Week 1 and stay there. The chart is read across the whole term, and switching halfway breaks the trend you are graded on.

I lost my printed chart.

Print it again from the chart page and copy your entries back in. Copying them back is a review, not wasted time.

Can I type the chart instead of writing it?

You can, but the comparison is the skill, and typing into a box lets you skip it. If you type, still put the new number beside the old one and look at both.

I am new to all of this and I feel behind.

You are not behind. This is the first week of a course built on drawing and comparing, and almost nobody has done a class this way before. Ask in the Virtual Office whenever a word or an instruction does not land. If one person asks, several more needed it.
