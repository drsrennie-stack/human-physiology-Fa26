Neurons and glia

A neuron has one job, which is to carry a signal from the place where it receives information to the place where it passes that information on. Today we look at how the cell is built to do that, and at the other cells in nervous tissue that keep it working.

  • We sort neurons by their shape and by their job. Then we divide one neuron into four regions and ask, for each region, which ion channels are in its membrane and what kind of signal it carries.
  • Then we go through the six kinds of glial cell and the job of each one. We spend the most time on the two that make myelin, and on what happens to a signal when myelin is lost.
  • Last, we look at how a neuron supplies an axon that can be a meter long, and we put real times on it. Five problems are worked on the slides. Try each one before you open the answer.

Silverthorn chapter 9. Week 4 competencies 7 to 11.

The problem for today

A 28 year old graduate student wakes up with blurred vision in her right eye, and the eye hurts when she moves it. Over several weeks her vision comes back. Months later she notices something strange. In a hot shower, or on a run on a hot day, the vision in that eye blurs again, and within an hour of cooling down it clears. Her eye is healthy, and every neuron that carries the signal from that eye to her brain is still there. What is missing, and why would warming her body by half a degree matter?

03

The map

Nervous tissue has two kinds of cells

  • Neurons carry electrical signals. Glia, also called glial cells or neuroglia, keep the neurons working. The word glia comes from the Greek word for glue, because early anatomists thought these cells only held the tissue together. They do far more than that.
  • The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and the spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is every nerve and every ganglion outside them. A ganglion is a cluster of neuron cell bodies outside the CNS.
  • This is a physiology course, so for every structure today ask one question: what does this part do to move a signal from input to output?
04

Neurons by shape

Four structural classes

  • A process is any extension of the cell, which means a dendrite or an axon. Dendrites receive signals. The axon carries the signal away from the cell body to the axon terminal.
  • Multipolar neurons are by far the most common. In a pseudounipolar neuron the dendrite and the axon fused during development into one long process, and the cell body was left on a short side branch. Many anaxonic neurons signal only over short distances with graded potentials.

The signal does not have to cross the cell body

The signal can travel from the receptor in the skin to the spinal cord along one continuous axon. In a multipolar neuron, signals from the dendrites have to spread across the cell body before a new action potential can start, and signals that spread that way fade with distance.

A touch neuron from your toe is about a meter long. With the cell body out of the path, the action potential that starts near the receptor runs the whole way to the spinal cord without interruption. The cell body still does its other job, which is to make the proteins that keep the whole cell alive.

05

Neurons by job · Worked problem 1

Three functional classes, in one reflex

  • Sensory (afferent) neurons carry signals from receptors toward the CNS. Interneurons lie entirely inside the CNS and connect neurons to each other. Motor (efferent) neurons carry signals away from the CNS to muscles and glands.
  • Efferent neurons also include the autonomic neurons that control smooth muscle, heart muscle and glands. Those come later in the course.

Worked problem 1

Cover the labels in the figure. Label each of the three neurons twice, once by shape and once by job, and say whether its cell body is inside or outside the CNS.

Solved

  1. Neuron 1 runs from the skin to the spinal cord, so it carries the signal toward the CNS. By job it is a sensory (afferent) neuron. By shape it is pseudounipolar. Its cell body is in a dorsal root ganglion, which is outside the CNS.
  2. Neuron 2 begins and ends inside the spinal cord. By job it is an interneuron. By shape it is multipolar. Its cell body is inside the CNS, as is every other part of it.
  3. Neuron 3 runs from the spinal cord to the muscle, so it carries the signal away from the CNS. By job it is a motor (efferent) neuron. By shape it is multipolar. Its cell body is inside the CNS, in the ventral horn of the spinal cord, and only its axon leaves.
  4. Check the pattern. Shape and job are two separate labels, but they pair up in a regular way. Most sensory neurons are pseudounipolar, and the sensory neurons for smell, vision and hearing are bipolar. Interneurons and motor neurons are multipolar, and a few interneurons are anaxonic.
  5. Interneurons outnumber the other two classes enormously. A common textbook estimate is that about 99 of every 100 neurons are interneurons. That tells you most of what the CNS does is process information between the input and the output.
06

One neuron, four regions

Input, trigger, conducting and output zones

  • Each region does a different job, and it can do that job because of the ion channels in its membrane. These are the four channel types from the last lecture, placed on a map of the cell.
  • A graded potential is a small change in membrane potential. Its size depends on the strength of the stimulus, and it fades as it spreads. An action potential is always the same size and does not fade, which is what all or none means.
  • The next lecture explains how both signals are produced. Today you need to know where in the cell each one happens.

Draw the neuron three times. In the first, shade where graded potentials happen. In the second, shade where voltage gated Na+ channels are dense. In the third, shade where the signal becomes chemical.

The trigger zone

Graded potentials start in the dendrites and cell body and spread as far as the trigger zone, so the trigger zone is shaded in the first drawing. It also has the highest density of voltage gated Na+ channels in the cell, so it is shaded in the second drawing, along with the axon. The axon terminal is the only region shaded in the third.

That overlap is what makes the trigger zone the decision point. If the graded potential is still at threshold, about −55 mV, when it reaches the trigger zone, the Na+ channels there open and an action potential begins. If it has faded below threshold, nothing is sent down the axon.

Predict

A motor neuron is stimulated at its dendrites, and it should make its muscle contract. For each toxin, say which regions still work normally and name the exact place where the signal stops.

Three different places

Tetrodotoxin. The input zone still works, because its ligand gated channels are not affected, so graded potentials still appear in the dendrites and cell body. The signal stops at the trigger zone. With no working voltage gated Na+ channels, no action potential can start, and nothing travels down the axon.

Omega-conotoxin. The input zone, the trigger zone and the axon all work, and action potentials arrive at the terminal. The signal stops at the output zone. With no Ca2+ entry, the vesicles do not release neurotransmitter.

Curare. Every region of the neuron works and neurotransmitter is released. The signal stops at the input zone of the next cell, because the ligand gated channels on the muscle cannot open.

The muscle fails to contract in all three cases. To say which region failed, you have to know which channel is found there.

09

Glia of the CNS

Four glial cells in the brain and spinal cord

  • Astrocytes control the fluid around neurons. They take up K+ and neurotransmitter, pass fuel from the blood to neurons, and help form the blood brain barrier.
  • Oligodendrocytes make the myelin of the CNS. One cell wraps segments on several axons.
  • Microglia are the immune cells of the CNS. They engulf debris, dead cells and microbes.
  • Ependymal cells form the lining of the fluid filled spaces of the brain and spinal cord. Their cilia move the cerebrospinal fluid, and specialized ependymal cells in the choroid plexus produce it.
10

Glia of the CNS · Worked problem 2

What astrocytes do for the fluid around a neuron

  • They take up K+. Every action potential lets a little K+ out of the neuron into a very narrow space. Astrocytes take it up and pass it along a network of connected astrocytes.
  • They take up neurotransmitter. Astrocytes wrap around synapses and remove glutamate and other neurotransmitters, which ends the signal.
  • They pass fuel to neurons. They take glucose from the blood and can supply neurons with lactate as a fuel for making ATP.
  • They help form the blood brain barrier. The barrier is the tight junctions between the cells of the capillary wall, and astrocyte end feet signal those cells to form them. O2, CO2 and alcohol cross freely, glucose and amino acids cross on transporters, and many drugs cannot cross.

Suppose the astrocytes around a busy group of neurons stop clearing K+, and the K+ outside those neurons rises from 4 mM to 8 mM. Use the Nernst and Goldman equations from the last lecture to find the new EK and the new resting potential. Keep 140 mM inside and PNa = 0.04.

Solved

  1. Potassium equilibrium potential with 8 mM outside: EK = 61.5 × log10(8 ÷ 140) = 61.5 × (−1.243) = −76 mV. With the normal 4 mM it was −95 mV.
  2. Goldman equation, with PK = 1 and PNa = 0.04 as in the last lecture. Top: 8 + (0.04 × 145) = 8 + 5.8 = 13.8. Bottom: 140 + (0.04 × 15) = 140.6.
  3. Ratio: 13.8 ÷ 140.6 = 0.0981. log10(0.0981) = −1.008. Vm = 61.5 × (−1.008) = −62 mV.
  4. The resting potential of every neuron in that region has moved from −71 mV to −62 mV. Threshold is about −55 mV, so these neurons are now only 7 mV away from firing, and they were never stimulated.
  5. This is what astrocytes prevent. Every action potential lets a little K+ out of the neuron, and the space between cells in the brain is very narrow, so the K+ concentration there can rise quickly. Astrocytes take the K+ up and pass it through the network of astrocytes to places where the concentration is lower. When that clearing fails, activity in one group of neurons can spread to its neighbors, which is one of the ways a seizure spreads. The 8 mM here is an illustration and not a measured value for a particular condition.

Nursing

Some medicines in the blood cannot get into the brain, because the walls of brain capillaries are sealed much more tightly than capillaries elsewhere.

Example: In Parkinson disease the brain is short of dopamine, but dopamine given as a drug cannot cross the blood brain barrier. Patients are given levodopa, which crosses on an amino acid transporter and is converted to dopamine inside the brain.

Radiologic technology

Contrast material normally stays inside brain blood vessels, so a place where it leaks into the tissue is a place where the barrier is broken.

Example: On CT or MRI with contrast, a brain tumor, an abscess or an active multiple sclerosis lesion lights up, which is called enhancement, because the blood brain barrier there is leaky and contrast escapes into the tissue.

Medicine

Most brain tumors grow from glial cells and not from neurons, because glial cells can still divide and mature neurons cannot.

Example: Astrocytoma, oligodendroglioma and glioblastoma are all gliomas, which means tumors that arise from glial cells.

Respiratory therapy

Carbon dioxide crosses the blood brain barrier easily, and hydrogen ions do not, and that is how the brain senses how well you are breathing.

Example: CO2 from the blood crosses into the cerebrospinal fluid and lowers its pH. Chemoreceptors in the medulla respond, and breathing increases. This is why a rise in arterial CO2 is the main minute to minute drive to breathe. It is covered in the respiratory weeks.

11

Glia of the PNS

Two glial cells in the nerves, and what myelin is

  • Satellite cells surround neuron cell bodies in ganglia. They support the cell body and control the fluid around it, much as astrocytes do in the CNS.
  • Schwann cells make the myelin of the PNS. Each Schwann cell wraps one segment, about 1 mm long, of one axon. The gaps between segments are the nodes of Ranvier.
  • Myelin is the glial cell's own membrane wound around the axon many times. Membrane is mostly lipid, and ions cannot cross lipid, so myelin is electrical insulation.

One cell to many axons, or one cell to one segment

An oligodendrocyte, in the CNS, sends out many arms, and each arm wraps one segment. One oligodendrocyte can supply segments to several dozen axons. A Schwann cell, in the PNS, wraps its whole body around a single segment of a single axon, so a long axon needs hundreds of Schwann cells in a row.

This difference matters in disease. Losing one oligodendrocyte strips a segment from many axons at once.

12

Myelin

How insulation makes a signal faster

  • An action potential does not slide along the axon. Each patch of membrane has to be depolarized to threshold by current from the patch before it, and then open its own Na+ channels. Opening channels is the slow step.
  • Current flowing along the inside of the axon is fast, but in a bare axon it leaks out through the membrane, so it reaches only a short distance ahead.
  • Myelin carries no signal itself. It stops the leak. Current from one node reaches the next node, about 1 mm away, still strong enough to bring it to threshold, so the action potential is made again only at the nodes.
  • A wider axon also conducts faster, because a wide axon has less resistance to current flowing along it. Myelin gives the same speed in a far thinner axon. The full electrical explanation comes with the action potential.

Worked problem 3

Two signals leave your toe at the same moment

You stub your toe. A touch signal travels in a large myelinated axon at about 50 m/s. The dull ache travels in a thin unmyelinated axon at about 1 m/s. The distance from the toe to the spinal cord is about 1 m. How long does each signal take to arrive?

time = distance ÷ speed

distance in meters, speed in meters per second, so time comes out in seconds.

What it says: a signal that travels 50 times faster arrives in one fiftieth of the time.

Solved

  1. Use time = distance ÷ speed, and keep the units matched: meters and meters per second.
  2. Touch, in a large myelinated axon: 1 m ÷ 50 m/s = 0.02 s, which is 20 milliseconds.
  3. Dull ache, in a thin unmyelinated axon: 1 m ÷ 1 m/s = 1 s.
  4. The touch signal arrives 50 times sooner. You have felt this. When you stub your toe you know at once that you hit something, and the ache arrives about a second later. That delay is the difference between a myelinated and an unmyelinated axon.
  5. The sharp first pain that comes before the ache travels in thinly myelinated axons at about 5 to 30 m/s, which puts it between the two. All of these speeds are approximate, and books give ranges.
14

Myelin and its loss

Why a demyelinated axon fails to conduct

  • Voltage gated Na+ channels are not spread evenly along a myelinated axon. They are packed densely at the nodes, and there are very few under the myelin.
  • Strip the myelin and two things go wrong together. Current leaks out through the bare membrane, and the bare membrane has too few Na+ channels to make a new action potential. The depolarization fades before it reaches the next node.
  • So conduction does not drop to the speed of an unmyelinated axon. It fails. This is called conduction block. If the myelin is only thinned, the signal gets through, but late.
  • Over weeks the axon can add Na+ channels to the bare stretch, and glial cells can partly remyelinate it, so some function returns.

Nursing

People with multiple sclerosis can feel weaker or see worse when they get hot, and they get better again when they cool off.

Example: A patient with multiple sclerosis reports that her legs feel heavy after a hot bath. This worsening with heat is temporary and reverses with cooling. A fever can do the same thing, so a return of old symptoms is a reason to check for an infection.

Radiologic technology

An MRI scan can show the patches where myelin has been lost.

Example: In multiple sclerosis, demyelinated patches called plaques show as bright spots in the white matter on T2 weighted and FLAIR images, often beside the ventricles. A plaque with active inflammation enhances with gadolinium contrast because the blood brain barrier there is leaky.

Medicine

Doctors can time how fast a signal travels along a nerve, and a slow signal points to a myelin problem.

Example: A visual evoked potential that arrives late but at close to normal size points to demyelination of the optic nerve. In Guillain-Barré syndrome, nerve conduction studies in the arms and legs show slowed conduction or conduction block.

Respiratory therapy

When myelin is lost from the nerves that drive the breathing muscles, breathing gets weak.

Example: In Guillain-Barré syndrome the weakness can move upward to the phrenic nerves and the nerves to the chest wall. Respiratory therapists measure vital capacity and inspiratory force repeatedly, and a falling trend shows that the patient may need mechanical ventilation.

Axonal transport

A neuron has to supply an axon that can be a meter long

  • Almost every protein in a neuron is made in the cell body, because that is where the nucleus, the rough ER and the Golgi are. This is the route from gene to membrane that you saw in the last lecture.
  • The axon and its terminal have almost no ribosomes. Yet the terminal needs a steady supply: channels and pumps for its membrane, the proteins of synaptic vesicles, enzymes that make neurotransmitter, and mitochondria.
  • Proteins wear out in days to weeks, so the supply can never stop.

Predict

Could diffusion deliver a protein from the cell body to a terminal 1 m away? Think back to Week 2: how does the time for diffusion change as the distance gets longer?

How long is that, really?

The cell body of a large motor neuron is about 0.1 mm across. Its axon to the foot is about 1 m long, which is 10,000 times the width of the cell body. If the cell body were the size of a tennis ball, the axon would be a tube about 670 m long, which is about 0.4 mile.

No. The time grows with the square of the distance

Diffusion is fast over the width of a cell and extremely slow over long distances, because the time it takes rises with the square of the distance. A protein can diffuse across a cell body in seconds. For the same protein to diffuse 1 m would take hundreds of years at the least. That is a rough estimate, but the conclusion does not depend on the exact number.

A neuron therefore needs a transport system that carries cargo in one direction, along a track, using energy.

17

Axonal transport

Motor proteins walk along microtubules and carry the cargo

  • Microtubules run the length of the axon and act as tracks. Every microtubule in an axon points the same way, with its plus end toward the terminal.
  • Kinesin walks toward the plus end, so it carries cargo anterograde, from the cell body to the terminal. Dynein walks toward the minus end, so it carries cargo retrograde, back to the cell body. Each step splits ATP.
  • Slow transport moves cytoskeleton proteins and dissolved enzymes. Current evidence is that this cargo rides on the same motors but spends most of its time paused, so its average speed is low.

Recycling and information

Retrograde transport brings worn out membrane and old organelles back to the cell body, where lysosomes break them down and the parts are reused.

It also carries information. Growth factors released by the target cell are taken up at the terminal and carried back to the nucleus, and they tell the neuron that its connection is alive and what the far end needs.

Worked problem 4

How long does a protein take to reach a terminal 1 meter away?

A motor neuron in the lower spinal cord has an axon 1 m long that ends on a muscle in the foot. Find the time to reach the terminal by fast anterograde transport at 400 mm per day, and by slow transport at 1 mm per day. Then find the time for the return by fast retrograde transport at 200 mm per day.

time = distance ÷ speed

distance in millimeters, speed in millimeters per day, so time comes out in days.

What it says: these speeds are approximate textbook values. Fast anterograde transport is about 400 mm per day, fast retrograde about 200 to 300, and slow transport about 0.2 to 8.

Solved

  1. Use time = distance ÷ speed, with the distance in millimeters: 1 m = 1000 mm.
  2. Fast anterograde transport: 1000 mm ÷ 400 mm per day = 2.5 days. A sodium channel made in the cell body on Monday morning is in the membrane near the foot by Wednesday evening.
  3. Slow transport at 1 mm per day: 1000 mm ÷ 1 mm per day = 1000 days, and 1000 ÷ 365 = about 2.7 years.
  4. Slow transport covers a range. At 8 mm per day the same distance takes 125 days. At 0.2 mm per day it takes 5000 days, which is more than 13 years. The cytoskeleton proteins are the slowest cargo.
  5. Fast retrograde transport at 200 mm per day: 1000 ÷ 200 = 5 days from the terminal back to the cell body.
  6. What the numbers say: fast transport can resupply the far end of the longest axon in the body within a few days, which is about how often membrane proteins need replacing. Slow transport is several hundred times slower, and that matters when an axon has to regrow, as you will see on slide 21.

2.5 days against 2.7 years

The synaptic vesicle protein arrives in 2.5 days of real time. The cytoskeleton protein takes 1000 days, which is about 2.7 years. These are the answers to worked problem 4. Now slide the axon length down to 1 mm, the size of a short interneuron in the brain, and both times shrink: a few minutes for fast transport and one day for slow transport.

Everything stops

Every motor stops where it is, in both directions, and the fast and slow cargo stop alike. Kinesin and dynein are ATPases. They split ATP for every step. A neuron that runs short of ATP loses its supply line to the terminal as well as its sodium potassium pump.

Predict

A drug takes microtubules apart in every cell of the body. Which neurons would you expect to fail first, and where on the body would the person notice it first?

The ones with the longest axons

With no tracks, kinesin and dynein have nothing to walk on, and the terminals stop receiving new proteins and mitochondria. The terminals farthest from their cell bodies run short first. Those belong to the longest axons, the ones to the toes and the fingertips.

The person notices numbness and tingling that begin in the feet and the hands. This happens with vincristine, a chemotherapy drug that blocks the assembly of microtubules. It is given to stop cancer cells from dividing, because dividing cells need microtubules to separate their chromosomes, and the effect on long axons is a side effect.

Worked problem 5

A nerve is cut at the wrist and the two ends are stitched together. The fingertip is 200 mm from the cut. If the axons regrow at 1 to 2 mm per day, how long before feeling could begin to return in the fingertip? Then compare: how long does an action potential at 50 m/s take to cover the same 200 mm?

Solved

  1. The axon has to regrow from the cut to the fingertip, 200 mm.
  2. At 2 mm per day: 200 ÷ 2 = 100 days. At 1 mm per day: 200 ÷ 1 = 200 days.
  3. So feeling could begin to return in the fingertip after roughly 3 to 7 months, and there is also a delay of days to weeks before regrowth starts. These numbers are approximate.
  4. Compare that with signaling. Once the axon is rebuilt, an action potential covers the same 200 mm at 50 m/s in 0.2 ÷ 50 = 0.004 s, which is 4 milliseconds.
  5. The regrowth speed is about the same as slow axonal transport, and that is not a coincidence. A growing axon is built from cytoskeleton proteins, and those arrive from the cell body by slow transport.

The patient, explained

Every neuron is present. Myelin is what is missing.

  1. The optic nerve is part of the CNS

    Its axons are myelinated by oligodendrocytes. In multiple sclerosis the immune system attacks CNS myelin. Her first episode was a patch of inflammation in the right optic nerve that stripped myelin from many axons. Those axons went into conduction block, and her vision blurred.

  2. Her vision came back, but the repair is thin

    The inflammation settled, oligodendrocytes partly remyelinated the patch, and the axons added Na+ channels to the bare membrane. Signals get through again, but slowly: 142 ms against 102 ms for the other eye.

  3. A repaired axon has very little to spare

    In a healthy myelinated axon, each node receives several times more current than it needs to reach threshold. In her repaired axons, the current that arrives is only just enough.

  4. Warming shortens every action potential

    Ion channels open and close faster when they are warmer, so the Na+ channels inactivate sooner and each action potential delivers less current to the next node. A healthy axon has plenty to spare. In her right optic nerve, a rise in body temperature of about 0.5 °C (0.9 °F) is enough to drop the current below what the next node needs, and those axons stop conducting until she cools down. No new damage has been done. This is called the Uhthoff phenomenon.

Treat it as an experiment

The variable that fails is the current reaching the next node, compared with the current that node needs to reach threshold. Before you open the box, name one change that would make each action potential last longer, so that it delivers more current.

Temperature, and potassium channels

Lower the temperature. Cooler channels inactivate later, so each action potential lasts longer and delivers more current to the next node. Conduction returns. This is why her vision clears when she cools down, and why a cool shower or a cooling vest reduces symptoms in people with multiple sclerosis.

Block voltage gated K+ channels. Losing myelin uncovers K+ channels that are normally hidden beneath it. When they open, positive charge leaves the axon and the action potential is cut short. A drug that blocks these channels lengthens the action potential and improves conduction in some people with multiple sclerosis.

Neither change repairs the myelin. They act on the electrical problem, and the immune attack is a separate target.
